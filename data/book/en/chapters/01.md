## Chapter overview

On July 1, 2021, the three astronauts aboard China's space station had been in residence for only two weeks. That day, the station carried out a "preventive collision-avoidance control": according to the Chinese account, a Starlink satellite (Starlink-1095) was closing in on the station. On October 21 of the same year, the same thing happened again, this time with Starlink-2305.

Neither avoidance resulted in an accident, but the dispute was only beginning. In December 2021, China submitted a note verbale to the UN Secretary-General, stating that the two close approaches endangered the lives and health of its astronauts. In January 2022, the United States replied with its own note, stating that U.S. Space Command had not assessed a significant probability of collision in either event. Independent analyses also reached no consensus on exactly how close the two approaches were.

The same set of orbits produced two accounts: one side held that avoidance was necessary, the other that the risk was low. Readers may want to know first who was right. This chapter asks a different question first: at the moment danger appears, who holds what data, and through what channels can they let the other side know in time?

Space has shifted from a field of scientific exploration to a domain of critical infrastructure supporting communications, navigation, the economy, disaster prevention, and national defense. With the rapid expansion of low-Earth-orbit mega-constellations, the volume and update frequency of satellite orbital data have risen sharply. National security therefore cannot stop at "seeing satellites"; it must also understand in near real time what a satellite is doing, why it is doing so, and whether its behavior constitutes a risk. This chapter takes the space information-sharing arrangements of Case 32 as its Main case to show that space situational awareness (SSA) is not only a technical problem but also a problem of institutions, trust, and collaboration.¹

In this book, space situational awareness refers to the monitoring, understanding, and prediction of space objects, the orbital environment, space weather, and related events. Space domain awareness (SDA) is broader in scope, adding the electromagnetic spectrum, cyberspace, and assessment of intent on top of this. The methods in this book belong to the former and deal with orbital behavior; the two terms are not treated as synonyms.

## Learning goals

After reading this chapter, readers should be able to answer the following questions:

- Why have low-Earth-orbit satellites become critical national infrastructure?

## Space as a National Security Domain

Satellites are now deeply embedded in the functioning of modern society. Communications satellites support connectivity in remote areas and disaster communications; navigation satellites underpin transportation, finance, and time synchronization; remote-sensing satellites support meteorology, agriculture, disaster assessment, and environmental monitoring; reconnaissance and early-warning satellites bear directly on national defense and the strategic situation.

Once these systems suffer interference, failure, collision, or anomalous behavior, what may be affected is the information resilience of an entire nation, not merely a single satellite. Space security is therefore a foundational issue for national security, economic resilience, and the functioning of society, not a remote technological topic.

The impact can be very concrete. On February 24, 2022, the day Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine, the KA-SAT satellite broadband network of the U.S. satellite operator Viasat was hit by a cyberattack, disabling tens of thousands of user modems in Ukraine and across Europe. Also affected were about 5,800 wind turbines of the German wind-power company Enercon: the turbines kept running, but remote monitoring and control via satellite link was cut off. On May 10, 2022, the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Estonia, and the European Union publicly attributed the attack to Russia.

This incident damaged user equipment on the ground; satellite orbits did not change. This book deals with orbital behavior and cannot detect this kind of attack. It is placed here to illustrate two points: once satellite services are interrupted, the consequences propagate to ground systems such as energy and communications; space situational awareness therefore cannot look only at orbital positions but must also cover cyberspace and the electromagnetic spectrum (see the SDA note after the Case 32 table in this chapter).

**Sidebar: Finnair Suspends Flights to Tartu in 2024**

In April 2024, Finnair announced that it would suspend its Helsinki–Tartu (Estonia) route from April 29 to May 31: in the previous week, two flights to Tartu had turned back due to GPS interference, and during the suspension the airport was to establish an approach method that does not depend on GPS. Finnair said this kind of interference had been increasing steadily since 2022. Estonia's foreign minister called it a deliberate Russian "hybrid attack" but did not make evidence public. The satellites themselves were not damaged; what was affected were ground activities that depend on space signals.

The expansion of low-Earth-orbit communications constellations makes this issue more urgent. Mega-constellations consist of thousands or even tens of thousands of satellites, and their orbit maintenance, batch deployment, deorbiting, and collision avoidance all generate large volumes of orbital data. Without automated and explainable analysis capabilities, manual interpretation will be unable to keep up.

**Sidebar: Fengyun-1C in 2007**

On January 11, 2007, China destroyed its own retired weather satellite, Fengyun-1C, with a missile; T.S. Kelso of CelesTrak estimated the impact at around 22:26 UTC. On January 23, China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs confirmed the test, saying it was "not targeted at any country." The same website records that, as of June 2012, NORAD had cataloged 3,312 objects from this impact, at altitudes extending from below 200 km to nearly 4,000 km; NASA estimated more than 150,000 debris fragments larger than 1 cm. These fragments do not respect nationality; Chapter 4 will show one of them forcing the International Space Station to fire its thrusters in 2021.

**Sidebar: Taiwan's Space Autonomy and International Cooperation (2024–2026)**

In early December 2024, during the Taiwan Space International Conference held in Kaohsiung, the Taiwan Space Agency (TASA) signed its first memorandum of understanding with the Polish Space Agency (POLSA); the areas of cooperation listed in TASA's announcement included space transportation systems, space situational awareness, and remote-sensing technology, and the Polish side described it as the first step in bilateral space cooperation. On November 13, 2026, at the unveiling ceremony of the National Ocean Technology Operations Center in Kaohsiung, President Lai Ching-te said the government is advancing the "Third Phase of the National Space Technology Long-Term Development Program," supporting indigenous development of satellites and rockets and moving steadily toward the goal of launching satellites independently; he also listed enhanced maritime domain awareness as a priority for ocean technology. Looking back through the methods of this book, independent launch and international cooperation expand what Taiwan can see and share; turning public orbital data into usable judgments still requires the detection and validation methods of the following chapters.

**Sidebar: China's Space Capabilities as Seen by the U.S. Defense Intelligence Agency (2026)**

The U.S. Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA) report *Challenges to Security in Space 2026*, released in October 2026, states that as of July 2026 China's intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance (ISR) satellite fleet contained more than 510 systems, having more than tripled since 2018; many are ostensibly commercial or civil, but the report judges that their dual-use data could be used by the People's Liberation Army (PLA). The report also says China has a network of telescopes, radars, and other sensors capable of searching for, tracking, and characterizing satellites in all Earth orbits, supporting intelligence collection, counterspace targeting, ballistic missile early warning, and spaceflight safety; PLA jamming combines ground-based and on-orbit systems, and China has multiple ground-based laser weapons able to disrupt, degrade, or damage satellites in LEO. These are assessments by a U.S. intelligence agency. Seen through this book's methods, space situational awareness works both ways: we watch others with public orbital data, and others watch us with their own surveillance networks.

## From Seeing Satellites to Understanding Satellites

Traditional space surveillance has often centered on orbit plots, ephemerides, or collision warnings. These tools remain important, but they cannot fully answer the questions modern space security most needs answered:

- Why did this satellite change its orbit?
- Is this change normal station-keeping, or anomalous behavior?
- Is this natural decay caused by atmospheric drag, or intervention by a propulsion system?
- Is this a problem with a single satellite, or collective behavior of an entire constellation?
- Is this change related to a conjunction, deployment, retrieval, malfunction, or non-cooperative behavior?

The focus of modern space situational awareness, therefore, is understanding what satellites are doing, not merely seeing them.

This book takes a three-layer "rule–statistics–machine learning" architecture as its technical backbone, with the atmospheric drag residual as a physical cross-check. The purpose of this approach is not to have AI replace human judgment; it aims to turn large volumes of noisy, rapidly changing orbital data into candidate events and decision evidence that can be ranked, reviewed, and traced to their source.¹

### Case Background

In the Tiangong events, the two sides each had their own account of the same risk, and the main public exchange afterward consisted of two notes submitted to the United Nations. Case 32 addresses a different level: within the United States, how the government and commercial satellite operators exchange information in tiers. Both point to the same question: when danger appears, who can obtain verifiable, actionable data, and how quickly?

No single nation, agency, or satellite operator can accomplish the mission of space security alone. As orbits become congested, collision risk rises, and non-cooperative behavior increases, information sharing becomes the foundation of early warning and risk management. Case 32 compares four information-sharing arrangements between the U.S. Space Force and commercial satellite operators, namely NOTSO, JCO, CIC, and Space ISAC, and describes the Orbital Watch program promoted by Space Systems Command.²

### Tiers of Sharing Arrangements

| Name | Nature | Participation threshold | Information level | Main function |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Orbital Watch | Information-sharing program announced by Space Systems Command | Phase 1: basic registration via Space Force Front Door | Phase 1 one-way, unclassified; Phase 2 two-way still being planned | Sends unclassified threat briefings to registered companies |
| NOTSO (Notification to Space Operators) | A notification/alert product, not an organization | Receiving the notification is sufficient | Unclassified | Informs of high-interest space events such as satellite maneuvers and launches |
| JCO (Joint Commercial Operations cell) | Government-led commercial data integration mechanism | Requires training, adherence to methodology, and completion of onboarding | Unclassified | Integrates commercial sensors and analysis, provides 24/7 unclassified SDA, and issues NOTSOs |
| CIC (Commercial Integration Cell) | Invitation-only classified coordination mechanism | Invitation, training, security clearance, and contractual basis | May include classified | Coordinates with CSpOC, participates in exercises and military planning |
| Space ISAC (Space Information Sharing and Analysis Center) | Membership-based industry information-sharing organization | Paid membership | Mainly unclassified, also involves cybersecurity | Compiles member reports, cybersecurity monitoring, 24/7 operations center, relays NOTSOs |

In the table, SDA refers to space domain awareness, which in addition to tracking object positions also covers the electromagnetic spectrum, cyberspace, and intent analysis; CSpOC is the U.S. Combined Space Operations Center.

This table is compiled from Episode 30 of the Elara Nova Podcast "The Elara Edge" cited in Case 32 and has not been independently verified by this project; the relevant arrangements and participation conditions remain subject to each agency's official announcements.²

According to the program's guest, Phase 1 of Orbital Watch has already sent unclassified threat briefings to more than 900 companies registered through Space Force Front Door; this figure is likewise as stated on the program and has not been independently verified by this project.²

The difference among these arrangements is not which one is best, but that each serves a different level of trust, information sensitivity, and collaboration need. Public information can build broad early warning; restricted information can support more precise risk assessment; classified sharing can handle threats and operational coordination that cannot be made public.²

**Sidebar: How JCO and NOTSO Actually Work**

The JCO was established by U.S. Space Command in 2020, originally named the JTF-SD Commercial Operations cell, and was renamed at the end of 2023; in the same year JCO-Meridian and JCO-Pacific were added, forming round-the-clock shifts, and by August 2024, 18 allied and partner nations were participating. Its main product, the NOTSO, covers events such as launch and orbit insertion, maneuvers, close approaches, breakups, and changes in behavior patterns, and is sent by unclassified email to personnel who have completed training and are registered on the list; unlike civil aviation's notices to airmen, it is not public.

**Sidebar: CIC, Starting from the 2015 Pilot**

The CIC is not new. The concept came from the Schriever wargame of September 2014; on June 1, 2015, the U.S. military launched a six-month pilot at the Joint Space Operations Center, with liaison personnel from six satellite operators, including Intelsat and Iridium, stationed on site to exchange space situational data, interference incidents, and conjunction assessments. In October 2024, the Space Force added five more companies, including BlackSky, LeoLabs, and ICEYE, with membership expected to grow from 10 to 17 companies and seats expanding from communications satellite operators to remote-sensing and space surveillance companies.

### Why Public TLEs Still Matter

Although public TLEs have limited accuracy, they offer broad coverage, frequent updates, long history, and accessibility. They can serve as the basis for unclassified early warning, helping identify suspicious orbital changes, abnormal close approaches, and potential risks.

TLEs cannot replace precision ephemerides, sensor observations, mission plans, or classified intelligence. They are best suited to serve as a first layer of screening and early warning; once a suspicious event is found, it is further verified with higher-quality data and cross-agency collaboration.

**Sidebar: Free Collision Warnings Began in 2009**

U.S. Strategic Command launched the SSA Sharing Program in 2009, providing basic, emergency, and advanced space situational data and services free of charge; in 2019, the program was transferred to U.S. Space Command. Under the current handbook, all active satellite operators can receive basic emergency conjunction assessments: in low Earth orbit, a predicted miss distance of less than 1 km within three days and a probability of collision reaching 10⁻⁷ trigger a conjunction data message (CDM) on Space-Track; a probability reaching 10⁻⁴ triggers an additional emergency notification. Most collision warnings received by operators worldwide originate from this service (see Chapter 19).

**Sidebar: TraCSS on the Pilot Path**

On September 30, 2024, the U.S. Department of Commerce announced that Phase 1 of the Traffic Coordination System for Space (TraCSS) had begun serving test users, with the first 9 organizations receiving conjunction data messages via Space-Track. This step traces back to Space Policy Directive-3 of 2018: civil and commercial space situational services were to be transferred from the Department of Defense to the Department of Commerce. According to an August 2026 report, TraCSS had more than 70 satellite operators and more than 10 countries participating, yet remained in pilot mode due to budget uncertainty (see Chapter 20).

### National Security Implications

What information sharing must solve is getting verifiable, actionable data to decision-makers in time, not competing over who has the most data. Insufficient sharing may delay warning, while excessive disclosure may expose sensitive capabilities or cause misjudgment. Therefore, the scope of sharing, the recipients, and responsibility for verification all need to be defined in advance; space situational awareness must build technical and institutional capabilities at the same time.

The two events at the start of this chapter can also be revisited from this angle. In the Tiangong events, the two sides judged the risk of the same approach differently, and publicly visible communication took place after the fact; the Viasat incident shows that the impact of interrupted satellite services extends to ground facilities, and what needs to be shared is not only orbital information.

**Sidebar: Why the EU Built Its Own Space Surveillance**

On April 16, 2014, the European Parliament and the Council adopted Decision No 541/2014/EU, establishing a framework for EU Space Surveillance and Tracking (SST) support. The EU's official summary states plainly that Europe at the time had no SST capability of its own, and collision-avoidance alerts for satellite and launch operators relied on U.S. data. In June 2015, five countries, France, Germany, Italy, Spain, and the United Kingdom, signed an agreement to form a consortium, and from July 1, 2016, it has provided conjunction assessment, fragmentation, and reentry services under the name EU SST. The decision also emphasized cooperation with the United States, with the goal of reducing dependence and increasing European autonomy.

**Sidebar: A U.S. Sensor Riding on a Japanese Satellite**

On February 2, 2025, an H3 rocket launched Japan's Quasi-Zenith Satellite QZS-6 from Tanegashima. The satellite carries a space domain awareness payload designed and built by MIT Lincoln Laboratory and operated by the U.S. Space Force's Mission Delta 2, providing the U.S. Space Surveillance Network with near-real-time data on the geosynchronous orbit region over the Indo-Pacific. This is the first bilateral U.S.–Japan space cooperation focused on national security; a second payload is scheduled to fly on QZS-7. Space situational cooperation among allies has extended from exchanging data to sharing physical sensors.
