# Chapter 7　A Three-Layer Detection Architecture: Rules, Statistics, and Machine Learning

> *This English edition is an AI-assisted translation of the Traditional Chinese manuscript (v1.2, 2026-11-15). Where they differ, the Chinese original prevails.*

## Chapter overview

Just after midnight on September 26, 1983, in an early-warning command bunker south of Moscow, Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov of the Soviet Air Defense Forces was on duty. The Oko early-warning system in front of him relied on infrared sensors aboard satellites to watch for U.S. missile launches. That night, the system reported that the United States had launched missiles, eventually reporting five in all.

Petrov judged this to be a false alarm and reported it as such to his superiors. In a 1999 interview with *The Washington Post*, he explained his reasoning: if the United States were going to start a war, it would not launch only five missiles; the detection system had only recently been put into service, and he did not fully trust it; and for a real attack, he expected other systems, such as ground radars, to confirm it as well.

Afterward, an analysis by Forden, Podvig, and Postol indicated that sunlight reflected from scattered high-altitude clouds had been taken by the satellite's infrared sensor for missile exhaust plumes; they estimated that the satellite responsible was Cosmos 1382, and that the clouds were over Montana.

The machine said "yes" five times; what stopped the alarm was one person's doubt about plausibility, and his expectation of a second source. If an alerting system has only one layer, who asks, "Does this make sense?"

Facing an orbital curve that changes suddenly, an analyst must answer at least three questions: Does the change exceed the operational threshold? Does it deviate from recent normal behavior? After weighing all the evidence, which event should be reviewed first? This study assigns these needs to three layers, rules, statistics, and machine learning, and adds the physical drag residual and the reentry gate.¹

This chapter views the architecture as a traceable evidence-processing workflow; the three layers are not competing models. Besides outputting scores, the system must let users know which data and which assumptions a score is based on, and what limitations remain unresolved.

## Learning goals

- Explain the responsibilities, inputs, and outputs of L1, L2, and L3.
- Distinguish fixed rules, statistical estimation, physical models, and machine learning.
- Distinguish parallel evidence generation, candidate-event management, and final alerts.
- Understand that LightGBM, Isolation Forest, and the fusion scorer are not the same model.
- Avoid treating research operating points, classification scores, or in-domain test results as deployment guarantees.

## Layering is not queued voting

In this study, L1 uses rules P1–P6; L2 uses four channels: CUSUM (cumulative deviation), BOCPD (Bayesian change-point detection), singular spectrum analysis, and robust MAD (thresholding by median absolute deviation); the machine-learning modules include LightGBM, Isolation Forest, and the fusion scorer. The physical path provides the drag residual and reentry interpretation.¹

| Layer or module | Core question | Output and its limits |
|---|---|---|
| L1 rule layer | Does it meet known trigger or suppression conditions? | Flags and reasons, not a confirmed maneuver.|
| L2 statistical layer | How unusual is the change in the time series? | Anomaly scores; the scales of different channels cannot be treated directly as the same probability.|
| L3 fusion layer | How does the multi-channel combination support ranking? | Combined score; must be combined with thresholds, validation, and review.¹ |
| Physical and applicability handling | Can known drag, reentry, or model limitations explain the signal? | Residual, gating, or routing basis, not an absolute causal verdict.¹,² |

A "layer" in the architecture describes a division of responsibility; it does not necessarily mean that each record can only pass through three closed checkpoints in sequence. Case 1 of this project's case collection describes evidence generation as parallel channels, while this study's architecture description describes the operational workflow as "from broad candidate sweep to fused alert." The two descriptions should be presented separately, and neither can be used to conclude that the actual program must pass L1 before computing L2.²,¹

## L1: rules leave explicit reasons

The rule layer turns domain knowledge into conditions that can be checked. This study's P1–P6 involve drag-decay suppression, altitude thresholds, high-drag suppression, short-window rescanning (called multi-window supplementary detection in Case 22, used to recover missed detections), solar activity, and orbital-population adjustment.¹

White-box execution means users can trace why something was triggered; it does not mean every rule is correct, nor that the rules apply to all orbits. When suppression conditions are too broad, real maneuvers may also be excluded. The rule layer should therefore preserve both "why it triggered" and "why it was suppressed," rather than leaving only a final Boolean value.

Specific thresholds and the experimental setup are in Case 22 in Chapter 8; this chapter only explains where the rule layer sits in the overall system.³

## L2: statistics provide an anomaly scale

The four main channels of L2 observe the signal from the perspectives of cumulative deviation, change-point probability, residual from the trend, and robust outlyingness. This project's case collection classifies them as classical, non-machine-learning statistical methods.²

"Non-machine-learning" here does not mean no parameters are estimated from data at all. Medians, scale of variation, trends, and posterior quantities may still be updated as data arrive; the basis for the classification is whether the method learns a classification decision through a training procedure, not whether it uses statistics.

Statistics with physical meaning are likewise affected by missing values, time intervals, and background drift. The quality checks of Chapter 6 must extend into this layer; otherwise anomalies at the data entry point may be misread as anomalous satellite behavior.

**Sidebar: A simulated attack on NORAD's screens in 1979**

On November 9, 1979, Soviet attack simulation data used to test NORAD's computers entered the live early-warning displays for unknown reasons, appearing at the Strategic Air Command and the National Military Command Center. According to declassified documents, National Security Advisor Brzezinski's military assistant first reported about 250 incoming missiles, and a second phone call changed this to 2,200; only the third call said that other early-warning systems had not seen any launches. Test data mixing into the operational data stream is the hardest kind of data-quality problem to detect; what stopped it was an independent second signal.

## L3: the models are not the same thing

This study uses LightGBM as a supervised classifier and Isolation Forest as an unsupervised anomaly-detection method; this project's case collection additionally uses HistGradientBoosting to describe the fusion scorer. The three differ in purpose and training conditions and cannot be merged into a single model.¹,²

| Model | Role in this book | What must be distinguished |
|---|---|---|
| LightGBM (a gradient-boosted tree model) | Learns the classification relationship between features and labels.| Not the same as the five-channel fuser; different experiments may use different data and labels. |
| Isolation Forest | Identifies anomalies from the structure of the data.| Unsupervised does not mean no data are needed, nor that the score is a calibrated maneuver probability. |
| HistGradientBoosting (a gradient-boosting model that can handle many features and missing values) | A fusion model integrating channel features.| The input configuration, evaluation unit, and training version must be stated separately. |
| bi-GRU (bidirectional GRU, a recurrent neural network) | A deep sequence model the study once tried.| That study's negative result does not mean all deep-learning methods are infeasible.² |

Even if a model's output falls between 0 and 1, it should first be called a classification score or fusion score; interpreting it as an actual event probability requires checking calibration and the deployment distribution. Feature importance helps in understanding the model but cannot by itself prove that the model's inferences have causal validity.

## The physical model is not the final judge

The physical drag residual compares the observed orbital change with the decay the model expects. Case 5 also revealed that when continuous thrust is absorbed by parameters self-calibrated for each satellite, the residual is closer to a "deviation relative to usual behavior" and cannot be read directly as thrust magnitude.²

Therefore, a large residual does not equal a confirmed maneuver, and a residual near zero does not guarantee the absence of thrust. The physical path provides another set of checkable hypotheses; event interpretation must still combine data quality, mission context, and external evidence. The full content of Case 5 and Case 30 is in Chapter 5.³

**Sidebar: The object released by Cosmos 2543**

On July 15, 2020, the Russian satellite Cosmos 2543 released an object near another Russian satellite; the United States cataloged it as 45915. The separation is a behavioral fact visible in the public catalog; its purpose is another layer of interpretation: U.S. Space Command claimed it was a non-destructive test of a space-based anti-satellite weapon, while the Russian Ministry of Defense said it was inspecting one of its own satellites at close range. Observing a separation does not confirm its purpose.

### Building a chain of evidence from public data

Case 1 places data preprocessing, rules, statistics, physical residuals, classifiers, fusion, and external validation in a single architecture diagram. From it, readers can see that the results of the various methods are not isolated from one another; each is evidence with a source and a purpose.⁴

Starting from a new TLE, the interpretation workflow summarized in this book is: confirm the source and epoch, build a consistent element or residual signal, compute each channel's score, then generate candidate events using the fusion method of a specified version, and only then compare against reference data and carry out manual review. This is this book's summary of the workflow; it has not re-verified the execution paths of all the programs.

### Different validations cannot be merged into one

Case 1 mentions 14 development satellites and 9 external held-out satellites; Case 9's generalization test held out 56 of 284 Starlink satellites. The two designs do not use the same set of satellites, so the sample size of one and the AUC (area under the ROC curve, a measure of a classifier's overall discriminative power) of the other cannot be pieced together into a single performance claim.⁴

The overview in this project's case collection cites an overall recall of about 94.6% and a large-event recall of about 97.0%, and cites Case 9's AUC of about 0.98. These numbers each belong to their corresponding experiments; they cannot be read as "23 external satellites jointly achieving the same set of metrics," nor extrapolated to any unknown constellation. Case 9 notes that its numbers were read from the output of an offline batch evaluation on 2026-08-03; they are frozen evaluation results, not live queries.⁴

### Fusion inputs must state their version

This study's architecture description describes the fuser as four statistical channels plus the drag residual; the overview narrative of Case 1 additionally includes rule scores and machine-learning classification scores. These two descriptions should not be treated directly as the same feature list.¹,⁴

This chapter therefore uses "multi-channel fusion" as the general term. When Chapter 10 discusses the five-channel baseline, its inputs, aggregation method, and data snapshot must be stated separately; the actual inputs should follow the corresponding program and frozen feature table and cannot be inferred from the overview narrative.

### The limits and value of AI

Case 2 classifies rules, statistics, and physical models as the non-AI path, classifies LightGBM, Isolation Forest, and the fusion scorer as traditional machine learning, and records the experience that bi-GRU did not deliver the expected gain. This shows that the system did not hand all of the analysis to a single neural network.⁵

In the comparison listed in that case, the random-baseline recall is 0.05126, L1 is 0.2513, the best single L2 channel CUSUM is 0.4958, and the fuser is 0.9464. These are numbers under specific comparison conditions; they do not represent the performance ceiling of all non-AI methods, and the gap cannot be attributed entirely to "using AI."⁵

The fuser uses both more evidence and a different decision method. To quantify the pure model gain, one would also need to control the data split, input features, operating point, and evaluation unit. The more reasonable conclusion is: this test supports the value of fusion, but does not produce a general ranking of rules, statistics, and deep models.

### Explainability is not a physical guarantee

Case 2 argues for keeping a non-AI path on the grounds that it allows the source of a judgment to be traced. This design philosophy is worth keeping; but statements such as "holds for any satellite" or "a complete physical guarantee" are too strong, because the same case material already lists limitations regarding orbital regime, reentry, and model applicability.⁵

Rules can be completely transparent yet still set wrongly; physical models can rest on sound equations yet still be inaccurate because of errors in attitude, parameters, or density. Credibility must be built through scope of applicability and validation; it is not obtained automatically by being labeled "non-AI."

**Sidebar: Tiangong-1's reentry window**

In 2018, the 13 member agencies of the IADC made the reentry of Tiangong-1 their annual test campaign, hosted by ESA's Space Debris Office. At the end of March, ESA said that the expected stream of solar particles had had no effect, the upper-atmosphere density had not increased as predicted, and the reentry window had therefore shifted later. In its final update on April 1, ESA still stressed that it was impossible at any time to give a precise time and location. The actual reentry occurred at about 00:15 (UT) on April 2, over the South Pacific. When the density forecast changes, the physical prediction moves with it, and in the end it can still only give a range.

## From scores to usable alerts

This study describes fused alerts in terms of a research operating point at which the FPR (false-positive rate) does not exceed 0.05. This is a threshold setting under specific evaluation conditions and does not guarantee that the FPR will stay within limits during any deployment period.¹

Case 9's held-out-satellite test lists an FPR of 0.07015, and its out-of-time test lists 0.04402. This shows that even if the threshold is determined using only training data, results on deployment or held-out data may still differ. The full comparison is in Chapter 10; here we only remind readers not to confuse the target operating point with the value realized in testing.²,³

**Sidebar: The 60 meters between OneWeb and Starlink**

On March 30, 2021, the U.S. 18 SPCS initially predicted that OneWeb-0178 would come within about 60 meters of Starlink-1546, with a probability of collision of 1.3%. LeoLabs assessed the risk as lower, and OneWeb ultimately chose to maneuver. SpaceX later told the FCC that the actual closest distance was about 1,120 meters and that the probability never exceeded its own threshold; the two sides gave differing accounts of whether the automated collision-avoidance system had been "turned off" or "paused." The same conjunction led to different action decisions because of new observations, data sources, and thresholds.

This book recommends that an event card contain at least four kinds of information: the signal and channel contributions, data quality, model and threshold versions, and the evidence needed for review. Before probability calibration is completed, the output should be labeled as a score; when data are insufficient, it should be labeled as unable to be reliably determined, and a low score must not be equated directly with no maneuver.

- [ ] Can explain that rules are traceable, statistics are quantifiable, and machine learning is integrative, as well as the limitations each still has.
