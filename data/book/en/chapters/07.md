# Chapter 7　A Three-Layer Detection Architecture: Rules, Statistics, and Machine Learning

> *This English edition is an AI-assisted translation of the Traditional Chinese manuscript (v1.3, 2026-11-19). Where they differ, the Chinese original prevails.*

## Chapter overview

Just after midnight on September 26, 1983, in an early-warning command bunker south of Moscow, Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov of the Soviet Air Defense Forces was on duty. The Oko early-warning system in front of him relied on infrared sensors aboard satellites to watch for U.S. missile launches. That night, the system reported that the United States had launched missiles, eventually reporting five in all.

Petrov judged this to be a false alarm and reported it as such to his superiors. In a 1999 interview with *The Washington Post*, he explained his reasoning: if the United States were going to start a war, it would not launch only five missiles; the detection system had only recently been put into service, and he did not fully trust it; and for a real attack, he expected other systems, such as ground radars, to confirm it as well.

Afterward, an analysis by Forden, Podvig, and Postol indicated that sunlight reflected from scattered high-altitude clouds had been taken by the satellite's infrared sensor for missile exhaust plumes; they estimated that the satellite responsible was Cosmos 1382, and that the clouds were over Montana.

The machine said "yes" five times; what stopped the alarm was one person's doubt about plausibility, and his expectation of a second source. If an alerting system has only one layer, who asks, "Does this make sense?"

Facing an orbital curve that changes suddenly, an analyst must answer at least three questions: Does the change exceed the operational threshold? Does it deviate from recent normal behavior? After weighing all the evidence, which event should be reviewed first? This study assigns these needs to three layers, rules, statistics, and machine learning, and adds the physical drag residual and the reentry gate.¹

This chapter views the architecture as a traceable evidence-processing workflow; the three layers are not competing models. Besides outputting scores, the system must let users know which data and which assumptions a score is based on, and what limitations remain unresolved.

## Learning goals

- Explain the responsibilities, inputs, and outputs of L1, L2, and L3.
- Distinguish fixed rules, statistical estimation, physical models, and machine learning.
- Distinguish parallel evidence generation, candidate-event management, and final alerts.
- Understand that LightGBM, Isolation Forest, and the fusion scorer are not the same model.
- Avoid treating research operating points, classification scores, or in-domain test results as deployment guarantees.

## Layering is not queued voting

In this study, L1 uses rules P1–P6; L2 uses four channels: CUSUM (cumulative deviation), BOCPD (Bayesian change-point detection), singular spectrum analysis, and robust MAD (thresholding by median absolute deviation); the machine-learning modules include LightGBM, Isolation Forest, and the fusion scorer. The physical path provides the drag residual and reentry interpretation.¹

| Layer or module | Core question | Output and its limits |
|---|---|---|
| L1 rule layer | Does it meet known trigger or suppression conditions? | Flags and reasons, not a confirmed maneuver.|
| L2 statistical layer | How unusual is the change in the time series? | Anomaly scores; the scales of different channels cannot be treated directly as the same probability.|
| L3 fusion layer | How does the multi-channel combination support ranking? | Combined score; must be combined with thresholds, validation, and review.¹ |
| Physical and applicability handling | Can known drag, reentry, or model limitations explain the signal? | Residual, gating, or routing basis, not an absolute causal verdict.¹,² |

A "layer" in the architecture describes a division of responsibility; it does not mean that each record can only pass through three closed checkpoints in sequence: every channel computes on the same orbital-element time series and produces evidence in parallel.⁶

How the evidence from the layers is combined into a formal result follows the four steps of the R7 single-satellite report workflow in the final report:⁶

1. **L1 rule flagging**: find adjacent TLE transitions whose change exceeds the satellite's normal range.
2. **Merging into candidate events**: flags within 48 hours of one another are chained into one event; those continuing in the same direction over several days are further merged into a continuous maneuver segment.
3. **Checking auxiliary channels**: check whether the L2 statistical change points, drag residual, ML window model, and fusion scorer also respond within 24 hours before or after the event.
4. **Assigning an evidence grade**: mark review priorities such as E3, E2, E1, and D according to fixed rules.

Whether an event exists is decided only by the rules in steps 1 and 2; the auxiliary channels in step 3 affect only the grade and neither add nor remove events. Periods that L1 did not flag but to which other methods responded are listed separately by a side branch as "low-confidence review items"; they are not counted as candidate events, receive no grade, and are left to analysts to interpret.⁶

The App also has a "unified detection summary" that selects the primary judging method by satellite type: Starlink satellites are judged primarily by Model 1 and the fusion scorer, and non-Starlink satellites by the NRLMSIS drag residual. It is used only to inspect the raw responses of each layer's detectors and does not decide candidate events; when the two disagree, the R7 single-satellite report prevails.⁶

## L1: rules leave explicit reasons

The rule layer turns domain knowledge into conditions that can be checked. In the R7 version described in the final report, P1–P6 fall into three types: detection, suppression, and adjustment:⁶

- **P2 altitude threshold** is the core of detection, multiplied in turn by **P5** (adjustment by F10.7 solar activity: ×1.0 at or below 70 sfu, ×1.6 at 200 sfu, capped at ×1.8) and **P6** (adjustment by orbital population: ×1.0 for the 53° band, ×1.2 for sun-synchronous, ×1.1 for mid-inclination) to obtain the effective semi-major axis threshold.
- **P1** suppresses the monotonic small-step decay caused by atmospheric drag; transitions exceeding the effective threshold are not suppressed by P1.
- **P4** flags a transition that exceeds the threshold together with the 2 transitions after it, to extend event boundaries and connect neighboring flags.
- Inclination and eccentricity have separate thresholds (|Δi|>0.02°, |Δe|>0.001).
- **P3** (high-drag auxiliary suppression) and the right ascension of the ascending node residual (ΔΩ) flag were checked by large-sample rule-by-rule ablation and FORMOSAT-7 precise ground-truth validation: P3 had no effect on recall or false alarms and ΔΩ only added false alarms, so R7 includes neither in its decisions.⁶

White-box execution means users can trace why something was triggered; it does not mean every rule is correct, nor that the rules apply to all orbits. When suppression conditions are too broad, real maneuvers may also be excluded. The rule layer should therefore preserve both "why it triggered" and "why it was suppressed," rather than leaving only a final Boolean value.

Specific thresholds and the experimental setup are in Case 22 in Chapter 8; this chapter only explains where the rule layer sits in the overall system. The recall of the rule layer is limited: comparing five rule combinations, the final report found Starlink ground-truth recall between 0.31 and 0.33 for all of them; the main causes of the low values are the thresholds and small maneuvers, not how the rules are combined.³,⁶

## L2: statistics provide an anomaly scale

The four main channels of L2 observe the signal from the perspectives of cumulative deviation, change-point probability, residual from the trend, and robust outlyingness. This project's case collection classifies them as classical, non-machine-learning statistical methods.²

"Non-machine-learning" here does not mean no parameters are estimated from data at all. Medians, scale of variation, trends, and posterior quantities may still be updated as data arrive; the basis for the classification is whether the method learns a classification decision through a training procedure, not whether it uses statistics.

Statistics with physical meaning are likewise affected by missing values, time intervals, and background drift. The quality checks of Chapter 6 must extend into this layer; otherwise anomalies at the data entry point may be misread as anomalous satellite behavior.

**Sidebar: A simulated attack on NORAD's screens in 1979**

On November 9, 1979, Soviet attack simulation data used to test NORAD's computers entered the live early-warning displays for unknown reasons, appearing at the Strategic Air Command and the National Military Command Center. According to declassified documents, National Security Advisor Brzezinski's military assistant first reported about 250 incoming missiles, and a second phone call changed this to 2,200; only the third call said that other early-warning systems had not seen any launches. Test data mixing into the operational data stream is the hardest kind of data-quality problem to detect; what stopped it was an independent second signal.

## L3: the models are not the same thing

The final report lists three machine-learning models: Model 1 (LightGBM, supervised), the fusion scorer (HistGradientBoosting, supervised), and Model 2 (Isolation Forest, unsupervised), all of which are traditional machine learning; deep learning is not used. The three differ in purpose and training conditions and cannot be merged into a single model.⁶

| Model | Role in this book | What must be distinguished |
|---|---|---|
| Model 1: LightGBM (a gradient-boosted tree model) | Learns classification from 40 TLE orbital-change features; in R7 it is the "ML window" auxiliary channel.| Not the same as the five-channel fuser. The current version was retrained on Starlink MEME precise-ephemeris ground truth; the original version evaluated for contract acceptance was trained on rule-derived labels, and the two must not be mixed up. |
| Model 2: Isolation Forest | Identifies anomalies from the physical residual after drag is removed; serves only as corroboration for non-Starlink satellites and is not used in R7.| Unsupervised does not mean no data are needed, nor that the score is a calibrated maneuver probability. |
| Fusion scorer: HistGradientBoosting (a gradient-boosting model that can handle many features and missing values) | Trained on MEME ground truth for 284 Starlink satellites; integrates the statistics of five channels (CUSUM, BOCPD, SSA, MAD 3σ, and the drag residual) within a ±24-hour window.| Trained only on Starlink; on other satellites its judgments fall outside the training range, so in R7 it serves only as an auxiliary channel. |
| bi-GRU (bidirectional GRU, a recurrent neural network) | A deep sequence model the study once tried.| That study's negative result does not mean all deep-learning methods are infeasible.² |

Model 1 and the fusion scorer are trained only on Starlink data (283–284 satellites, altitudes 324–588 km). Therefore, in R7, machine learning neither adds nor removes events, and it cannot on its own raise an event of a non-Starlink satellite to the highest grade, E3; events that meet the E3 conditions only with machine-learning support are listed as "E2 (ML-assisted)."⁶

Even if a model's output falls between 0 and 1, it should first be called a classification score or fusion score; interpreting it as an actual event probability requires checking calibration and the deployment distribution. Feature importance helps in understanding the model but cannot by itself prove that the model's inferences have causal validity.

## The physical model is not the final judge

The physical drag residual compares the observed orbital change with the decay the model expects. Case 5 also revealed that when continuous thrust is absorbed by parameters self-calibrated for each satellite, the residual is closer to a "deviation relative to usual behavior" and cannot be read directly as thrust magnitude.²

Therefore, a large residual does not equal a confirmed maneuver, and a residual near zero does not guarantee the absence of thrust. The physical path provides another set of checkable hypotheses; event interpretation must still combine data quality, mission context, and external evidence. The full content of Case 5 and Case 30 is in Chapter 5.³

On July 15, 2020, the Russian satellite Cosmos 2543 released an object near another Russian satellite; the United States cataloged it as 45915. Observing a separation does not confirm its purpose.

### Building a chain of evidence from public data

Case 1 places data preprocessing, rules, statistics, physical residuals, classifiers, fusion, and manual review in a single architecture diagram (Figure 7-1 has been updated to follow the R7 workflow in the final report). From it, readers can see that the results of the various methods are not isolated from one another; each is evidence with a source and a purpose.⁴

Starting from a new TLE, the interpretation workflow summarized in this book is: confirm the source and epoch, build a consistent element or residual signal, flag with the L1 rules and merge into candidate events, then compare against the scores of the auxiliary channels to assign an evidence grade, and only then compare against reference data and carry out manual review. This is this book's summary of the workflow based on the final report; it has not re-verified the execution paths of all the programs.⁶

### Different validations cannot be merged into one

Case 1 mentions 14 development satellites and 9 external held-out satellites, which according to the final report belong to the NASA/ILRS external benchmark; Case 9's generalization test held out 56 of 284 Starlink satellites, with an AUC of 0.980. The two designs do not use the same set of satellites, nor do they evaluate the same method, so the sample size of one and the AUC (area under the ROC curve, a measure of a classifier's overall discriminative power) of the other cannot be pieced together into a single performance claim.⁴,⁶

In the final report's three-layer comparison, the fusion scorer's overall recall under the acceptance basis is 0.948 and its large-event recall 0.970; the large-event recall for contract acceptance is 0.973. These numbers each belong to their corresponding evaluation populations; they cannot be read as "23 external satellites jointly achieving the same set of metrics," nor extrapolated to any unknown constellation. Case 9 notes that its numbers were read from the output of an offline batch evaluation on 2026-08-03; they are frozen evaluation results, not live queries.⁴,⁶

### Fusion inputs must state their version

The overview narrative of Case 1 also draws rule scores and machine-learning classification scores into the fusion; the final report, by contrast, states explicitly that the inputs of the current fusion scorer are five channels (CUSUM, BOCPD, SSA, MAD 3σ, and the drag residual), each taking statistics within a ±24-hour window around the event, without L1 rule scores or Model 1 scores.¹,⁴,⁶

This chapter follows the final report. The final report also lists a "fusion scorer v2," which is a research version not connected to the online system; when Chapter 10 discusses the five-channel baseline, its inputs, aggregation method, and data snapshot must still be stated and cannot be inferred from the overview narrative.⁶

### The limits and value of AI

Case 2 classifies rules, statistics, and physical models as the non-AI path, classifies LightGBM, Isolation Forest, and the fusion scorer as traditional machine learning, and records the experience that bi-GRU did not deliver the expected gain. This shows that the system did not hand all of the analysis to a single neural network.⁵

The final report compares the three layers on the same set of evaluation units at the same operating point (FPR ≤ 0.05), presenting two evaluation bases side by side:⁶

| Method | Uses machine learning | Basis 1 recall | Basis 1 large-event recall | Basis 2 recall | Basis 2 large-event recall |
|---|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| Random baseline | — | 0.044 | 0.043 | 0.049 | 0.048 |
| L1 rules | No | 0.251 | 0.333 | 0.085 | 0.161 |
| Best single L2 method (CUSUM) | No | 0.444 | 0.437 | 0.235 | 0.292 |
| Fusion scorer (current system) | Yes | 0.948 | 0.970 | 0.472 | 0.643 |

Basis 1 is the basis for contract acceptance, with a fusion-scorer AUC of 0.981; Basis 2 takes negatives only from windows within each satellite's MEME ground-truth coverage and also includes small events among the positives, and the fusion scorer's AUC falls to 0.814, closer to the system's performance on new data. The ranking is the same under both bases: fusion scorer > best single L2 method > L1 rules > random baseline. The earlier numbers in the case collection (random 0.05126, L1 0.2513, CUSUM 0.4958, fuser 0.9464) belong to a different comparison.⁵,⁶

These are all numbers under specific comparison conditions; they do not represent the performance ceiling of all non-AI methods, and the gap cannot be attributed entirely to "using AI."

The fuser uses both more evidence and a different decision method. Under Basis 1, the fusion scorer detected all 331 events hit by L1 and L2 and another 235 missed by both; under Basis 2, it recovered 712 events missed by the first two layers but also missed 142 that the first two layers detected.⁶ To quantify the pure model gain, one would also need to control the data split, input features, operating point, and evaluation unit. The more reasonable conclusion is: both bases support the value of fusion, but they do not produce a general ranking of rules, statistics, and deep models.

### Explainability is not a physical guarantee

Case 2 argues for keeping a non-AI path on the grounds that it allows the source of a judgment to be traced. This design philosophy is worth keeping; but statements such as "holds for any satellite" or "a complete physical guarantee" are too strong, because the same case material already lists limitations regarding orbital regime, reentry, and model applicability.⁵

Rules can be completely transparent yet still set wrongly; physical models can rest on sound equations yet still be inaccurate because of errors in attitude, parameters, or density. Credibility must be built through scope of applicability and validation; it is not obtained automatically by being labeled "non-AI."

**Sidebar: Tiangong-1's reentry window**

In 2018, the 13 member agencies of the IADC made the reentry of Tiangong-1 their annual test campaign, hosted by ESA's Space Debris Office. At the end of March, ESA said that the expected stream of solar particles had had no effect, the upper-atmosphere density had not increased as predicted, and the reentry window had therefore shifted later. In its final update on April 1, ESA still stressed that it was impossible at any time to give a precise time and location. The actual reentry occurred at about 00:15 (UT) on April 2, over the South Pacific. When the density forecast changes, the physical prediction moves with it, and in the end it can still only give a range.

## From scores to usable alerts

This study describes fused alerts in terms of a research operating point at which the FPR (false-positive rate) does not exceed 0.05. This is a threshold setting under specific evaluation conditions and does not guarantee that the FPR will stay within limits during any deployment period.¹

The FPR measured for contract acceptance is 0.0498, corresponding to 214 false alarms among 4,294 quiet windows, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.044–0.057: the point estimate falls within the threshold, while the upper end of the interval still exceeds 0.05.⁶ Case 9's held-out-satellite test lists an FPR of 0.07015, and its out-of-time test lists 0.04402. This shows that even if the threshold is determined using only training data, results on deployment or held-out data may still differ. The full comparison is in Chapter 10; here we only remind readers not to confuse the target operating point with the value realized in testing.²,³

**Sidebar: The 60 meters between OneWeb and Starlink**

On March 30, 2021, the U.S. 18 SPCS initially predicted that OneWeb-0178 would come within about 60 meters of Starlink-1546, with a probability of collision of 1.3%. LeoLabs assessed the risk as lower, and OneWeb ultimately chose to maneuver. SpaceX later told the FCC that the actual closest distance was about 1,120 meters and that the probability never exceeded its own threshold; the two sides gave differing accounts of whether the automated collision-avoidance system had been "turned off" or "paused." The same conjunction led to different action decisions because of new observations, data sources, and thresholds.

This book recommends that an event card contain at least four kinds of information: the signal and channel contributions, data quality, model and threshold versions, and the evidence needed for review. Before probability calibration is completed, the output should be labeled as a score; when data are insufficient, it should be labeled as unable to be reliably determined, and a low score must not be equated directly with no maneuver.

- [ ] Can explain that rules are traceable, statistics are quantifiable, and machine learning is integrative, as well as the limitations each still has.
