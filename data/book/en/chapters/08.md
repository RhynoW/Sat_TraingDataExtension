# Chapter 8　The Rule Layer: A Reason for Every Alert

## Chapter overview

On June 3, 1980, NORAD's computers sent routine messages to the Strategic Air Command and the National Military Command Center as usual. One field in the messages recorded the number of missile launches detected; normally it was a row of zeros. That day, some of the "0"s were replaced by "2"s: first "002," then "200," read as 2 and then 200 incoming submarine-launched missiles.

The Strategic Air Command ordered bomber crews to board and start their engines; the National Emergency Airborne Command Post taxied into position but did not take off; the Pacific Command's airborne command post took off and stayed aloft for several hours. What brought things to a halt was another set of data: the missile early-warning sensors had seen nothing, and commanders canceled further action on that basis. On June 6, the same error occurred again.

The Pentagon attributed the cause to a faulty integrated circuit costing 46 cents and to "poor message design," but the task force was not entirely certain. Secretary of Defense Harold Brown told President Carter that they had to be prepared for another, unrelated failure to produce a false alarm again someday.

A field changing from 0 to 2 was enough to make bombers start their engines. How can an alert rule leave checkable reasons for its judgments?

The task of the rule layer is to write "why this record deserves a look" as checkable conditions and hand them to analysts for interpretation; it does not replace analysts. Using TLE differences and six rules, P1–P6, this study explains how to reduce false alarms caused by atmospheric drag and data conditions in public orbital data of limited accuracy; according to the final report, R7 actually adopts five of them, and P3 is excluded from the determination after validation.¹,⁵

This chapter treats rules as reviewable decision components: each rule should have a clear scope of applicability, trigger reasons, suppression reasons, and known failure modes. Transparent rules are not always correct; transparency only allows errors to be found, corrected, and incorporated into regression tests.

## Learning goals

- Understand the value and limitations of TLE differences as the starting point for maneuver detection.
- Distinguish trigger conditions, suppression conditions, and quality gates.
- Understand the problem each of P1–P6 addresses.
- Use Case 22 to understand how the rule layer was validated with experiments at two scales.
- Avoid extrapolating the rule layer's recall or thresholds into general guarantees for all orbits and all maneuvers.

## Starting with a simple question

The most intuitive rule is to compare the semi-major-axis difference between adjacent TLEs and flag a possible maneuver when the change exceeds a threshold. This method is easy to understand, but it mixes atmospheric drag, data errors, observation conditions, and real thrust into a single number.¹

Therefore, when designing the rule layer, rather than pursuing the largest change, first ask:

- Can this change be explained by a known physical process?
- Is this comparison based on comparable data conditions?
- Is this threshold adjusted by altitude, population, or environment?
- After triggering, what evidence should the system output to the analyst?

## Rules, suppression, and gates

This book recommends splitting the rule layer into three kinds of components. This is an engineering interpretation framework; according to the final report, the system labels each TLE as good, suspect, or rejected at ingestion, the detection layer down-weights suspect data, and events showing a "rose and then fell back" pattern are graded D, with the data checked first and the grade then recomputed; the division into three kinds of components, however, remains this book's own arrangement.⁵

| Component | Question | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger rule | Does the signal exceed the threshold? | Candidate event and trigger reason |
| Suppression rule | Is there a known reason to lower confidence? | Suppression flag and reason |
| Quality gate | Are the data sufficient to support a judgment? | Down-weighting, deferral, or manual review |

The three must be recorded separately. If "insufficient data" and "known drag" are both written into the same suppression flag, it will later be impossible to tell whether the system raised no alert because of physical conditions, data quality, or threshold design.

## The roles of P1–P6

P1–P6 are six rules for eliminating atmospheric-drag false alarms. Case 22 in this project's case collection explains that each of the six targets one misclassification scenario; in the R7 version of the final report, P2 is the core detector, P5 and P6 adjust the threshold multiplier, P1 is a suppression rule, P4 is neighbor marking (marking a transition that exceeds the threshold together with the next 2 transitions), and P3 is not used after validation. The case collection once called P4 "multi-window supplementary detection," which is an earlier design description.¹,⁵

| Rule | Type | Trigger condition and effect | What must not be over-interpreted |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 drag-decay suppression | Suppression | When 5 consecutive Δa values are all below 0.1 km, there is no jump of 2 km or more within the window, and B\*>0, the change is treated as monotonic small-step decay caused by atmospheric drag and suppressed; transitions exceeding the effective threshold are not suppressed by P1. | Cannot prove that every decline is due to drag.|
| P2 altitude-adaptive threshold | Detection | \|Δa\| exceeds the altitude threshold: about 2.0 km at 400 km, decreasing with altitude, and 0.4 km above 700 km; the actual determination uses the "effective threshold," obtained by multiplying P2 successively by the P5 and P6 multipliers. | Cannot replace satellite-by-satellite or period-by-period quality checks.|
| P3 high-drag satellite suppression (B\* auxiliary condition) | Not used | The original design suppressed small declines when B\* was abnormally high; the final report's rule-by-rule ablation and validation against FORMOSAT-7 precise ground truth show that P3 has no effect on either recall or false alarms, so R7 excludes it from the determination. | The risk of the original design is that B\* is also elevated when a continuously propelled constellation lowers its orbit, so it may wrongly block real orbit lowering.|
| P4 neighbor marking | Neighbor marking | Marks a transition that exceeds the effective threshold or another orbital-element threshold together with the next 2 transitions, to extend event boundaries and connect adjacent marks; the neighbor-marked transitions themselves do not necessarily exceed the threshold. Removing P4 would reduce false alarms in IDS quiet periods by about 40%, but recall of visible burns would drop from 68 to 63, so it is retained. | Neighbor marking does not mean the marked transitions are themselves anomalous; not triggering cannot prove there was no real event.|
| P5 solar-activity adjustment | Adjustment | Adjusts the P2 threshold according to F10.7 (the solar flux index); the multiplier is parabolic, ×1.0 at or below 70 sfu, ×1.6 at 200 sfu, capped at ×1.8; F10.7 is the observed value on the UTC day of the epoch of the later TLE in the transition. | A weather index cannot be treated as complete density ground truth.|
| P6 orbital-population adjustment | Adjustment | Adjusts the multiplier by inclination population (53° band ×1.0, sun-synchronous ×1.2, mid-inclination ×1.1); for MEO, GEO, and HEO the threshold is changed to 0.05 km. | A population average cannot be treated as the ground truth for an individual.¹,⁵ |

Besides these six rules, inclination and eccentricity have independent thresholds (|Δi|>0.02°, |Δe|>0.001); the RAAN residual (ΔΩ) was shown in validation only to add false alarms, and R7 does not use it as a mark. The final marks are "effective-threshold marks ∪ other orbital-element marks ∪ P4 neighbor marks," minus P1 suppression.⁵

The risk of P3 wrongly blocking real events was recorded in the 2026-10-21 addendum to Case 22 in this project's case collection: the addendum states that the measured impact is very small; for detailed analysis, see Case 30 in Chapter 5. The final report further confirmed, through large-sample rule-by-rule ablation and validation against FORMOSAT-7 precise ground truth, that P3 has no effect on either recall or false alarms, so R7 does not include P3.⁵

These rules keep the system from treating every semi-major-axis change as the same kind of event; the risk is that suppression conditions may mask real maneuvers. The rule layer must therefore keep records of suppressed events for later review and rule tuning.

**Sidebar: The Gannon storm of May 2024**

From May 10 to 12, 2024, the strongest geomagnetic storm in more than two decades struck, with ap peaking at 400. An analysis by Parker and Linares indicated that before the storm, about 300 of roughly 10,000 active low-Earth-orbit payloads were maneuvering; after the storm, thousands of satellites began maneuvering en masse, mainly due to Starlink's autonomous orbit maintenance. At the same time, drag decay across the whole catalog was also accelerating. Looking back with this book's methods, examining only semi-major-axis jumps would mix the two together; and if P5 uniformly relaxed thresholds, it might also suppress the real compensating maneuvers.

## A threshold is not a single number

The semi-major-axis threshold of L1 is not a fixed number but an effective threshold obtained by multiplying the P2 altitude threshold successively by the P5 (solar activity) and P6 (orbital population) multipliers. This fits the actual conditions of low Earth orbit better than a single absolute threshold, but "adaptive" does not mean "automatically correct."¹,⁵

This book recommends that every threshold carry at least:

- The applicable altitude band and population.
- The data time window and sampling conditions.
- The statistic used in the calculation.
- The trigger and suppression thresholds.
- Known failure scenarios.
- The version and regression test results.

Without these fields, even if a threshold produces high scores, it is hard to judge whether it remains reliable for different satellites, different periods, or different data conditions.

**Sidebar: Where the ISS's 10⁻⁴ comes from**

According to NASA's Carpenter and Markley, citing a paper by Foster (they give it as 1997; the original appears in the proceedings of the Third European Conference on Space Debris in 2001), the International Space Station's rule is: if the probability of collision exceeds 10⁻⁴ and the conjunction geometry is stable in three of the four most recent state-vector updates, avoid when feasible. Foster's original paper estimated an average of about two avoidance maneuvers per year, with residual risk of about 0.15–0.22 (prediction horizons of 8 to 24 hours); Carpenter and Markley summarized it as reducing total risk by about 80%. 10⁻⁴ is not a physical constant but a number arrived at by trading off "how many maneuvers per year" against "how much residual risk to tolerate."

## Main case: Case 22

> **Evidence level: A | Independently recomputed**
>
> **Data sources**: Experiments at two scales: 30 days, 14,019 satellites (P1–P4 ablation); 54 days, 14,090 low-Earth-orbit satellites (adding P5–P6, observation window 2026-05-01 to 06-23). In addition, 99 real orbit-shift events observed in MEME precise ephemerides were used for independent held-out validation.
>
> **Ground truth**: The two main experiments use the satellite's propulsion capability as proxy ground truth (10,186 positives in the 54-day experiment), not event-by-event external ground truth; the held-out validation of 99 events uses SpaceX MEME precise ephemerides as the reference.
>
> **Key assumptions**: Natural decay caused by atmospheric drag follows a monotonic, small-step, continuous pattern that can be distinguished from propulsive maneuvers; megaconstellations in the same inclination population have similar drag characteristics and can share one baseline multiplier.
>
> **Limitations**: The 54-day experiment had an overall recall of 26.9% and a false-alarm rate of 5.4% (Precision@1000 of 98.2%); the event-level recall of the 99-event held-out validation was 57.6%; an attempt at weak supervision using conjunction data messages (CDMs) produced no usable quantitative evidence; P3 may wrongly block real orbit lowering by continuously propelled constellations (the 2026-10-21 addendum states that the measured impact is very small; the final report has confirmed that P3 has no effect, and R7 does not adopt it); the experiments above belong to the case collection's rule version, which differs from R7's current rules (P2×P5×P6 effective threshold + P4, without P3 and ΔΩ).
>
> **When not to use this method**: When a continuously propelled constellation lowers its orbit, the rule layer alone should not be relied on for the determination (P3 in the case-collection version may wrongly block it, and R7 has removed P3; in addition, L1 looks only at single-step changes, so small thrusts spread over several days may still go unmarked); targets undergoing natural reentry with rapidly falling perigees should first pass the reentry gate (see Chapter 12); the rule layer's recall and thresholds must not be extrapolated into general guarantees for all orbital regimes and all maneuver types.

**Data specification**: Nature measured; Period experiment 1 about 30 days (start and end dates not stated in the case file), experiment 2 2026-05-01 to 2026-06-23 (54 days); TLE count not stated in the case file (samples counted by satellite: experiment 1 14,019 satellites, experiment 2 14,090 satellites with 10,186 positives, plus held-out validation on 99 real MEME orbit-shift events); Threshold no fixed threshold (P2 adapts to altitude, ≈2.0 km at 400 km and a floor of 0.4 km at 700 km and above; P5 multiplies according to F10.7 from ×1.0 at 70 sfu to ×1.6 at 200 sfu; P6 applies inclination-population multipliers); False-alarm rate experiment 2 FAR 5.4%, Precision@1000 = 98.2%; experiment 1 (P1–P4 ablation) false positives 68 → 29, precision 94.8% → 97.5%.

### Positioning the case

The core question of Case 22 is "Can satellite maneuvers be detected using only public TLEs?" This study uses six rule ideas to explain how to reduce atmospheric-drag false alarms, and validates them with experiments at two different scales.²

This book treats Case 22 as a demonstration case for the rule layer, not as a complete validation of the entire system. The performance of the rule layer must be understood together with the statistical layer, the fusion layer, physical gates, and data quality.

### Experiments at two scales

Case 22 validates the rule ideas with two experiments of very different scales; they are different experiments and cannot be treated as the results of a single experiment or the same sample.²

The first experiment covers 30 days and 14,019 satellites and is used to compare the ablation effects of each of rules P1–P4; the second covers 54 days (observation window 2026-05-01 to 06-23) and 14,090 low-Earth-orbit satellites, adds P5–P6, and uses whether a satellite has propulsion capability as proxy ground truth, with 10,186 positives. The second experiment's overall recall was 26.9% and its false-alarm rate 5.4%; taking the top 1000 by score, precision (Precision@1000) was 98.2%. In addition, 99 real orbit-shift events observed in MEME precise ephemerides were used for independent held-out validation, with an event-level recall of 57.6%. Proxy ground truth is not event-by-event external ground truth, and these numbers belong to different experiments and different evaluation units, so they cannot be merged into a single score.²,⁴

The final report also evaluated R7's current rules against strict ground truth: Starlink ground-truth recall 0.322 (283 MEME satellites), false alarms in IDS quiet periods 0.85 per thousand satellite-days, recall of IDS visible burns 68/102, and rocket-body false alarms 0.16 per thousand satellite-days; the recall of all five rule combinations lies between 0.31–0.33, and the main reasons it is low are the thresholds and small maneuvers, not the way the rules are combined. In the three-layer comparison, L1 recall is 0.251 (FPR 0.062) under evaluation basis 1 and 0.085 under evaluation basis 2. These numbers differ from Case 22 in evaluation unit and rule version and cannot be combined.⁵

When interpreting the rule layer's experimental results, at least the following fields must be confirmed.

| Field to know | Why it is necessary |
|---|---|
| Sample sizes of experiments 1 and 2 | Avoid mixing different populations into one group. |
| Data time window | Avoid directly comparing results from different periods. |
| Evaluation unit | Clarify whether the unit is the satellite, the event, the TLE pair, or the time window. |
| Threshold and rule version | Avoid merging results from different versions. |
| Recall, precision, and FPR | Avoid reporting only a single favorable metric. |
| Untriggered and suppressed events | Avoid looking only at hits. |

### What the rule layer can answer

The rule layer can answer: "Does this record deserve priority review, and why?" In the R7 workflow of the final report, candidate events are generated only by the L1 rules, and marks within 48 hours of each other are merged into one; but it cannot on its own answer: "Is this definitely a maneuver?" Candidate events still require review by analysts.⁵

**Sidebar: ESA's alert funnel**

ESA said in 2019 that a typical low-Earth-orbit satellite receives several hundred conjunction alerts per week; in ESA's active low-Earth-orbit fleet, about two alerts per satellite per week require detailed examination by analysts, each taking several hours, and on average each satellite needs more than one collision-avoidance maneuver per year. ESA believed that manual processes would not keep up as the number of satellites grew, and therefore planned to automate risk assessment. Filtering from several hundred down to two relies on rules and thresholds; the value of the rule layer, too, lies in letting people look only at the few that need looking at.

A good rule output should include:

- The triggered rule number and threshold.
- The TLE epochs compared and the data interval.
- The change in the semi-major axis or other elements.
- Known drag or population conditions.
- Quality status and the reason for down-weighting.
- Suggested data for follow-up review.

With these fields, what the analyst sees is not just a red flag; they can reconstruct the judgment process.

### National security implications

From Case 22 we can conclude:

> **The value of the rule layer lies not in catching every anomaly, but in giving every candidate event a checkable reason.**²

When used in national security settings, the rule layer must be explainable, auditable, and regression-testable, and must indicate its own scope of applicability; it must be able to down-weight when data are insufficient, and suppressed events must also be preserved for review.

## The division of labor between the rule layer and other layers

The rule layer does not replace the fusion layer, nor is it subordinate to the statistical layer. In the R7 workflow of the final report, the rule layer decides the candidate events: after L1 marks are merged within 48 hours into candidate events, the system checks whether L2 statistical change points, the drag residual, the ML window model, and the fusion scorer also responded within 24 hours before or after the event; these auxiliary channels affect only the evidence grade (E3, E2, E1, D) and neither add nor remove events. Periods that L1 did not mark but to which other methods responded are listed separately as low-confidence review items and are not counted as candidate events. A table of the division of labor among the layers is in Chapter 7.⁵

Each channel can be computed in parallel, but the determination order for formal reports has been fixed by the R7 workflow of the final report; other systems may adjust to deployment conditions and need not copy it.⁵
