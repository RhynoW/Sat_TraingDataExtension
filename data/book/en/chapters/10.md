# Chapter 10　The Fusion Scorer: Five Witnesses, One Confidence Score

> *This English edition is an AI-assisted translation of the Traditional Chinese manuscript (v1.3, 2026-11-19). Where they differ, the Chinese original prevails.*

## Chapter overview

In the early hours of March 26, 2016 (Japan time), Hitomi, the Japanese X-ray astronomy satellite launched just over five weeks earlier, completed an attitude maneuver to point its telescope at an active galactic nucleus. From 03:02 to 03:13 the Uchinoura ground station was in contact, and everything was normal.

At 05:49 a ground station in Spain made contact again: the attitude was abnormal, power was low, and the temperature distribution was wrong.

JAXA later inferred that at about 04:10, during an arc not visible from the ground, things had already gone wrong. After the maneuver, the satellite's estimate of the inertial reference unit's bias rate rose temporarily; it should have been corrected back by star tracker data, but this time it stayed high. The system therefore "believed" the satellite was rotating when it was not. The reaction wheels worked to cancel this nonexistent rotation and in doing so actually set the satellite spinning; after it entered safe mode and switched to thrusters, inappropriate control parameters caused the thruster firings to speed up the rotation. In the end, the solar panels and other components broke off at their roots and separated. JAXA estimated the separation at about 10:37, and the U.S. side assessed the breakup at about 10:42 (both Japan time).

An estimate that had never been independently checked was treated by the system as fact. When a model believes completely in the numbers it computes, who checks it?

What the fusion scorer deals with is the question of which combinations are most worth reviewing first when multiple sources of evidence may contradict one another, overlap, or each be limited in its own way; its task is not to pick "the strongest single model." This study uses five witnesses as a metaphor for five input channels, and uses Case 9, Case 16, and Case 23 to illustrate generalization, deep models, and label leakage.¹

This chapter treats the fusion scorer as a decision component that requires rigorous validation. Its output must be interpreted together with data quality, rule suppression, the statistical channels, physical residuals, and human review; a single high score cannot replace a complete chain of evidence.

## Learning goals

- Understand the inputs, outputs, and scope of applicability of the fusion scorer.
- Distinguish the LightGBM classifier from the HistGradientBoosting fusion scorer.
- Understand the difference between the unseen-satellite hold-out test and the out-of-time test.
- Use Case 16 to appreciate the value of a deep model's negative result.
- Use Case 23 to understand how label leakage distorts model performance.
- Avoid treating a research operating point, a classification score, or in-domain test results as a deployment guarantee.

## What the fusion scorer is asking

The fusion scorer does not presume that any channel is necessarily correct. What it asks is:

> Among multiple sources of evidence, which combinations are most worth reviewing first?¹

Before answering this question, the inputs must be confirmed. The five witnesses are CUSUM, BOCPD, SSA, 3σ-MAD, and the NRLMSIS drag residual. The first four are statistical change-point channels: CUSUM is the cumulative deviation; BOCPD is Bayesian online change-point detection; SSA here refers to Singular Spectrum Analysis (not the abbreviation for space situational awareness); 3σ-MAD sets a threshold based on the median absolute deviation. The NRLMSIS drag residual is what remains after the orbital decay caused by drag is first estimated with the NRLMSIS atmospheric density model and then subtracted from the observed change. According to the final report, the fusion scorer (HistGradientBoosting) uses as features the statistics of these five channels within a ±24-hour window (maximum, mean, and 90th percentile), and is trained on MEME precise-ephemeris ground truth for 284 Starlink satellites.¹,⁷

The overview narrative of Case 1 in this project's case collection once also listed the rule score and the ML classification score as fusion inputs; the final report states clearly that the current fusion scorer's inputs are only the five channels above, and that the rules and Model 1 are separate determination and auxiliary channels in R7.¹,⁷

This book therefore follows the final report and describes the fusion scorer's inputs as five channels; when citing numbers from other versions or from the overview narrative, their inputs, aggregation method, and data snapshot must still be stated.⁷

## Five witnesses are not five times the evidence

Comparing the five input channels to five witnesses aids understanding, but must not be misread as five times the evidence or five times the confidence.¹

| Channel | Core question | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| CUSUM | Is there a sustained deviation? | Sensitive to trends and the choice of baseline.|
| BOCPD | Is there a likelihood of a change point? | A posterior is not a confirmed event.|
| SSA | Is there a trend or residual anomaly? | Window length and reconstruction choices affect results.|
| 3σ-MAD | Does it deviate beyond a robust outlier scale? | Sensitive to sample size and time spacing.|
| NRLMSIS drag residual | Can known drag explain the change? | A large residual does not mean a maneuver is confirmed.¹ |

The five channels may complement one another, or they may overlap. If they all rely on the same set of TLEs, the same time window, or the same comparison baseline, the independence of the evidence declines. Whether the fusion scorer has value depends on whether each channel provides different and checkable information; the number of channels by itself says nothing.

## A score is not a probability

The goal of the fusion scorer is not to give an absolute "yes or no" answer, but to provide rankable results, an adjustable operating threshold, and a quantifiable false-alarm cost; in the R7 workflow of the final report, it does not generate candidate events, but serves only as an auxiliary channel affecting the evidence grade (score ≥ 0.659), and provides low-confidence review items for Starlink satellites.⁷ This study once described the first of these as a "rankable maneuver probability"; in light of the explanation below in this section, until calibration is complete, this output should be called a rankable score.¹

This book recommends that the fusion scorer's output be labeled with:

- The composite score.
- The ranking of candidate events.
- The trigger threshold and its version.
- Each channel's contribution or importance.
- The data quality status.
- The evidence needed for review.

To call the output a "maneuver probability," calibration and validation on the deployment distribution must first be completed. Until then, a score of 0.9 only means that the model considers the event worth reviewing first; it does not mean the true event probability is 90%.

**Sidebar: The worse the data, the lower the probability of collision?**

In 2019, Balch, Martin, and Ferson published a paper in *Proceedings of the Royal Society A* discussing "probability dilution" in satellite conjunction analysis: the larger the observation noise, the lower the computed probability of collision becomes, making things look safer. The authors argue that this is a symptom of a more fundamental problem of "false confidence," which leads to persistent underestimation of collision risk; this is the researchers' claim, and other scholars have since written rebuttals. Its relevance to this section is this: a small number does not mean safety; one must first ask what quality of data it was computed from.

### The unseen-satellite hold-out test

Case 9 asks: is the model still accurate on "satellites it has never seen"? The method is to randomly hold out 20% (56 satellites) as whole groups, with a fixed random seed, from the 284 Starlink satellites that have precise-ephemeris ground truth, train the model on the other 228, and then apply it directly to these 56 never-seen satellites.²

This evaluation uses data from 284 Starlink satellites within a 67-day dense-coverage window (2026-05-02 to 07-08, 19,066 satellite-days in total), with 597 positive and 6,636 negative evaluation units.²

The design aims to prevent the model from merely memorizing each satellite's individual characteristics without learning "what a maneuver looks like." Case 9 also stresses that the decision threshold is determined entirely from training-fold data; test-fold data may not be touched even "for choosing the threshold."²

The ground truth here comes from MEME precise ephemerides. MEME is SpaceX's own force model and predictions, not measured truth (see Chapter 5).

### The out-of-time test

Case 9 also lists an out-of-time test: the model is trained on data from the first 60% of the time span and then blind-tested on the last 40%, which lies entirely in the future, to check whether the model can handle time drift and future data.²

Both tests are rigorous, but they answer different questions:

| Test | Question answered | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| unseen-satellite | Is it still accurate on a satellite it has not seen? | Does not mean time drift has been handled.|
| out-of-time | Is it still accurate at a future time? | Does not mean generalization to new satellites has been handled.² |

**Sidebar: Google Flu Trends' overestimate (a non-space analogy)**

Google Flu Trends estimated the proportion of influenza-like-illness doctor visits from search volume. According to a February 2013 report in *Nature*, its estimate for the 2012–13 flu season was more than double the figure from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Lazer et al., writing in *Science* in 2014, pointed out that the overestimation had existed for a long time: it overestimated in 100 of the 108 weeks from August 2011 to September 2013, and the more likely cause was changes to Google's own search algorithm. Once the way data are generated changes, the model quietly drifts; this is exactly the problem the out-of-time test is meant to catch.

### The boundaries of the performance figures

Case 9 lists the following results: the unseen-satellite test had an AUC of 0.9802, large-maneuver recall of 1, overall recall of 0.9487, a false-alarm rate (FPR) of 0.07015, and 1457 test units; the out-of-time test had an AUC of 0.9372, large-maneuver recall of 0.8, overall recall of 0.75, an FPR of 0.04402, and 2893 test units. These figures are read from the frozen output of a single offline batch evaluation (the output file date listed in Case 9 is 20260803), not results recomputed in real time. AUC is the area under the ROC curve; 1.0 is perfect and 0.5 is equivalent to random guessing.²

Section 7.3 of the final report lists the following results: for 56 Starlink satellites that never took part in training, AUC 0.980 and large-event recall 1.000 (81/81), consistent with the above; training on the earlier period and testing on the later period gives AUC 0.945 and large-event recall 0.783, which differ from the out-of-time numbers in Case 9's frozen output, and the final report should be followed when citing them. The final report also notes that out-of-time performance is lower than same-period testing, and that periodic retraining is required for deployment.⁷

These figures belong to a specific data snapshot, specific evaluation units, and a specific threshold version, and should not be extrapolated to all satellites, all orbits, or all times. They are research results and do not mean "the system has passed all deployment validation." The same Case 9 page also contains recall values obtained with a different evaluation method; recall values computed on different bases cannot be combined into a single performance claim.

After the above results there was a subsequent rerun whose basis differs from the figures earlier in this section; they cannot be combined, and a cell-by-cell comparison is inappropriate. According to the 2026-10-21 supplement to the technical appendix, the same design was rerun on the 2026-10-21 database with a corrected basis for quiet samples: the out-of-time AUC was about 0.60 (0.591 for the original model, 0.600 for the revised model), failing to sustain the earlier performance; the unseen-satellite AUC fell to 0.808, and 0.858 for the revised model (L3 v2). The threshold was set differently in the two runs: this time the threshold was taken from the negative samples among GroupKFold cross-validation scores within the training set, whereas the 2026-08-03 frozen output set the threshold using the training samples' self-predictions; the numbers of test units also differ (this time 2,855 out-of-time and 1,400 unseen-satellite). The technical appendix interprets this as follows: cross-satellite extrapolation still broadly holds, but out-of-time extrapolation was not sustained, and the earlier conclusion that the model "has out-of-time extrapolation ability" applies only to the 67-day window of the 2026-08-03 snapshot. A possible cause is time drift from the overall lowering of the orbital shells and changes in solar activity, which remains to be verified; in practice, deployment requires periodic retraining on the latest ground truth and resetting of the threshold.⁶

The final report did not adopt the numbers from this rerun; instead it discloses the method's limitations under evaluation basis 2 (using only negative samples within each satellite's ground-truth coverage period): the fusion scorer's recall is 0.472 and AUC 0.814, clearly lower than the acceptance numbers (0.948, 0.981). The acceptance determination is not changed by this, but actual performance after deployment should be estimated at the magnitude of evaluation basis 2. The revised model v2 is a research version and has not yet been connected to the online system.⁷

**Sidebar: Change the split, and the champion loses to the baseline**

In 2019, ESA held a collision avoidance competition using 15,321 conjunction events and 199,082 conjunction data messages (CDMs) from 2015–2019; 96 teams took part. The simplest baseline was to carry forward the risk value of the latest CDM, and only 12 teams beat it; the winner used not machine learning but hand-designed thresholds. After the competition, the organizing team switched to 505 stratified random splits: LightGBM averaged only 1.27% better than the baseline, while the winning method was 14% worse than the baseline. The paper points out that differences between the training and test distributions were the main reason most models failed to generalize.

### National security implications

The lesson of Case 9 is:

> Generalization is not a single score but a set of questions that must be tested separately.²

In practice, unseen-satellite and out-of-time results must be reported separately, and different data snapshots cannot be compared directly; thresholds must state their version and source, and performance figures must be accompanied by their evaluation units. Even a very high score does not mean that all leakage and bias have been ruled out.

## Main case 2: Case 16

> **Evidence level: A | Independently recomputed**

> **Data sources**: This project's bi-GRU (Model 3, a 2-layer bidirectional GRU) trained on Starlink data, with the same 4 physical-residual channels as Model 2 as input; out-of-distribution checks used FORMOSAT-3A, which is always excluded from training; in addition, 4 deep-model concept proxies were tested under leave-one-satellite-out cross-validation on the 23-satellite benchmark. >

>
> **When not to use this method**: When the ground-truth resolution is coarser than the required labeling granularity, a per-time-point supervised sequence model should not be used as a detector; deep models trained on small samples from a single constellation should not be applied directly to out-of-distribution satellites (such as FORMOSAT-3A, which undergoes pure atmospheric decay).

**Data specification**: Nature measured; Period not stated in the case file; TLE count not stated in the case file (trained on Starlink data, FORMOSAT-3A used for out-of-distribution checks, and 4 concept proxies tested under leave-one-satellite-out cross-validation on the 23-satellite benchmark); Threshold no fixed threshold (threshold sweep to find the best episode F1); False-alarm rate false-alarm rate not stated in the case file (the FORMOSAT-3A out-of-distribution test is recorded only as producing many false alarms, not quantified); under the 23-satellite benchmark, the 4 concept proxies had precision 0.46-0.74 (recall 0.009-0.018).

### Why the deep model loses

Case 16 asks: why does a deep learning sequence model lose on this task? This study dissects the complete negative result of a bidirectional GRU (bi-GRU, a recurrent neural network that can look at both past and future time points, called Model 3 within the project), and shows that the ceiling AUC under per-point evaluation is only 0.572; the problem lies not in model capacity but in the resolution of the ground truth itself. Ground-truth resolution here means that the MEME precise-ephemeris ground truth has one grid point every 8 hours, and TLE epochs do not line up with the ground-truth grid; any supervised model that demands precise per-time-point labels will run into this granularity limit.³

If the same data are instead evaluated with the episode-level fusion scorer (HistGradientBoosting, not a sequence model), the AUC reaches 0.982. The two figures use different evaluation granularities; 0.982 is the result of the episode-level fusion scorer and cannot be read as the bi-GRU's result.³

The value of this conclusion lies in recording the failure faithfully rather than pretending that deep learning necessarily brings gains. Case 2 classifies the bi-GRU under deep learning that was "tried and abandoned"; this does not mean that all deep learning methods are infeasible.³

### The value of negative results

Case 2 records that the bi-GRU's performance collapsed on satellites it had not been trained on (OOD, out-of-distribution); the project faithfully recorded this negative result and switched to the more effective and more stable Model 2 (Isolation Forest unsupervised anomaly detection); according to the final report, Model 2 serves only as corroboration for non-Starlink satellites in the App's unified detection summary, does not decide results, and is not used in the R7 single-satellite report.⁷ Case 16 explains that this out-of-distribution test was performed on FORMOSAT-3A, which never took part in training and undergoes pure atmospheric decay, and that the bi-GRU produced many false alarms on it; it is not the same kind of test as the unseen-satellite hold-out test that Case 9 performed on Starlink satellites.³

This book treats this process as a methodological lesson. Model capacity does not equal the information content of the data, and ground-truth resolution limits the learnable ceiling; therefore one cannot look only at the model architecture but must check the ground-truth resolution, and OOD performance must also be tested separately. Negative results should be kept and should not be rewritten as successes; deep learning is not a cure-all either.

The final report's review of deep learning reached the same conclusion: on the same set of evaluation units, the LSTM autoencoder had an AUC of 0.704 and PatchTST 0.676, and the bi-GRU showed no stable discriminative power; all were below the fusion scorer (0.982) and were not included in the delivered system.⁷

### National security implications

The lesson of Case 16 is:

> No matter how complex a model is, it cannot exceed the information content of the data itself.³

When evaluating a detection model, check its architecture, the ground-truth resolution, and its OOD performance together, keep the failure results, and avoid treating deep learning as a cure-all.

## Main case 3: Case 23

>
> **Ground truth**: The internal training labels (`maneuver_detected`) are generated by rule-based flags; they are self-labels, not external ground truth; independent Starlink MEME ground truth is additionally used for external validation.

**Data specification**: Nature measured; Period not stated in the case file; TLE count not stated in the case file (14,023 satellites, with 20 aggregated TLE features extracted from each satellite's observation window); Threshold classifier threshold not stated in the case file (the fusion scorer threshold against which the three examples are compared is 0.129); False-alarm rate corrected internal test precision 99.5% (self-labels), external MEME validation precision 100% (recall 39.7%); Table 1 of the technical appendix records an internal test FPR of 0.12% for single-satellite supervised classification (Layer 3, LightGBM); the final report records that Model 1 takes 40 TLE orbital-change features (per record) as input, and that the R7 fusion scorer threshold is 0.659 and the ML window model threshold is 0.478, which differ from the settings in this case.

### How label leakage distorts performance

The title of Case 23 is "An idea for teaching AI to be a space detective." Using 20 aggregated TLE features and 14,023 satellites, it describes a LightGBM classifier (LightGBM is a gradient-boosted tree model; the case title calls it the "LightGBM fusion classifier") and an honest catch of a leak. This classifier performs a binary "maneuvered / not maneuvered" classification on each satellite's observation window; Case 23 also notes that the system has a separate fusion scorer trained on Starlink whose architecture differs from this classifier, and Case 1 records the latter as a HistGradientBoosting model.⁴

Case 23 records that a label leak was caught by the team itself during training, and only after it was fixed did the model's performance become credible. This process shows that label leakage can make a model look very good while it has in fact not learned generalizable maneuver features. Even after the fix, Case 23 records that in external validation against independent Starlink MEME ground truth, recall dropped to 39.7% (precision remained 100%), showing a generalization gap when the model is transferred across label sources; this figure uses a different evaluation basis from Case 9 and cannot be compared side by side.⁴

The final report records that the contract evaluated this original version trained on rule-based labels, with an external MEME event-level recall of 0.383; the current Model 1 has been retrained on Starlink MEME precise-ephemeris ground truth, with its input recorded as 40 TLE orbital-change features (Case 23 records 20 aggregated features; the relationship between the two is to be confirmed), and in R7 it serves only as the "ML window" auxiliary channel (probability ≥ 0.478 and an orbital change exceeding the physical threshold). Results from the two versions must not be mixed.⁷

Case 23's fix was to remove the 4 features that shared rule-based flags with the training labels and add two independent features; the split used a stratified random split at the satellite level (70%/15%/15%) so that the same satellite would not appear in both the training and test sets. After the fix, internal test precision was 99.5% and recall 97.5%. These results were scored against self-labels generated by rule-based flags, not against external ground truth, and are on a different basis from the 39.7% in the MEME external validation above.⁴

### Common sources of label leakage

This book recommends checking the following possible sources of leakage one by one:

- Features and labels share the same time window.
- Features are produced only after the event.
- Labels are generated from the same set of data.
- Evaluation units overlap with training units.
- Thresholds are tuned on the test data.
- The data snapshot and label timing are inconsistent.

Not all of these will necessarily occur, but every one must be checked. Label leakage does not disappear on its own, nor does it disappear because the model is more complex.

**Sidebar: The pneumonia model that learned to recognize hospitals (a non-space analogy)**

In 2018, Zech et al. published a study in *PLoS Medicine* that trained pneumonia detection models on a total of 158,323 chest X-rays from three institutions. A model trained on two hospitals combined had an internal AUC of 0.931, but only 0.815 at the third. The researchers found that the model could identify which hospital an image came from with over 99.95% accuracy, and the hospitals had different pneumonia prevalence rates; ranking by "which hospital" alone gave an AUC of 0.861. What the model may have learned was where the data came from, not the lesions themselves.

### National security implications

The lesson of Case 23 is:

> A model that performs very well is not necessarily free of leakage.⁴

When checking, look at the temporal relationship between features and labels, whether evaluation units overlap with training units, and which data the threshold was tuned on; keep a record of the leak-hunting process, and do not look only at a single metric.

## Division of labor between the fusion scorer and other layers

The fusion scorer does not replace the rule layer, nor is it subordinate to the statistical layer. In the R7 workflow of the final report, the fusion scorer does not generate candidate events; it serves only as an auxiliary channel within 24 hours before or after an event, affecting the evidence grade; for Starlink satellites, time points not marked by L1 but with a score ≥ 0.659 are listed as low-confidence review items. It is trained only on Starlink, and its above-threshold time points on non-Starlink satellites all fell within quiet periods in the ground-truth evaluation, so they are not listed as review items; events of non-Starlink satellites that meet the E3 condition only because a machine-learning channel is included are graded "E2 (ML-assisted)" instead.⁷

| Question | Component best used first |
|---|---|
| Does it exceed a known threshold? | L1 rule layer. |
| Does it deviate from the recent norm? | L2 statistical layer. |
| Do multiple channels jointly support it? | Auxiliary-channel check (L2 statistical change points, drag residual, ML window model, fusion scorer; in the final report's R7 workflow, affects only the evidence grade). |
| Can it be explained by drag or reentry? | Physics and applicability processing. |
| Are the data sufficient to judge? | Quality gate. |

This table is this book's arrangement of the engineering division of labor; each channel can be computed in parallel, but the determination order for formal reports has been fixed by the R7 workflow of the final report, and other systems may adjust to deployment conditions.⁷
