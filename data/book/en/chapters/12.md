# Chapter 12　The Reentry Gate: Knowing When Not to Use the Model

> *This English edition is an AI-assisted translation of the Traditional Chinese manuscript (v1.3, 2026-11-19). Where they differ, the Chinese original prevails.*

## Chapter overview

At 06:04 UTC on May 10, 2025, Germany's TIRA radar saw it once again: an object weighing about half a ton and about one meter in diameter. It was part of Kosmos 482, a 1972 Soviet Venus probe mission; that year its propulsion burn failed to free it from Earth, and it had circled in Earth orbit ever since, for 53 years.

The next pass was scheduled for 07:32. The radar searched in the planned direction and found nothing. The object had already reentered, but the exact time and place have never been confirmed by observation.

The answers from different parties did not agree: the EU Space Surveillance and Tracking (EU SST) gave 06:04 ±20 minutes; the European Space Agency had given 06:37 ±197 minutes in advance; The Aerospace Corporation gave 06:29 ±120 minutes; the U.S. post-event reentry notice gave 05:32 ±12 minutes, which some analysts pointed out contradicts the radar still seeing it at 06:04; Roscosmos stated 06:24, falling into the Indian Ocean, with no uncertainty range. The epoch of the last U.S. orbit was already about six hours before reentry.

When five agencies give five answers for the same object, which one should the reentry gate believe? And relying only on public TLEs, how far can the gate go?

The reentry gate does not predict when a satellite will fall; what it judges is whether, under the current orbital and data conditions, the model is still suitable for interpreting maneuvers. This chapter uses Case 12 to explain on which orbit types this system can be trusted and on which it cannot yet, and faithfully distinguishes the ranges that are validated, have limited validation, and are not applicable.¹

This chapter treats the reentry gate as a mechanism for "knowing when not to use the model." It does not treat all LEO decay as reentry, nor treat all high orbits as applicable; instead, based on orbital-regime capability, data conditions, and validation scope, it decides what level of judgment the system should output.

## Learning goals

- Understand the difference between the reentry gate and reentry prediction.
- Understand the uses and limitations of capability grading by orbital regime.
- Use Case 12 to understand the validated, limited-validation, and not-applicable ranges.
- Avoid treating a gate trigger as validation of full reentry prediction capability.
- Avoid treating model output as a universal guarantee for all orbits.

## What the reentry gate is asking

What the reentry gate must answer is:

> Are these orbital and data conditions still suitable for interpreting maneuvers with the model?¹

Before answering, the inputs must be confirmed. Case 12 records that the gate logic uses perigee altitude and the decay slope over the most recent 45-day window to distinguish satellites that are "reentering naturally" from those "maintaining orbit with thrusters" (for example, the ISS and Tiangong).¹ The final report states the specific conditions: using the most recent 45 days of data, a satellite is judged to be reentering naturally when its perigee is below 150 kilometers, or below 350 kilometers with a descent rate exceeding 2 kilometers per day, and its maneuver count is then output as 0.⁴

**Sidebar: The controlled deorbit of Tiangong-2**

Starting on July 18, 2019, Tiangong-2 was lowered from its operational orbit of about 380 kilometers with two propulsion burns, and on July 19 it performed a final deorbit burn, making a controlled reentry at 21:06 Beijing time, with a small amount of debris falling into the planned area of the South Pacific. Officials chose to deorbit it deliberately while propellant was still sufficient; Tiangong-1 the year before had made an uncontrolled reentry (see Chapter 7). Looked at with this book's method, a controlled deorbit appears in TLEs as a deliberate, large orbit lowering, entirely different from a natural decay curve, and the gate must be able to tell these two kinds of "descent" apart.

This book recommends that the reentry gate's output be labeled with:

- Perigee altitude and decay rate.
- Atmospheric density and solar activity indicators.
- Data quality status.
- The reason the gate was triggered.
- Recommended follow-up data for review.
- Scope of applicability and limitations.

With these fields, an analyst can reconstruct the reasoning process instead of seeing only a red flag.

## Capability grading by orbital regime

This study uses capability grading by orbital regime to describe the system's confidence and limitations on different orbit types.¹

| Grade | Coverage | Degree of validation | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Validated with confidence | Starlink LEO | High | Does not mean all LEO is covered.|
| Special handling but limited validation | Reentry determination | Medium | Does not mean full reentry prediction capability is validated.|
| Code path exists but lacks quantitative validation | GEO/MEO | Low | Does not mean deployment validation has been passed.|
| Independent branch not integrated | Galileo | Low | Does not mean it has been incorporated into the main system.|
| Known not applicable | HEO non-terminal phase (normal elliptical-orbit operating phase) | None | The terminal low-perigee phase is handled by the reentry gate; the gate does not cover the full HEO life cycle.|
| Preliminary supporting evidence | Sun-synchronous | None | Does not mean applicability has been ruled out.¹ |

In the table, LEO is low Earth orbit, GEO and MEO are geosynchronous orbit and medium Earth orbit respectively, and HEO is highly elliptical orbit. This table is this book's recommended engineering grading and does not mean every system must be graded in the same way; actual systems should adjust it according to deployment conditions, data sources, and validation scope.

For the two rows "Reentry determination" and "Sun-synchronous," Case 12 performed additional validation on 2026-09-10; the results are given in the sections below. The grading table itself retains its original grades.

**Sidebar: CAPSTONE's two mishaps in cislunar space**

NASA's CAPSTONE CubeSat launched in June 2022, bound for a near-rectilinear halo orbit near the Moon. Starting July 4, the team sent an incorrectly formatted command that disabled the radio, and together with a flight software defect, communications were interrupted until July 6. At the end of an orbit correction on September 8, a thruster valve was partially open, and the satellite went into an uncontrolled spin that was not stopped until October 7. The dynamics and tracking systems of cislunar space are entirely different from LEO TLEs, and such targets were always outside the scope of this book's methods.

## Main case: Case 12

> **Evidence level: A | Independently recomputed**
>
> **Data sources**: A compilation of the quantitative acceptance sections of the technical appendix and the actual logic of each code module, plus additional validation performed by this project: Starlink evaluated with 284 satellites that have MEME precise ephemerides (plus 56 held-out satellites not used in training); the reentry gate tested on 58 real satellites (16 confirmed reentries, 42 confirmed quiet); a total of 3,422 GEO/MEO/GEO+ targets routed to Model 2 in the database snapshot; the Galileo branch covering 30 satellites over a 120-day time window; OneWeb and Qianfan tested by synthetic injection (30 random trials per magnitude).
>
> **Ground truth**: Starlink checked against MEME precise ephemerides (SpaceX's predictions, not measured truth); for reentry, the ground truth is a physical criterion built by this project (perigee once fell below 120 kilometers, and no TLE for 14 days or more afterward), not independent third-party ground truth; GEO/MEO and non-Starlink constellations have no ground truth, and for OneWeb and Qianfan, known synthetically injected magnitudes are used instead.
>
> **Key assumptions**: The validation figures are credible only on data from the same kind of population as the training/validation data; the reentry gate uses perigee altitude and the decay slope over the most recent 45-day window to distinguish natural reentry from satellites maintaining orbit with thrusters.
>
> **Limitations**: There are only 16 reentry positives, and the lower bound of the Wilson 95% confidence interval is only 80.6%; for GEO/MEO only correct routing was validated, and only 2 of the 3,422 had case-by-case trajectory checks; the Galileo normal-direction error may be contaminated by numerical differentiation artifacts; the "zero anomalies" for OneWeb and Qianfan cannot distinguish whether the constellation is truly quiet or the model is insensitive; Qianfan's batch threshold is raised by its background to about 126 satellites/day. The synthetic injection part is a simulation under fixed assumptions.
>
> **When not to use this method**: Extrapolating Starlink validation figures to non-Starlink satellites; the normal elliptical-orbit operating phase of HEO (the apogee phase is dominated by third-body perturbations, and the drag residual model does not apply); treating a gate trigger as a reentry prediction or reentry confirmation; claiming maneuver detection recall or false-alarm rates for GEO/MEO; targets outside the LEO TLE regime, such as those in cislunar space.

**Data specification**: Nature measured + simulated (reentry gate measured, plus OneWeb and Qianfan batch injection tests); Period overall period not stated in the case file; the reentry gate decides using the decay slope over the most recent 45-day window, the Galileo branch uses a 120-day time window, and the batch injection test dates are OneWeb 2026-08-16 and Qianfan 2026-07-28; TLE count not stated in the case file (reentry gate: 58 satellites; targets routed to Model 2: 3,422), record counts used for the HEO residual comparison: CLUSTER II-FM8 668, CLUSTER II-FM7 667, ISS (ZARYA) 47589; Threshold reentry gate: perigee < 150 km within the most recent 45 days, or < 350 km with a descent rate > 2 km/day (final report §4.5); the confirmed-reentry criterion is perigee having fallen below 120 km with no TLE for 14 days or more afterward; batch determination uses a mean+3σ adaptive threshold (OneWeb K≈1.0 satellites/day, Qianfan K≈126 satellites/day); False-alarm rate reentry gate quiet specificity 42/42 = 100% (Wilson 95% CI [91.6%, 100%]); GEO/MEO and non-Starlink constellations not evaluated (no external ground truth).

### Positioning of the case

Case 12 asks: on which orbit types can this system be trusted? On which can it not yet be trusted? It uses grading to describe the validated, limited-validation, and not-applicable ranges.²

This book treats Case 12 as a case of an applicability-domain gate, not as a complete validation of the whole system. The gate's performance must be interpreted together with data quality, rule suppression, the statistical layer, the fusion layer, and physical residuals.

On the same day (2026-09-10), Case 12 also tested batch maneuver determination end to end: a 5-kilometer change in semi-major axis was injected simultaneously into N real satellites on the same day, and the full analysis was rerun. OneWeb's background baseline is about 1 satellite per day, and injecting 2 satellites simultaneously was enough to be flagged as a batch event; Qianfan's background baseline is about 126 satellites per day, so injecting 2 satellites simultaneously was not flagged, and it took 126 or more to trigger. In other words, Qianfan's batch threshold is raised by its own background, and dozens of satellites maneuvering simultaneously could still be judged normal. This is a deliberately idealized synthetic scenario, and the measured detection rates may be more optimistic than for real batch events.²

The final report illustrates the same phenomenon with an actual Qianfan orbit-insertion event in July 2026: on ordinary days 65–72 Qianfan satellites maneuvered significantly each day; on July 14 and 15 this rose to 82 and 91, still below the threshold of 113 computed at the time as the 30-day rolling mean plus 3σ, so no trigger occurred.⁴ The two sets of figures differ in data date and definition and should not be combined for comparison.

### Validated with confidence: Starlink LEO

Case 12 lists Starlink LEO as validated with confidence, meaning that for that data snapshot, that population, and those evaluation conditions, the system has established a comparable baseline. Case 12 also explains the boundaries of the validation: the validation population is limited to the 284 Starlink satellites with MEME precise-ephemeris ground truth available for checking (MEME is SpaceX's predictions, not measured truth; see Chapter 5), and the technical appendix also states that the results cannot be extrapolated to non-Starlink satellites.²

Case 12 also cites the hold-out test from Case 9: on 56 held-out Starlink satellites that took no part in training, the ROC-AUC was 0.980 and large-maneuver recall was 100% (81/81). This is still a generalization test within the same Starlink population and does not change the boundaries described above.²

The final report also discloses two limitations: when training on an earlier period and testing on a later one, the AUC drops to 0.945 and large-maneuver recall to 0.783, so periodic retraining is needed in deployment; if negative samples are taken only from each satellite's ground-truth coverage period, the fusion scorer's AUC is 0.814 and large-maneuver recall 0.643. Acceptance is judged on the former (0.982, 0.973), and the latter is disclosed as a methodological limitation.⁴

This therefore does not mean all LEO is covered. Different altitudes, inclinations, missions, and data conditions may still require revalidation.

### Special handling but limited validation: reentry determination

Case 12 lists reentry determination as having special handling but limited validation: the system has established a gate mechanism but has not yet completed validation of full reentry prediction capability.²

On 2026-09-10, Case 12 expanded the validation sample for the reentry gate: using a physical criterion that does not depend on manual labels, satellites whose perigee once fell below 120 kilometers and that had no TLE for 14 days or more afterward were treated as confirmed reentries, and the ISS, the Tiangong core module, and randomly sampled long-tracked Starlink satellites were treated as confirmed quiet. Among these 58 real satellites, all 16 confirmed reentries were judged correctly (16/16), and all 42 confirmed quiet satellites were also judged correctly (42/42); the lower bound of the Wilson 95% confidence interval for reentry recall is only 80.6%, and the lower bound for quiet specificity is 91.6%. Because there are only 16 positives and the ground truth is a criterion built by this project rather than independent third-party ground truth, this grade remains "limited validation"; what this test examined was the gate's determination, not reentry time prediction.² The validation formally listed in the final report is 6 cases, all judged correctly: the Van Allen Probes and Cluster Samba and Tango were judged to be reentering, and the International Space Station, Tiangong, and FORMOSAT-3 were judged not to be; the 58 satellites above are a supplementary test from the casebook and are not in the final report.⁴

**Sidebar: The ERS-2 prediction window**

In February 2024, the European Space Agency continually updated its reentry prediction for ERS-2: on February 8 the uncertainty was still ±63.76 hours, on February 16 ±26.62 hours, on February 20 it narrowed to ±7.48 hours, and the final prediction on February 21 was 17:05 UTC ±0.55 hours. ESA confirmed that it reentered over the North Pacific around 17:17 UTC, and explained that the difficulty of prediction came mainly from atmospheric density, which depends on model quality and current space weather. The window became narrow enough only on the last day; this is the work of reentry prediction, not of the gate.

This book recommends that the reentry gate clearly distinguish three things:

- Gate trigger: the system judges that the model is not suitable for interpreting maneuvers.
- Reentry prediction: the system estimates when the satellite will fall.
- Reentry confirmation: the system confirms with external data that reentry has occurred.

The three must not be conflated. A gate trigger does not mean reentry is confirmed, nor does it mean the reentry time has been predicted.

**Sidebar: Where did the Long March 5B core stage come down?**

In May 2021, the roughly 21-metric-ton core stage of Long March 5B Y2 made an uncontrolled reentry. The Aerospace Corporation's prediction window on May 4 was ±28 hours, narrowing on May 8 to 03:22 UTC on the 9th ±4 hours. After reentry, U.S. Space Command said it reentered over the Arabian Peninsula at about 02:15 UTC and that it was uncertain whether debris fell on land or at sea; the China Manned Space Agency announced 02:24 UTC, with the impact point in waters near the Maldives. Even at the "confirmation" step the two sides gave different accounts, so reentry confirmation must be listed separately, with its data source noted.

### Code path exists but lacks quantitative validation: GEO/MEO

Case 12 lists GEO/MEO as having a code path but lacking quantitative validation.² According to the final report, the app's unified detection summary hands non-Starlink targets (including GEO/MEO) to the NRLMSIS drag residual as the primary judge, with Model 2 (Isolation Forest unsupervised anomaly detection) only as corroboration; the formal R7 single-satellite report does not branch, and determines candidate events for GEO/MEO with the L1 rules as well (P6 changes the semi-major axis threshold for MEO/GEO/HEO to 0.05 kilometers), without using Model 2.⁴ What has been validated is only that these code paths exist and direct targets correctly, not the maneuver detection recall or false-alarm rate on these orbits.

On 2026-09-10, Case 12 added a quantification of scale: in the latest database snapshot, 3,422 targets were classified as GEO/MEO/GEO+, and in the unified detection summary all fall on the non-Starlink path (drag residual as primary judge, Model 2 as corroboration); only 2 of them had case-by-case trajectory checks, and the remaining 3,420 were not checked individually. This quantifies the size of the unchecked range; it is not a validation of detection accuracy.²

GEO/MEO should therefore be labeled "code path exists, quantitative validation not yet complete," should not be extrapolated to all GEO/MEO, and should be revalidated according to deployment conditions.

### Independent branch not integrated: Galileo

Case 12 lists Galileo as an independent branch not yet integrated: this branch compares precise ephemerides with TLE/SGP4 to assess the magnitude of prediction errors for Galileo satellites, and already covers all 30 Galileo satellites, but it is currently shown only on a separate tab in the App and has not yet been connected to the L1/L2/L3 fusion detection pipeline.²

Galileo should therefore be labeled "independent branch exists, not yet incorporated into the main system." The results of the error comparison should not be extrapolated to mean that maneuver detection covers Galileo; if it is to be incorporated, it should be revalidated according to deployment conditions.

### Known not applicable: HEO non-terminal phase

Case 12 lists the normal elliptical-orbit operating phase of HEO as known not applicable: orbital changes during the apogee phase are dominated mainly by third-body perturbations from the Moon, the Sun, and so on, rather than atmospheric drag, so the physical drag residual model does not apply here; the reentry gate is only a safety net for the terminal low-perigee phase, not a solution covering the full HEO life cycle.²

HEO should therefore be labeled known not applicable, and the handling of the terminal phase by the gate should not be extrapolated to other phases of HEO; if it must be used, it should be reassessed according to deployment conditions, and model output cannot be treated as ground truth.

### Preliminary supporting evidence: Sun-synchronous

Case 12 lists Sun-synchronous orbits as a gap awaiting testing, meaning the system has not yet completed testing for that range.²

On 2026-09-10, Case 12 added preliminary figures: taking the 23 external benchmark satellites of Case 4 as the sample, it fit a linear regression to each satellite's right ascension of the ascending node (RAAN) time series and determined whether each was strictly Sun-synchronous based on the measured nodal precession rate (not just the inclination). Of these, 9 were confirmed as strictly Sun-synchronous, with an L3 weighted recall of 54.5%; 14 were confirmed as not Sun-synchronous, with a weighted recall of 56.3%. The two differ by less than 2 percentage points, and no systematic weakness specific to Sun-synchronous orbits was seen; but after subgrouping the samples are even smaller and within-group variation is large, so Case 12 lists this as preliminary supporting evidence, not a completed stratified validation.²

Sun-synchronous orbits should be labeled as awaiting testing, should not be extrapolated to all Sun-synchronous orbits, and should be revalidated according to deployment conditions; model output cannot be treated as ground truth either.

### National security implications

The lesson of Case 12 is:

> A gate trigger does not mean reentry is confirmed, nor does it mean the reentry time has been predicted.²

When using this system, gating, prediction, and confirmation must be kept separate; each orbital regime must be labeled with its scope of applicability and limitations; unvalidated results must be kept; model output must not be treated as ground truth; and revalidation must be performed according to deployment conditions.

**Sidebar: Salyut 7 came down in Argentina**

At 03:25 UTC on February 7, 1991, the combined Soviet Salyut 7 space station and Kosmos 1686 made an uncontrolled reentry, and debris fell within Argentina, with no reported casualties. After Soyuz T-15 departed in 1986, it could only decay naturally under air drag; a European team at the time made lifetime predictions using radar data; according to reports, high solar activity caused it to fall earlier than expected. For the account of Skylab debris falling in Western Australia in 1979, see Chapter 5. Once decay is faster than expected, routine models should no longer be used for interpretation, and the matter should be handed over to dedicated reentry prediction.

## Division of labor between the reentry gate and other layers

The reentry gate does not replace the rule layer, nor is it subordinate to the statistical layer. According to the final report, it is the first step of routing in the app's unified detection summary, and when triggered the maneuver count is output as 0; the formal R7 single-satellite report instead determines candidate events with the L1 rules.⁴ It provides the applicability-domain judgment; the rule layer provides interpretable candidate events and reasons for suppression; the statistical layer, physical residuals, and the fusion scorer serve as auxiliary channels that check whether there is also a response around an event, and they affect only the evidence grade.⁴

**Sidebar: The week after Progress MS-08 undocked**

At about 02:16 UTC on August 23, 2018, the Russian Progress MS-08 cargo ship undocked from the Zvezda module of the International Space Station, then flew autonomously for about a week to conduct experiments; on August 30 it performed a deorbit burn of about 3 minutes, and the fragments that did not burn up fell into the Pacific no-fly zone. Undocking, free flight, and active orbit lowering form a regular sequence. Looked at with this book's method, the rule layer could identify it as a planned maneuver, and the reentry gate would take over after the orbit lowering; a single model need not handle everything.

| Question | Component best used first |
|---|---|
| Does it exceed a known threshold? | L1 rule layer. |
| Does it deviate from the recent norm? | L2 statistical layer. |
| Do multiple channels jointly support it? | Auxiliary-channel comparison (L2, drag residual, ML window model, fusion scorer); affects only the evidence grade. |
| Can it be explained by drag or reentry? | Physics and applicability processing. |
| Are the data sufficient to judge? | Quality gate. |
| Are the orbital and data conditions suitable for using the model? | Reentry gate. |

This table is this book's summary of the engineering division of labor; the channels can be computed in parallel, but the decision order for formal reports has been fixed by the R7 workflow in the final report, and other systems may adjust it to their deployment conditions. Under the final report's R7 single-satellite report workflow, candidate events are determined only by the L1 rules; the other components affect only the evidence grade, or list periods not flagged by L1 as low-confidence review items for analysts to interpret.⁴
