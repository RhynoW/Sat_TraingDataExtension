# Chapter 14　Maneuver Timing Detection: Knowing Not Just "Whether," but "When"

## Chapter overview

On July 16, 2020, the European Space Agency's ice-observing satellite CryoSat-2 fired its thrusters for nearly 600 seconds. This was a test burn, and also the start of a two-week campaign. The goal was to raise the orbit's semi-major axis by 887 meters so that it would periodically overlap with NASA's ICESat-2: every 19 orbits of CryoSat-2 and every 20 orbits of ICESat-2, the two would meet at the same place, about once every 1.5 days.

The 887 meters were split into 14 maneuvers: one test, then three batches of four maneuvers each, with a final fine adjustment on July 30, using a total of only 0.48 kilograms of fuel. During the process, the Space Debris Office found that the 14th maneuver carried a high collision risk and recommended not executing it as originally planned; after the flight dynamics team adjusted the timing, safety was verified once more. (An ESA press release gives a different account of "15 burns, about 900 meters.")

On average, each maneuver raised the orbit by only a few tens of meters. Could an observer with only TLEs find these 14 burns one by one? Or would they see only a slowly rising curve whose start and end cannot be distinguished?

What maneuver timing detection must answer is not only "was there a maneuver" but also "when did the maneuver happen." This chapter uses Case 15 to show how accurately the thrust vector of a maneuver can be recovered from TLEs, and faithfully discloses the failure conditions in different scenarios.¹

As for how precisely the timing itself can be pinned down, the final report compared against minute-by-minute MEME precise ephemerides: the median error of TLE-determined maneuver times is 2.74 hours, with 86.9% within 8 hours; the median detection latency in the contract metrics is 0.1 hours.⁴ These figures apply only to Starlink and MEME ground truth and cannot be extrapolated to other satellites.

Maneuver timing detection can be seen as an extension "from event existence to event localization." It must be interpreted together with data quality, rule suppression, the statistical layer, the fusion layer, and the physics gate; a single timestamp cannot replace a complete chain of evidence.

## Learning goals

- Understand the difference between maneuver timing detection and maneuver existence detection.
- Understand the uses and limitations of thrust vector inversion.
- Use Case 15 to understand the differences among impulsive chemical propulsion, the cross-track component, contamination from neighboring maneuvers, and continuous electric-propulsion thrust.
- Avoid treating the burn epoch (the estimated burn time) as a fully validated, precise burn time.
- Avoid extrapolating inversion results to all propulsion types.

## What maneuver timing detection is asking

The question maneuver timing detection must answer is:

> **When did the maneuver happen?**¹

Before answering, the comparison baseline must first be confirmed. The same timestamp may mean something entirely different for impulsive chemical propulsion versus continuous electric-propulsion thrust, under different data conditions, and for different missions.

This book recommends that maneuver timing detection distinguish at least three outputs:

| Output | Meaning | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Event existence | Was there a maneuver? | Does not mean the time has been localized. |
| Event time window | The interval in which the maneuver may have occurred. | Does not mean a precise time. |
| burn epoch | The estimated burn time of the maneuver. | It is an estimate with a timing error; it does not mean a fully validated, precise burn time; nor is it a reentry prediction. |

The three are at different levels and cannot substitute for one another; in interpretation, each should be explained according to the limitation column in the table.

The final report's R7 rules specify that when an event spans a TLE gap of 7 days or more, the net change can be measured but the maneuver time cannot be localized, and the evidence grade is capped at E2; with shorter gaps, too, it can only be established that the change falls within the gap. For example, SJ-26's event on 2026-11-09 falls within a 5.3-day gap.⁴

**Sidebar: The encounter time of Cosmos 2558 and USA 326 (2022)**

On August 1, 2022, Cosmos 2558 was launched from Plesetsk. Satellite observer Marco Langbroek calculated from public orbits that its right ascension of the ascending node (RAAN) differed from that of the U.S. satellite USA 326 by only 0.04 degrees, and predicted an encounter at about 14:47 UTC on August 4 at a distance of about 75 kilometers. A post-event analysis two days later revised this to 14:16:27 UTC and about 67 kilometers, of which about 64 kilometers was altitude difference. The two differed by about 30 minutes and 8 kilometers; and this was the encounter time, not a maneuver time. A time derived from TLEs should always be read together with its time window.

## Thrust vector inversion

Thrust vector inversion recovers the thrust vector of a maneuver from orbital changes. Thrust direction can be divided into the along-track direction (which accelerates or decelerates the satellite) and the cross-track direction (which changes the orbital plane); at TLE precision, the difficulty of recovering the two is entirely different. Case 15 checks each event against ΔV (velocity increment) ground truth officially certified by IDS/DORIS (IDS is the International DORIS Service; DORIS is the international Doppler satellite orbit determination system), showing the accuracy and limitations in different scenarios.¹

This book recommends that the output of thrust vector inversion be labeled with:

- The inversion method and version.
- The data time window and sampling conditions.
- The thrust vector components.
- Comparison with ΔV ground truth.
- Known failure scenarios.
- Recommended follow-up data for review.

With these fields, an analyst can reconstruct the reasoning process and see more than just a red flag.

**Sidebar: Dragon reboosts the International Space Station for the first time (2024)**

At 12:50 p.m. U.S. Eastern Time on November 8, 2024, a SpaceX cargo Dragon fired its Draco thrusters for about 12 minutes 30 seconds, demonstrating a reboost of the International Space Station for the first time. NASA's published results were an apogee increase of 0.07 miles and a perigee increase of 0.7 miles. The burn time was given to the minute and the altitude change had official figures, so announcements like this serve well as external references. Looked at with this book's method, one could compare the burn time and ΔV inverted from the TLEs before and after against the announcement to measure how large the inversion error is.

### Positioning of the case

The question of Case 15 is: "How accurately can the thrust vector of a maneuver be recovered from TLEs?" It checks each event against ΔV ground truth officially certified by IDS/DORIS, showing the accuracy and limitations in different scenarios. This ground truth comes from maneuver logs certified by the satellite operators, not computed by this project.²

The role of Case 15 in this chapter is that of a thrust vector inversion case, used to supplement the characterization of events after their timing has been localized; it is not a validation of the burn epoch method itself, nor a complete validation of the whole system. For validation of time localization, see the final report: compared against MEME, the median error of TLE-determined times is 2.74 hours; analyzing the thrust arc of STARLINK-37471 with minute-by-minute MEME, the start of the arc could be localized to about 1.5 hours, but this requires precise ephemerides and applies only to Starlink.⁴ The inversion's performance must still be interpreted together with the layers described above.

**Sidebar: MEV-1 docks with Intelsat 901 (2020)**

On February 25, 2020, MEV-1 docked with Intelsat 901 in a graveyard orbit about 300 kilometers above geosynchronous orbit. The public approach procedure has only a few hold points: first waiting at 80 meters for a ground command, then advancing autonomously to 20 meters, and finally completing capture at about 1 meter. How many thruster firings occurred between hold points, and when, is not stated in public materials. Without the operator's maneuver log, even if TLEs show that the orbit changed, there is no way to check the inverted burn times. The full account of this cooperative rendezvous is in Chapter 19.

### Impulsive chemical propulsion

In the along-track direction, recovery for impulsive chemical propulsion is nearly perfect: for CryoSat-2, which uses chemical propulsion with impulsive burns, the correlation coefficient between recovered values and ground truth is r=0.975. This means that for that data snapshot, that population, and those evaluation conditions, a comparable baseline has been established.²

If the scope is widened to the entire clean subset free of contamination from neighboring maneuvers (n=207), the correlation coefficient between recovered along-track ΔV and ground truth is r=0.942, with a median error of 2.4 mm/s; the CryoSat-2 r=0.975 above is the result computed separately for the n=166 events within it.²

This result cannot be generalized directly to all impulsive chemical propulsion. When the propulsion type, data conditions, or mission differ, revalidation may still be needed.

### Three failure scenarios

Case 15 records that three scenarios fail, as reported faithfully: the cross-track component, neighboring-maneuver contamination, and continuous electric-propulsion thrust. These scenarios have been checked against ground truth; the result is that the method does not apply in them, and the cause of each failure can be identified.²

**The cross-track component.** For the frozen Sun-synchronous orbits involved in the case, the true inclination changes are far below the inclination noise of TLEs themselves; it is physically impossible to recover such a small signal from TLEs. The problem is not the algorithm.

In Case 15's full sample (n=246), the correlation coefficient between the recovered cross-track component and ground truth is r≈0.00, with a median error as large as 39.1 mm/s, while the median true inclination change of these events is only 0.2 mm/s.²

**Neighboring-maneuver contamination.** When two consecutive burns are too close together, the two signals contaminate each other at TLE resolution and cannot be cleanly separated.

SWOT is an example: its events also belong to the clean subset (n=41), but 41% of them are less than 7 days apart, and the correlation coefficient of the recovered along-track ΔV is only r=0.512.²

**Continuous electric-propulsion thrust.** This method assumes that a maneuver is a single instantaneous impulse; the thrust of electric-propulsion satellites, however, is applied continuously, spread over dozens of orbits, with no single step to recover, and applying the single-step assumption systematically underestimates.²

On the detection side, the final report's R7 handles such changes as a "continuous maneuver segment": events that are adjacent in time with net Δa in the same direction are merged into one segment and interpreted by start and end, cumulative Δa, and average rate. For example, STARLINK-4314's 26-day descent totaling −83.8 kilometers was graded E3. But for climbs spread over many days with every step below the threshold, L1 covers only part (a median of about 40% in the Starlink V3 orbit-raising phase), and the rest must be picked up by low-confidence review items.⁴ This addresses "whether, and in which segment," not the time of a single burn.

These three scenarios should therefore be labeled as known failure boundaries: although the inversion method has been established, its results in these scenarios cannot be relied upon; the performance for impulsive chemical propulsion cannot be extrapolated to other propulsion types, and deployment to new satellites or propulsion types requires revalidation according to deployment conditions.

**Sidebar: The rescue burns of Galileo 5 and 6 (2014–2015)**

The two Galileo satellites delivered to the wrong orbit at the start of Chapter 13 were later partly rescued with their own thrusters. Satellite 5 performed 11 maneuvers over 17 days, gradually raising its perigee by more than 3,500 kilometers in total, and first broadcast navigation signals on November 29, 2014; satellite 6, starting in mid-January 2015, performed about 14 maneuvers over about six weeks. There was not enough fuel to return to the nominal orbit, only enough to make the orbit more circular. Looked at with this book's method, when the interval between adjacent burns is shorter than the TLE update interval, TLEs show only the cumulative effect, and the timing and ΔV of individual burns are blended together.

**Sidebar: ABS-3A's all-electric climb to geosynchronous orbit (2015)**

On March 2, 2015, a Falcon 9 launched two Boeing 702SP all-electric satellites, ABS-3A and Eutelsat 115 West B, together into a supersynchronous transfer orbit of about 63,000 × 400 kilometers, after which each climbed using xenon ion thrusters. ABS-3A completed in-orbit handover and entered service on August 31, about a month ahead of schedule. For nearly half a year from launch to service, the orbit changed slowly every day, and no single instant could be called "the burn time"; this is exactly the scenario where the single-impulse assumption does not fit.

### National security implications

The lesson of Case 15 is:

> **Good inversion results do not mean all propulsion types are covered.**²

In practice, results for impulsive and continuous thrust should be reported separately, each labeled with its own scope of applicability and limitations; failure results should be kept on record, inversion results cannot be used as ground truth, and revalidation is required when moving to new deployment conditions.

**Sidebar: SMART-1 spirals to the Moon on electric propulsion (2003–2004)**

The European Space Agency's SMART-1 was placed into geosynchronous transfer orbit by an Ariane 5 on September 27, 2003, and, using a Hall-effect thruster with a thrust of about 70 millinewtons, raised its orbit in a spiral, loop by loop, entering lunar orbit in mid-November 2004, a little more than 13 months from launch to lunar capture. Its orbital elements drifted slowly each day rather than jumping all at once. Looked at with this book's method, an approach centered on jump detection would very likely miss this kind of propulsion or treat the signal as noise; this is also why continuous thrust must be reported separately from impulsive thrust.

## Division of labor between maneuver timing detection and other layers

Maneuver timing detection does not replace the rule layer, nor is it subordinate to the statistical layer. It provides event localization; the rule layer provides interpretable candidate events and reasons for suppression; the statistical layer, physical residuals, and the fusion scorer serve as auxiliary channels that check whether there is also a response around an event, and they affect only the evidence grade.⁴

| Question | Component best used first |
|---|---|
| Does it exceed a known threshold? | L1 rule layer. |
| Does it deviate from the recent norm? | L2 statistical layer. |
| Do multiple channels jointly support it? | Auxiliary-channel comparison (L2, drag residual, ML window model, fusion scorer); affects only the evidence grade. |
| Can it be explained by drag or reentry? | Physics and applicability processing. |
| Are the data sufficient to judge? | Quality gate. |
| When did the maneuver happen? | Maneuver timing detection. |

This table is this book's summary of the engineering division of labor; the channels can be computed in parallel, but the decision order for formal reports has been fixed by the R7 workflow in the final report, and other systems may adjust it to their deployment conditions. Under the final report's R7 single-satellite report workflow, candidate events are determined only by the L1 rules; the other components affect only the evidence grade, or list periods not flagged by L1 as low-confidence review items for analysts to interpret.⁴
