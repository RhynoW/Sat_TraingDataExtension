## Chapter overview

Around April 5, 2010, Intelsat's Galaxy 15 stopped responding to ground commands. It had been located near 133 degrees west longitude (according to reports), and afterward drifted eastward along the geostationary belt. Engineers sent 150,000 to 200,000 commands, none of which managed to recover it.

The trouble was that it was not dead. Its communications payload kept operating, relaying whatever signal it received, and its antenna was still pointed at Earth. Ahead of it lay SES's AMC-11, which delivered digital cable television programming across the United States. SES repositioned satellites, and Intelsat used a 19-meter antenna in Clarksburg, Maryland, to focus energy on AMC-11; on June 1, Intelsat confirmed that Galaxy 15 had passed and that customer signals had not been interrupted.

The drift lasted more than eight months, passed more than ten C-band satellites, and required 15 fly-by avoidance operations in total, until in December its battery was depleted, its equipment reset, and it began accepting commands again. The failure review later concluded that electrostatic discharge had scrambled the onboard software, and ruled out solar activity.

From the outside, its orbital data were not strange: its position was predictable, it was transmitting signals, and its attitude was stable. When the data look normal, how is a system to know that this case should be handed to a human?

The purpose of routing and degradation is not to cram all data into the same model, but to decide which path the system should take, what level of judgment it should output, and when to hand over to human review, under specific data conditions, a specific orbital regime, and a specific mission. This chapter uses Case 24 as the main case, turning cross-case resolution limits into requirements for routing, down-weighting, human review, and data upgrades.¹

Taking this project's system as an example, the final report distinguishes two ways of combining methods. The app's "unified detection summary" first passes through the reentry gate and then branches by a fixed rule (catalog name beginning with "STARLINK"): Starlink is judged primarily by Model 1 and the fusion scorer, and non-Starlink primarily by the NRLMSIS drag residual, with Model 2 only as corroboration; it is only a reference view and does not determine candidate events. The formal R7 single-satellite report instead applies the same workflow to all satellites, with the L1 rules determining candidate events and the other methods affecting only the evidence grade. The reason is that formal reports must be reproducible item by item for any satellite, while the machine-learning models were trained only on Starlink and cannot be validated when used on other satellites.⁴

## Learning goals

- Understand the differences among routing, degradation, and out-of-scope scenarios.
- Understand the sources of cross-case resolution limits.

- Avoid treating routing rules as a cure-all.
- Avoid extrapolating degradation results to all scenarios.

## What routing and degradation ask

The question routing and degradation must answer is:

> **How should the system handle this scenario?**¹

Before answering, the basis of comparison must first be established. The same routing rule may mean entirely different things for different orbital regimes, data conditions, and missions.

This book recommends that routing and degradation distinguish at least three kinds of output:

| Output | Meaning | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Routing | Which path the system should use. | Does not mean all paths have been verified. |
| Degradation | The system should lower its confidence or defer. | Does not mean all risks have been ruled out. |
| Out of scope | The system should not use the model. | Does not mean all applicability has been ruled out. |

The three are at different levels and cannot substitute for one another; when interpreting them, each should be explained according to the limitation column in the table.

The final report's R7 has set out several specific degradation rules: events spanning a TLE gap of 7 days or more are capped at E2; events with a "rose then fell back" rebound pattern are graded D, and the original TLEs and catalog are checked first; single jumps of 50 kilometers or more are still detected, but a catalog check is prompted first; events of non-Starlink satellites that meet the E3 conditions only because of machine-learning assistance are regraded "E2 (ML-assisted)"; for non-Starlink satellites, fusion-scorer threshold crossings, all of which were false alarms in the ground-truth evaluation, are not listed as low-confidence review items; and TLEs flagged suspect by the data audit are down-weighted in the detection layer.⁴

**Sidebar: The battery breakups of DMSP F13 and NOAA-16 (2015)**

On February 3, 2015, DMSP F13, launched in 1995, experienced a power anomaly, began to tumble, and then broke up; a U.S. Air Force investigation concluded that the battery charger harness had failed, causing a short circuit and overcharging that ultimately ruptured the battery, and the tally came to 147 trackable objects (43 were initially announced at the end of February). On November 25 of the same year, the decommissioned NOAA-16 broke up in an 841 × 857 km orbit; by the time NASA published its report, 136 pieces had been cataloged, and a battery failure was judged to be the possible cause. Abrupt orbital changes of this kind are not maneuvers and should be judged out of scope and routed to a human; a similar example in geostationary orbit is Intelsat 33e in Chapter 9.

>
> **Key assumptions**: The core variable monitored by this project is the semi-major axis; the evidence in each row comes from different cases, different data, and different evaluation units, and cannot be stitched together into a single set of comparable numbers.
>
> **Limitations**: The strength of the conclusions depends on each originating case; the methods for the noise floor and drag residuals have been established, but full verification has not yet been completed; the threshold figures in the atmospheric-drag row come from synthetic injection (a simulation under fixed assumptions), not detection rates for actual maneuvers.
>
> **When not to use this method**: The maneuver amplitude is close to or below the noise floor for the current orbital altitude/drag state; the maneuver is drawn out and spread over several hours to several days; the maneuver direction falls in variables other than the semi-major axis that are below the L1 inclination and eccentricity thresholds (|Δi| 0.02°, |Δe| 0.001) (phase, small cross-track); the goal is to infer a physical quantity (such as atmospheric density) rather than to detect "whether it moved." When any of these situations applies, switch to precise ephemerides, or accept a substantial drop in recall.

### Case positioning

The question in Case 24 is: "How fine can TLEs actually resolve?" Using a cross-case summary table of physical resolution limits, it shows that this project ran into the same wall from four different angles.²

In this chapter, Case 24 is a routing and degradation case, not a complete validation of the whole system; its conclusions must still be interpreted together with the layers described above.

### Noise floor

Case 24 lists the noise floor as one of the cross-case resolution limits: the noise floor varies with orbital altitude and is a physical property of the data itself, which cannot be solved by algorithmic improvements. The noise-floor method has been established, but full verification has not yet been completed.²

Take Case 18 as an example: it used 8 passive geodetic spheres, which have no thrusters and do not actively maneuver, to scan TLE semi-major axis noise across orbital altitudes of 800–19,126 km; satellites of this kind are guaranteed to be quiet and are the cleanest control group for measuring the noise floor.²

It should therefore be clearly stated that the results should not be extrapolated to all altitudes, and that they must be re-verified against actual conditions at deployment.

### Atmospheric-drag state

Case 24 lists the atmospheric-drag state as one of the cross-case resolution limits: the current atmospheric-drag state dominates the detection threshold, which can be mitigated with adaptive thresholds but not eliminated. The drag-residual method has been established, but full verification has not yet been completed.²

The final report's L1 rules take exactly this approach: P2 sets the semi-major axis threshold by altitude (about 2.0 kilometers at 400 kilometers, 0.4 kilometers above 700 kilometers), and P5 raises it according to the day's F10.7 (×1.6 at 200 sfu, capped at ×1.8). The drag residual currently uses a fixed threshold of 0.3 kilometers, which produces too many false alarms below 400 kilometers and is overly conservative above 600 kilometers; in out-of-sample validation, none of the altitude-dependent schemes met the target at all altitudes, so for now this is only a directional recommendation.⁴

The evidence for this row comes from Case 17: against a background of real FORMOSAT-7/COSMIC-2 TLEs, 15,775 maneuver-injection trials were run, covering 478 satellite × window combinations. At the original update frequency, a 50 m injection already has a 99% detection rate in the low-drag state; in the high-drag state, 150–200 m is needed to reach about 90%. These are synthetic-injection results under fixed assumptions, not detection rates for actual maneuvers.²

It should therefore be clearly stated that the results should not be extrapolated to all orbits, and that they must be re-verified against actual conditions at deployment.

**Sidebar: The assisted reentry of Aeolus (2023)**

In July 2023, the European Space Agency's Aeolus did not have enough fuel for a fully controlled reentry, so its remaining fuel was used instead to carry out what ESA called the first "assisted reentry." It first decayed naturally to about 280 km, and from July 24 lowered its orbit in stages: from 280 to 250 km, then to 230 km; the final maneuver on July 28 was planned to lower it from 150 to 120 km, and it reentered over Antarctica that evening. Looking back with this book's method, this stretch of TLEs is natural decay interspersed with a series of active orbit-lowering burns, and distinguishing these two kinds of descent is exactly what the reentry gate must do. The final report's example is a Starlink performing disposal orbit lowering, whose perigee descended in steps; the reentry gate did not trigger, and the maneuvers were detected normally. Conversely, once the gate triggers, the maneuver count is output as 0 and active orbit lowering may be dropped along with it, so the gate's determination must be read together with the rule layer's candidate events.⁴

### Observational blind spots

Case 24 lists observational blind spots as one of the cross-case resolution limits. A blind spot here means that the signal is not in the monitored variable: the core variable monitored by this project is the semi-major axis.² According to the final report, the L1 of R7 additionally uses |Δi| > 0.02° and |Δe| > 0.001 as independent flags, so larger changes in inclination or eccentricity can still be caught; but for changes in along-track phase, and out-of-plane components below the inclination threshold, the semi-major axis and these two quantities barely move; the RAAN residual, in ablation and validation against FORMOSAT-7 ground truth, only increased false alarms and is not included in the determination.⁴ Such maneuvers are a structural blind spot, not a problem that adjusting thresholds can solve.

It should therefore be clearly stated that the data-quality and observation-window methods deal with data gaps and cannot cover maneuvers of this kind, which require monitoring different variables; the results should not be extrapolated to all observation conditions, and they must be re-verified against actual conditions at deployment.

### Inversion ceiling

Case 24 lists the inversion ceiling as one of the cross-case resolution limits. It comes from Case 10's attempt to infer atmospheric density from TLEs: that case has candidly acknowledged that it did not reach its goal, and even with precise ephemerides, the density estimates still have a scatter that cannot be circumvented; the ceiling comes from the inversion method itself, not only from the shortcomings of TLEs.²

It should therefore be clearly stated that this is a methodological ceiling that cannot be fully eliminated even by switching data sources; the results should not be extrapolated to other inversion problems, and they must be re-verified against actual conditions at deployment.

### National security implications

The lesson of Case 24 is:

> **The resolution limit of TLEs is not a single number but a set of limitations that must be handled separately.**²

In practice, the noise floor, atmospheric drag, observational blind spots, and the inversion ceiling should be handled separately, each with its scope of applicability and limitations indicated; unverified results should be retained and annotated, routing rules are not a cure-all, and re-verification is required when moving to new deployment conditions.

**Sidebar: The IRAS and GGSE-4 conjunction (2020)**

On January 29, 2020, IRAS and GGSE-4 passed each other at about 900 km above Pittsburgh, with a relative velocity of 14.7 km per second. According to media accounts, LeoLabs initially estimated the probability of collision at about 1/100; after an update on January 28 it fell to about 1/1000; on the 29th it was found that GGSE-4 had a stabilization boom about 18 m long of unknown orientation, which raised the combined hard-body radius from 5 m to 10 m, and the probability rose again to about 1/20. The two ultimately passed safely. For the same conjunction, the number flipped twice as the assumptions changed; outputs must be reported together with their assumptions.

## Designing routing and degradation

This book recommends that routing and degradation include:

- Routing conditions and priorities.
- Degradation conditions and confidence levels.
- Conditions for human review.
- Data upgrade requirements.
- Known failure scenarios.
- Version and regression test results.

With these fields, analysts can reconstruct the reasoning, rather than seeing only a red flag.

**Sidebar: Intensified observation after Envisat lost contact (2012)**

On April 8, 2012, the European Space Agency's Envisat suddenly lost contact. To determine its state, France's CNES turned its Pleiades satellite on April 15 to image Envisat from about 100 km away and check the pointing of its solar array; the TIRA imaging radar of Germany's Fraunhofer FHR imaged the main body, the solar array, and the radar antenna; and several laser ranging stations confirmed that the orbit was stable. On May 9, ESA announced the end of the mission, listing only three presumed scenarios. Orbital data can only answer "where it is"; to answer "what happened to it," the data source must be upgraded.

**Sidebar: The geostationary anomalies of AMC-9 and Telkom-1 (2017)**

On June 17, 2017, SES lost contact with AMC-9, and the satellite then drifted steadily westward; ExoAnalytic's telescopes saw several larger fragments separate from the main body on June 25. On August 25, the main antenna pointing of PT Telkom Indonesia's Telkom-1 malfunctioned, and five days later the satellite was declared beyond recovery; ExoAnalytic likewise observed several large fragments. The causes of neither event have been determined. When operators lost telemetry, independent commercial optical observation became the eyes that filled the gap; the TLE update frequency in geostationary orbit could not keep pace with the shedding of fragments.

## Division of labor between routing and degradation and other layers

Routing and degradation do not replace the rule layer, nor are they subordinate to the statistical layer. They are responsible for handling out-of-scope scenarios; the rule layer provides interpretable candidate events and reasons for suppression; the statistical layer, physical residuals, and the fusion scorer serve as auxiliary channels that check whether there is also a response around an event, and they affect only the evidence grade.⁴

| Question | Component best suited to use first |
|---|---|
| Does it exceed a known threshold? | L1 rule layer. |
| Does it deviate from recent normal behavior? | L2 statistical layer. |
| Do multiple channels jointly support it? | Auxiliary-channel comparison (L2, drag residual, ML window model, fusion scorer); affects only the evidence grade. |
| Can it be explained by drag or reentry? | Physics and applicability handling. |
| Are the data sufficient for a judgment? | Quality gate. |
| How should this scenario be handled? | Routing and degradation. |

This table is this book's summary of the engineering division of labor; the channels can be computed in parallel, but the decision order for formal reports has been fixed by the R7 workflow in the final report, and other systems may adjust it to their deployment conditions. Under the final report's R7 single-satellite report workflow, candidate events are determined only by the L1 rules; the other components affect only the evidence grade, or list periods not flagged by L1 as low-confidence review items for analysts to interpret.⁴
