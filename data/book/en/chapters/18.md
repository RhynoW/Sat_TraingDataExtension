# Chapter 18　Field Validation: FORMOSAT, Starlink, and Non-Cooperative Targets

## Chapter overview

On June 25, 1997, Mir commander Tsibliyev, inside the core module, used the TORU remote control system to manually guide the Progress M-34 cargo ship to a redocking. According to reports, the test was intended to assess whether the Kurs automated docking system could be dispensed with in order to reduce mission costs.

Progress "came in too fast and could not stop." It first struck the solar array of the Spektr module, then the module itself; the hull was breached and began to lose pressure. After Spektr was isolated, the station's pressure dropped from about 750 mmHg to 675 mmHg before recovering and stabilizing at 692 mmHg. The impact set the entire station rotating at about 1 degree per second; a power loss then disabled the gyroscopes, the station lost the ability to sense its own attitude, and for a time the rotation was out of control.

Foale, aboard the station, estimated the rotation rate by watching the stars, and the ground then used engines to stop the rotation.

When your own instruments cannot tell you what is wrong with you, judgment can rely only on reference points outside the station. When validating a detection system, where are the "stars" we have at hand?

Running scores only on one's own data is not enough to show that a system is trustworthy. Field validation must answer whether the system can maintain trustworthy performance in a fair contest, against external ground truth, and under different mission conditions. Case 14 compares this project with a research institution's existing method in the same contest. The case collection uses the curve method as reproduced by this project as the comparator and shows how sample size can change the conclusion; the final report instead uses the results of the curve method as run by TASA itself, and both the 14 development satellites and the 9 parameter-frozen satellites show this system performing better, although the conclusion depends on the hit-tolerance setting.¹,⁴

## Learning goals

- Understand the difference between field validation and internal testing.
- Understand the design and limitations of external benchmark comparisons.
- Use Case 14 to learn how sample size can change the conclusion.
- Use FORMOSAT and non-cooperative targets to learn about different mission conditions.
- Avoid extrapolating a single comparison result to all missions.

## What field validation asks

The question field validation must answer is:

> **Is this system still accurate in a fair contest and against external ground truth?**¹

Before answering, the basis of comparison must first be established. The same comparison result may mean entirely different things under different missions, data conditions, and sample sizes. This book recommends that field validation distinguish at least three kinds of output:

| Output | Meaning | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| External benchmark comparison | Comparison with existing methods. | Does not mean all methods have been verified. |
| FORMOSAT validation | Validation on a specific mission. | Does not mean all missions have been verified. |
| Non-cooperative target validation | Validation under non-cooperative conditions. | Does not mean all non-cooperative conditions have been verified. |

Each of the three has its own scope; the results cannot substitute for one another, nor can one of them be used to infer that the other two have been completed.

**Sidebar: The U.S. Space Force's SACT exercises**

Since 2019, the U.S. Joint Task Force-Space Defense (JTF-SD) has held SACT exercises three times a year, providing an entry point for commercial operators to take part in space domain awareness (SDA) operations; the operating model of its commercial operations cell, the JCO, came from the 2020 SACT series. According to a summary in a U.S. Space Force news release, the first SACT of 2021 validated the rapid sharing of unclassified products with Australia, Canada, the United Kingdom, and France. The technical details and outcomes of the exercises have not been made public; what can be said with certainty is that external data must first be tested under near-operational conditions before entering operations.

## External benchmark comparison

This chapter uses Case 14 as an example of an external benchmark comparison: this project and a research institution's existing method are compared in the same contest, and from this one can see how sample size changes the conclusion.¹

This book recommends that external benchmark comparisons indicate:

- The comparison methods and versions.
- The data snapshot and time window.
- The sample size and evaluation unit.
- The metrics and thresholds.
- Known failure scenarios.
- Recommended data for follow-up review.

With these fields, analysts can reconstruct the reasoning, rather than seeing only a red flag.

**Sidebar: The International Space Station avoids DMSP F13 debris in 2024**

On November 19, 2024, the International Space Station raised its orbit with a 5-minute 31-second burn of Progress 89's thrusters to avoid a piece of debris; NASA estimated that without the maneuver, the closest approach might have been about 4 km. NASA's *Orbital Debris Quarterly News* later identified the debris as coming from the DMSP 5D-2 F13 weather satellite, which exploded accidentally in 2015. Maneuvers of this kind, whose date, duration, and direction are published by the operator, are ready-made ground truth for testing whether a TLE detector can "see" a maneuver; however, the burn times recorded in the NASA blog and the quarterly do not match, so citations should specify only the date.

## Main case: Case 14

> **Evidence level: A | Independently recomputed**
>
> **Data sources**: 14 development altimetry satellites, expanded to 23 (adding 9 hold-out satellites that never took part in tuning: SPOT-2/3/4/5, Sentinel-6B, and the GRACE series), covering 460–1,340 km and inclinations of 66–99°; the figures are read from frozen per-satellite result files produced by this project's existing batch runs, plus a time slice frozen on 2025-05-01, the same snapshot as the TASA briefing.
>
> **Ground truth**: Operator-certified maneuver records (see the three sources in Case 4, Chapter 17); the maneuver counts of the 14 satellites in the 2025-05-01 slice were checked satellite by satellite against the TASA briefing, and 14/14 matched.
>
> **Key assumptions**: A hit is defined as a detection time falling within "the true maneuver window extended by ±1.5 days," because TLEs typically have only 0.5–2 records per day; this project's method uses zero per-satellite tuning, receiving the same treatment as the global version of the curve method.
>
> **Limitations**: In the case collection, the "curve method" is a version reproduced by this project from the methodology in the external literature, not the original authors' code; under the case-collection conditions (against the reproduced curve method), the result is 9 wins and 5 losses for 14 satellites (p = 0.81) and 17 wins and 6 losses for 23 satellites (p = 0.006). The final report (Section 7.6) instead uses the results of the curve method as run by TASA itself as the comparator: for 14 satellites with a ±1.5-day tolerance, this system's mean F1 is 0.496 versus 0.330 for TASA's best global configuration, with 11 wins and 3 losses per satellite (p＝0.020); when the tolerance is changed to ±1.0 day, the two are comparable. For the 9 parameter-frozen satellites never involved in development, this system's per-satellite F1 is higher than the best global configuration of the curve method in every case (sign test p≈0.0039). For the 9 extension satellites, the rule-based channel was computed with only one method, iter2. The TASA briefing figures were transcribed verbatim and not recomputed by this project, and are at the level of a primary source; the case collection also records that in the 2025-05-01 slice, the mean F1 of the best configuration of TASA's curve method (0.52) was slightly higher than this project's (0.423); the final report does not include this set of figures.
>
> **When not to use this method**: Targets without operator maneuver ground truth (such as non-cooperative targets) cannot be scored in this contest; missions outside the above altitude and inclination ranges, or with different data conditions, should not directly carry over the 23-satellite conclusion. For performance on FORMOSAT-7, see the leoOrb precise ground-truth validation in Section 7.4 of the final report; the F1 of 0.07–0.24 for 4 TLE methods on FORMOSAT-7 recorded in Case 24 of the case collection still lacks a source.

**Data specification**: Nature Measured; Period start and end not stated in the case file (the main comparison uses the latest data; a separate set of ground truth and TLEs is frozen at 2025-05-01); TLE count not stated in the case file (the evaluation unit is the maneuver event; per-satellite event counts for 14 and 23 satellites are in the case file); Threshold a hit is defined as a detection time falling within the true maneuver window extended by ±1.5 days; this method (iterative + level shift) uses a global k=6 with zero per-satellite tuning; False-alarm rate the case collection records that in the 2025-05-01 slice, this project's mean Precision is 0.454 (best configuration of TASA's curve method 0.80, transcribed verbatim from its briefing), and the final report does not include this set of figures; the final report (Section 7.6) separately measured, over the operator-certified quiet periods of 14 precise-orbit satellites (1,138 quiet windows, 104,338 transitions), a per-transition false-alarm rate of 0.00089, or 2.03 per 1,000 satellite-days.

### Case positioning

The core question of Case 14 is "this project vs. a research institution's existing method, head to head in the same contest," that is, a direct comparison on the same data, against the same ground truth, and under the same treatment. According to the case collection, with the curve method as reproduced by this project as the comparator, the two were statistically tied on the original 14-satellite benchmark, and only after expanding to 23 satellites, 9 of which are true hold-out satellites (never involved in any tuning), did this project win significantly.² The final report instead uses the results of the curve method as run by TASA itself as the comparator: on 14 satellites this project already has 11 wins and 3 losses (p＝0.020), and the sign test for the 9 parameter-frozen satellites gives p≈0.0039.⁴ Taken together, the two comparisons show that sample size, the comparator, and the tolerance setting can all change the conclusion.

The comparison covers orbital altitudes of 460–1,340 km and inclinations of 66–99°. A hit is defined as a detection time falling within the true maneuver window extended by ±1.5 days, because TLEs typically have only 0.5–2 records per day, and requiring detections to fall within the original maneuver window would be almost impossible to achieve.²

Case 14 is a field validation case and does not represent a complete validation of the whole system.

According to Case 14's description, the "curve method" it is compared against is a version reproduced by this project from the methodology in the external literature, not the original authors' source code; the figures on the page are read from frozen result files produced by existing batch runs, and detection is not rerun every time the page is opened. Case 14 also used data frozen on 2025-05-01 to compare directly against the original figures in the TASA briefing; in that time slice, the mean F1 of the best configuration of TASA's curve method was slightly higher than this project's. Case 14 explains that this and the comparison in this section are independent comparisons with different time slices, different ground-truth sets, and different baselines, and the two do not contradict each other.

In the 2025-05-01 slice, the mean F1 of the best configuration of TASA's curve method was 0.52, and this project's was 0.423; TASA's figures were transcribed verbatim from its briefing and were not recomputed by this project. These are case-collection figures; the final report (Section 7.6) uses the results of the curve method as run by TASA itself on 14 satellites (0.330, versus 0.496 for this system). The two comparisons differ in time slice and comparator and should not be mixed.⁴

### The original 14-satellite benchmark

According to the case collection, the result of Case 14's comparison on the original 14-satellite benchmark against the curve method as reproduced by this project is a statistical tie. This indicates that, for that data snapshot, that population, and those evaluation conditions, the system has established a comparable baseline.²

Under the case-collection conditions, comparing this project with the global version of the curve method using the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, this project had 9 wins and 5 losses among the 14 satellites, p = 0.81.² Compared with the results of the curve method as run by TASA itself, the final report gives 11 wins and 3 losses among the 14 satellites (p＝0.020); this conclusion depends on the ±1.5-day tolerance setting, and at ±1.0 day the two are comparable.⁴

This result does not mean it applies to all missions. When the mission, data conditions, or sample size change, re-verification may still be needed.

### 23 satellites and the 9 hold-out satellites

According to the case collection, only after expanding to 23 satellites and including 9 true hold-out satellites did this project win significantly; the same test on 23 satellites gives 17 wins and 6 losses, p = 0.006.²

The final report also tests the 9 parameter-frozen satellites never involved in development separately, and this system's per-satellite F1 is higher than the best global configuration of the curve method in every case (sign test p≈0.0039).⁴ This indicates that the system has established a comparison method, but full verification has not yet been completed.

### How sample size changes the conclusion

Under the case collection's comparison against the reproduced curve method, a tie with 14 satellites and a win with 23 can be seen as an example of sample size changing the conclusion.² But when the final report uses the curve method as run by TASA itself as the comparator, 14 satellites already give 11 wins and 3 losses, and the conclusion changes with the hit tolerance (at ±1.0 day the two are comparable).⁴ The comparator and the tolerance setting can thus change the conclusion just as sample size can; all of these results show only that the comparison method has been established, not that full verification has been completed.

This book recommends that the results for 23 satellites and the 9 hold-out satellites, as well as the effect of sample size, all be clearly labeled as: comparison method established, full verification not yet completed, not to be extrapolated to all missions, and to be re-verified according to deployment conditions.

**Sidebar: Using DORIS maneuver records as ground truth**

There is precedent for validating TLE maneuver detection with operator maneuver records. At the 2026 International DORIS Service (IDS) workshop, a project by University College London and the UK Space Agency detected maneuvers from public TLEs and then compared them with IDS maneuver records to compute precision, recall, and detection latency, using Sentinel-3A as the case. Shorten et al. (2022) built a benchmark dataset containing maneuver-time ground truth for 15 satellites, and pointed out that the ground truth in many studies comes from subjective interpretation. Ground truth of this kind exists only for a small number of cooperative satellites, and Case 14's sample is limited by this as well.

### National security implications

The core lesson of Case 14 is:

> **Sample size, the comparator, and the tolerance setting can all change the conclusion; one cannot look at a single comparison alone.**²,⁴

The results for 14 satellites, 23 satellites, and the hold-out satellites should therefore be presented separately, with the data snapshot, time window, comparator, and hit tolerance indicated. Unverified results should be retained, and comparison results must not be treated as ground truth; when deployment conditions change, re-verification is also required.

## FORMOSAT, Starlink, and non-cooperative targets

The final report (Section 8.3) analyzes the FORMOSAT series using public TLEs from 2024-01 to 2026-07 (16 targets, 30,584 records), without adjusting any parameters for FORMOSAT. The 6 FORMOSAT-3 satellites show natural decay, with drag residuals close to 0 and no false maneuver alarms; over two and a half years, 64 maneuver events (|Δa| > 1 km) were detected for FORMOSAT-7, of which 61 (95%) had a synchronized maneuver by another FORMOSAT-7 satellite within 5 days before or after. All of these interpretations use only public TLEs, and the Taiwan Space Agency (TASA) can check them against telemetry and precise orbit determination data.⁴

For the interpretation of FORMOSAT-7 FS7-3 ceasing station-keeping, see Chapter 4; for the TLE gaps and misjudgments for FORMOSAT-7/COSMIC-2 satellite 1, see Chapter 6.

According to the compilation in Case 24, in Case 14's validation on FORMOSAT-7, the F1 of 4 TLE methods is only 0.07–0.24 (a case-collection figure; source to be added).³ The final report (Section 7.4) separately validates R7's L1 rules using as ground truth the 784 maneuvers detected in FORMOSAT-7 leoOrb precise orbit determination (2024-01 to 2026-11, 1,047 days): of the 56 ground-truth maneuvers at 20σ or above, 29 were covered; among the 55 L1 events, 59% of the 22 E3 events, 25% of the 28 E2 events, and 0% of the 5 E1 events corresponded to ground truth, so the grade ordering is consistent with the proportion of true maneuvers. However, 35 events had no corresponding ground truth; they may be L1 false alarms, or the ground truth's consistency check may have been too strict and missed them, and the two cannot yet be distinguished.⁴ This also shows that the 23-satellite conclusion cannot be carried over directly to FORMOSAT-7.

On Starlink, the final report (Section 8.1) takes as an example the +50.3 km orbit raising of STARLINK-37471 (NORAD 68802) on 2026-06-21, confirmed by MEME, for which the L1 rules had already accumulated flags about one day before the ground-truth time. Chapter 9 analyzes the first batch of 26 Starlink V3 satellites with the R7 workflow: of 74 candidate events, all 39 E3 events were established by the rules, independently of machine learning; L1 coverage of the orbit-insertion climb phase was about 40%, and the uncovered portions were added as L2 low-confidence review items. The inclination and insertion altitude of V3 both lie outside the training range of the machine-learning models.⁴

On non-cooperative targets, the final report (Section 8.6) conducted an external delivery test on 15 satellites designated by the Taiwan Space Agency (TASA) (8 U.S., 7 Chinese): 131 rule candidate points were merged into 23 candidate events, of which 5 were E3 (all established by the rules), 12 were E2 (9 of them "E2 (ML-assisted)" for non-Starlink satellites), 4 were E1, and 2 were D, and 8 satellites had no candidate event above the 5σ limit over the entire period. The 9 E2 (ML-assisted) events were checked one by one afterward, and none could be either confirmed or refuted. Candidate events do not equal confirmed maneuvers. For the reconstruction of the Shenlong spaceplane's relative motion, see Section 8.4 of the final report; it is a TLE/SGP4 reconstruction and is not sufficient on its own to prove controlled rendezvous and proximity operations.⁴

This book recommends that the validation of FORMOSAT and non-cooperative targets be clearly labeled with the data sources on which the validation is based, that it should not be extrapolated to all missions, and that it should be re-verified according to deployment conditions.

**Sidebar: Lasers measure Envisat's spin in 2013**

After Envisat lost contact in 2012 it no longer cooperated, but the laser retroreflectors it carried for cooperative ranging were still there. Kucharski et al. used seven months of International Laser Ranging Service (ILRS) observations from 2013 to infer its spin from meter-scale ranging oscillations within a single pass: the spin period on September 25, 2013, was 134.74 seconds. External ground truth does not necessarily require the operator's cooperation; the same was true of Hitomi in Chapter 10: when the operator's telemetry could not explain what had happened, it was external space surveillance units, radars, and telescopes that first concluded the satellite had broken up.

## Division of labor between field validation and other layers

Field validation does not replace the rule layer, nor is it subordinate to the statistical layer. It provides external comparison. Under the final report's R7 workflow, candidate events are generated only by the L1 rules, with explainable trigger and suppression reasons; L2 statistical change points, the fusion scorer, the ML window model, and the drag residual serve as auxiliary channels that affect only the evidence grade and neither add nor remove events; physics and applicability handling provide additional plausibility checks.⁴

| Question | Component best suited to use first |
|---|---|
| Does it exceed a known threshold? | L1 rule layer. |
| Does it deviate from recent normal behavior? | L2 statistical layer. |
| Do multiple channels jointly support it? | Auxiliary-channel cross-check (ML window model, fusion scorer, drag residual, L2 statistical change points; under the final report's R7 workflow, these affect only the evidence grade). |
| Can it be explained by drag or reentry? | Physics and applicability handling. |
| Are the data sufficient for a judgment? | Quality gate. |
| Is it still accurate in a fair contest and against external ground truth? | Field validation. |

This table is this book's summary of the engineering division of labor; the channels can be computed in parallel, but the decision order for the formal report has been fixed by the final report's R7 workflow, and other systems may adjust according to their deployment conditions.
