## Chapter overview

At 15:02 (UTC) on February 10, 2009, CelesTrak's conjunction screening service SOCRATES published its report as usual. One entry was Iridium 33 and the Russian satellite Cosmos 2251, with a predicted minimum distance of 584 meters. It was not the closest conjunction in that report, nor even the closest among all Iridium satellites that week.

All 14 reports in the week before the collision listed this conjunction, yet it never once made the top ten. The predicted distance jumped between 117 meters and 1.8 kilometers; at the time of the collision, it ranked 152nd overall. T.S. Kelso, who analyzed the event, attributed the jumps in distance to the uncertainty inherent in the TLEs themselves.

At the predicted time of closest approach, 16:56 (UTC), Iridium 33 suddenly went silent. By 2012, about 2,200 pieces of debris from the two satellites had been cataloged. That week, the Iridium constellation as a whole had more than a thousand predicted conjunctions within 5 kilometers. The one that would actually collide was on the list—it just ranked 152nd. When the distance used for ranking is itself unreliable, which number can we still trust?

Not every orbital anomaly is a threat. What national-security decisions must answer is: under specific evidentiary conditions, mission context, and decision needs, how should an event be understood and handled? This chapter uses Case 7, Case 28, and Case 31 to establish three things in turn: approach is not threat; the limitations of covariance and Pc; and the chain of evidence for non-cooperative approach events.¹

## What national-security decisions are asking

The question national-security decisions must answer is:

> **How should this event be understood and handled?**¹

Before answering, the baseline of comparison must be confirmed. The same distance or Pc can mean something entirely different under a different mission, data condition, and decision need. This book recommends distinguishing at least three kinds of output for national-security decisions:

| Output | Meaning | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Approach event | Geometric distance is close. | Does not mean a threat has been confirmed. |
| Collision risk | Possibility of collision. | Does not mean a threat has been confirmed. |
| National-security judgment | Handling after synthesizing the evidence. | Does not mean all risks have been eliminated. |

Neither an approach event nor a collision risk is yet a threat judgment; even when a national-security judgment has been made, it does not mean all risks have been eliminated. The three must not be conflated.

The final report (Section 5.1) positions the evidence grades output by the system (E3, E2, E1, D) as alert levels used to set the order of review, in the same spirit as collision warning, which uses Pc as a reference for judgment: a higher grade means the item should be handled first, not that a maneuver has been confirmed. The recommended handling for E3 is immediate review; only after human confirmation is it assessed whether to update collision-risk products.⁶

## TCA and Pc

The question Case 7 addresses is: how close must two satellites be to count as a "dangerous approach"? How are Pc and TCA computed? TCA is the Time of Closest Approach, the moment when the predicted trajectories of two satellites are closest; Pc is the Probability of Collision, computed by combining the minimum distance with the orbital uncertainties of both objects, with a value between 0 and 1.¹

According to Case 28, the Pc this project used in Case 7 is a simplified demonstration algorithm (two-dimensional, with assumed covariance), not a formal conjunction assessment product, and it should not be used as a basis for collision avoidance maneuvers.

This book recommends that TCA and Pc be labeled with:

- The computation method and version.
- The data snapshot and time window.
- Covariance and uncertainty.
- Known failure scenarios.
- Recommended follow-up data for review.

With these fields, analysts can reconstruct the judgment process instead of seeing only a red flag.

### TJS-10 versus TJS-3

In the GEO close-in reconnaissance of TJS-10 against TJS-3, the minimum distance was 15.8 km.²

TJS-10 belongs to China's "Communication Technology Experiment Satellite" (TJS) series, whose missions are not disclosed; according to Case 7, this event is often classified as "co-location inspection / close-in reconnaissance" behavior. The value of this case lies in showing the difference between a non-cooperative close approach and a planned docking; it does not mean a threat has been confirmed, nor that all risks have been eliminated.

**Sidebar: America's own GEO inspection satellites**

In February 2014, General William Shelton, commander of Air Force Space Command, publicly revealed for the first time the previously classified Geosynchronous Space Situational Awareness Program (GSSAP), calling it a "neighborhood watch" for satellites: the satellites drift just above and just below the GEO belt and observe targets with electro-optical sensors. The first two were launched on July 28 of that year and reached initial operational capability on September 29, 2015. Drifting above and below the GEO belt so that targets sweep through the field of view in turn is the standard technique for close-in inspection; the same orbital behavior, carried out by different countries, is often given different interpretations.

**Sidebar: SJ-21 tows away Beidou G2**

On January 22, 2022, SJ-21—officially described as testing debris mitigation technology—after close-proximity operations with the defunct Beidou G2, disappeared for several hours from the telescopes of the commercial optical company ExoAnalytic: docking took place during daytime, when the telescopes could not image. It then maneuvered substantially, pulling G2 out of the GEO belt; by January 26 the two had separated, with G2 left in an eccentric orbit about 290 × 3,100 km above GEO. The capture mechanism has not been disclosed, and Chinese officials have not confirmed it. SJ-17, which roamed GEO a few years earlier, mainly approached China's own satellites (see Chapter 9).

### ISS versus Cygnus

In the normal docking of the ISS with the Cygnus cargo spacecraft, the minimum distance was about 0 km.² This project's automated Pc screening pipeline computed a minimum distance of 3.8 m for this approach event (time of closest approach 2026-05-13 14:42 UTC).²

This project's automated Pc screening pipeline computed Pc = 0.0005 and risk level MEDIUM for this docking. Looking at these numbers alone, it is almost indistinguishable from a truly dangerous close approach, yet it was a planned docking in which both sides knowingly cooperated.²

This is a cooperative docking, in contrast to a non-cooperative close approach. Likewise, it does not mean a threat has been confirmed, nor that all risks have been eliminated.

**Sidebar: Aolong-1 and cooperative docking**

On June 25, 2016, Aolong-1 lifted off from Wenchang on the maiden flight of Long March 7, with the stated mission of capturing simulated space debris with a robotic arm and sending it into a reentry orbit; it reentered the atmosphere on August 27, after about two months in orbit. ## Main case 2: Case 28

> **Evidence level: B | Official or primary source**
>
> **Data sources**: Conclusions follow NASA CARA recommendations and related NASA, AMOS, and Space-Track documents; the Pc demonstration is computed in real time by this project using numerical integration in polar coordinates (not Monte Carlo; the same input always gives the same output).
>
> **Ground truth**: None; official recommendations serve as the basis, and this project has no VCM access.
>
> **Key assumptions**: The Pc demonstration places the combined position uncertainty of the two objects on the encounter plane and integrates over a circle of radius HBR; Figure 19-3 fixes the minimum distance at 300 m and the hard-body radius at 20 m.
>
> **Limitations**: The Pc curve and scenarios such as "fresh TLE / TLE from several days ago" are hypothetical values (simulations under fixed assumptions), and TLEs do not actually provide covariance; Breaking Defense's reporting on the High Accuracy Catalog (HAC) and VCMs is media reporting that this project cannot independently verify and is not used as a basis for technical claims.
>
> **When not to use this method**: With only public TLEs and no covariance, one should not proceed to formal risk assessment (narrow-phase), nor decide on collision avoidance on that basis; a larger conservative σ should not be used in place of covariance, because an overly large σ enters the probability dilution region, making a dangerous conjunction look harmless.

**Data specification**: Nature simulation (numerical Pc computation under fixed assumptions); Period not applicable (simulation under fixed assumptions, not observational data); TLE count not applicable (Pc computed in real time by numerical integration in polar coordinates, not Monte Carlo; this project has no VCM access); Threshold no fixed threshold of this project (the case file lists reference operational thresholds, set by each mission and not a single standard: Pc ≥ 1e-3, most missions require a collision avoidance maneuver; 1e-4 ≤ Pc < 1e-3, further assessment and intensified observation are needed); False-alarm rate not evaluated (no external ground truth).

### Case positioning

The core question of Case 28 is "How exactly do VCMs and traditional TLEs differ in satellite collision prediction?" Its answer is: a TLE describes only the nominal orbit and has no covariance, so it cannot yield a credible probability of collision.³

A VCM (Vector Covariance Message) is the position and velocity state vector at a given moment plus covariance; covariance describes how uncertain this estimate is in each direction and the correlation between position and velocity errors. Case 28 is a case on covariance and Pc limitations; it does not represent complete validation of the entire system.

### VCMs and traditional TLEs

VCMs and traditional TLEs differ markedly in satellite collision prediction. According to Case 28, this project uses only public TLEs and has no VCM access; it currently has a simplified Pc demonstration computation but lacks covariance data, so on collision issues it can only perform wide-area preliminary screening (broad-phase) and situational display, and full validation has not yet been completed.³

**Sidebar: An alert with only 14 hours to go**

At 08:08 (U.S. Eastern Daylight Time) on June 28, 2011, a piece of debris of unknown identity passed the International Space Station, with NASA's best-estimate minimum distance at 335 meters. The flight control team received the "red threshold" conjunction notification only at about 18:00 the previous evening, about 14 hours before the time of closest approach—too late to plan a collision avoidance maneuver. The 6 crew members took shelter in the two Soyuz spacecraft and returned to the station afterward. NASA explained that the object had high atmospheric drag, making its trajectory very sensitive to errors in atmospheric density forecasts. When uncertainty converges too late, there is not enough warning time.

**Sidebar: Pegaso grazes a debris cloud**

At about 05:38 (UTC) on May 23, 2013, the Ecuadorian CubeSat Pegaso, over the Indian Ocean, grazed the debris cloud around a 1985 Soviet rocket remnant (called S14 in AFP reporting). Ecuador's space agency EXA said it was not a direct hit but a glancing collision with small debris; Pegaso immediately began spinning violently and lost communication, and on August 28 EXA decided to end efforts to reestablish contact; a later EXA conference paper also stated that some signals were recovered again in January 2014. Conjunction predictions are computed only between cataloged objects, and so are the notifications Space-Track has issued since 2009 through the SSA Sharing Program (see Chapter 1); small uncataloged debris next to large objects is not in any Pc.

This book recommends that both the comparison of VCMs and TLEs and the use of covariance and Pc be clearly labeled: only a simplified Pc demonstration computation exists at present, full validation has not been completed, results should not be extrapolated to all collision predictions, and they should be revalidated according to deployment conditions.

**Sidebar: The Cosmos 1408 debris cloud**

On November 15, 2021, Russia destroyed the defunct Cosmos 1408 with a direct-ascent anti-satellite missile. U.S. Space Command initially reported more than 1,500 pieces of trackable debris; according to reports, the station's 7 crew members took shelter in Crew Dragon and Soyuz for about two hours. The Russian side claimed the debris posed no threat; NASA later said the risk of debris penetrating the station had roughly doubled. ### National-security implications

The core lesson of Case 28 is:

> **Without covariance, Pc is not a credible probability of collision.**³

Therefore, the nominal orbit and covariance must be viewed separately; when presenting Pc, the uncertainty and the covariance assumptions used should be labeled at the same time. Unverified results should be retained, and Pc must not be treated as ground truth; when deployment conditions change, revalidation is also needed.

### Non-cooperative approach events

Case 31 reconstructs a close-range conjunction with public TLEs, which means the system has established a method for analyzing non-cooperative approach events, but full validation has not yet been completed.⁴

According to Case 31, positions computed from public TLEs with SGP4 have errors on the order of kilometers. The minimum distance reconstructed by this project is below the roughly 1 km accuracy of public TLEs; it is an SGP4 reconstruction rather than a measurement and can only be interpreted as an order of magnitude, not used to corroborate external reports to the hundred-meter level. Public TLEs can reconstruct the sequence and magnitude of maneuvers, but cannot reveal sub-kilometer distances or exact burn times, nor can they determine intent.

As for external reports, LeoLabs and independent trackers reported a closest approach of about 500 m between the two satellites; the 0.16 km reconstructed by this project is of the same order of magnitude, but the two should not be used to corroborate each other to the hundred-meter level.⁴

**Sidebar: Shenlong's Release and Recapture (2022–2026)**

**Sidebar: The 6 kilometers computed by amateurs**

The orbital elements of USA 276, a classified U.S. satellite launched on May 1, 2017, have never been published. Dutch amateur trackers observed it and determined its orbit on their own; Marco Langbroek later analyzed that it approached the International Space Station at 14:01:52 (UT) on June 3 at a distance of about 6.4 km, with an error of ± 2 km. Langbroek personally leaned toward the view that the approach was deliberate, but acknowledged it was unconfirmed; the NRO gave no explanation. Amateur observation can fill gaps in the public catalog, but an accuracy of ± 2 km is not on the same level as official data, so interpretations of intent must be conservative.

## Division of labor between national-security decisions and other layers

National-security decisions do not replace the rule layer, nor are they subordinate to the statistical layer. They provide handling judgments. Under the final report's R7 workflow, candidate events are generated only by the L1 rules, with explainable trigger and suppression reasons; L2 statistical change points, the fusion scorer, the ML window model, and the drag residual serve as auxiliary channels that affect only the evidence grade and neither add nor remove events; physics and applicability handling provide additional plausibility checks.⁶

| Question | Component best used first |
|---|---|
| Does it exceed a known threshold? | L1 rule layer. |
| Does it deviate from recent normal? | L2 statistical layer. |
| Do multiple channels jointly support it? | Auxiliary-channel cross-check (ML window model, fusion scorer, drag residual, L2 statistical change points; under the final report's R7 workflow, these affect only the evidence grade). |
| Can it be explained by drag or reentry? | Physics and applicability handling. |
| Are the data sufficient for a judgment? | Quality gate. |
| How should this event be understood and handled? | National-security decisions. |

This table is this book's summary of the engineering division of labor; the channels can be computed in parallel, but the decision order for the formal report has been fixed by the final report's R7 workflow, and other systems may adjust according to their deployment conditions.

- [ ] Can explain why, without covariance, Pc is not a credible probability of collision.
- [ ] Can explain why non-cooperative approach events require an auditable chain of evidence, with external reports kept separate from this project's interpretation.
- [ ] Can explain that national-security judgments must be interpreted together with data quality, rule suppression, the statistical layer, physical residuals, and fusion decisions, and that no single distance or Pc can replace the complete chain of evidence.
- [ ] Can explain that even if public TLEs reconstruct a series of maneuvers and a close-range conjunction, what the system detects is still a "deviation from normal"; this is not equivalent to a confirmed maneuver, much less to hostility or a violation.
