## Chapter overview

This book relies mainly on twenty chapters of theory and engineering discussion, supported by this project's case collection, to answer one core question: on public, limited-precision, rapidly changing space data, how can one build a maneuver detection system that is explainable, verifiable, and maintainable? Through thirty-two cases, the book illustrates the complete chain from data quality, rules, statistics, machine learning, and physical gating to national-security decision-making.¹

This chapter distills the cases from each chapter into principles. It does not repeat technical details; it only reviews the book's core limitations, core principles, and future directions.

## Core principles of the book

What runs through the book is not a single method but several principles that must be observed together:

- First confirm what judgments the data can support, then decide what conclusions the algorithm should output.
- Rules, statistics, machine learning, and physical models complement one another; none can replace another.
- Every alert must be able to answer: why it was triggered, which layer produced the signal, which rule or statistic was anomalous, whether the physical model supports it, how likely a false alarm is, and what additional evidence is needed.¹
- Numbers from different data snapshots or evaluation units are not directly merged or compared.
- MEME forecasts are not uniformly equivalent to after-the-fact observed ground truth; thrust plans must be distinguished from actual execution.
- Close approaches do not directly imply hostility; a Pc lacking credible covariance should state its assumptions.
- Failures, non-triggers, and unverified results are retained, not rewritten as successful validations.²

These principles form this book's framework for engineering interpretation. Implementations can differ between systems and should be adapted according to deployment conditions, data sources, and validation scope.

## Closing words

This book does not claim that the problem has been solved. The answer it can honestly give is:

> **Space situational awareness is not a single model, but a reproducible, auditable, and maintainable chain of evidence.**¹

A single data source, a single model, a single metric, or a single decision is not sufficient to support space situational awareness; what must be built is a reproducible, auditable, and maintainable chain of evidence.

**Sidebar: UN General Assembly Resolution 77/41 (2022)**

On April 18, 2022, the United States announced that it would not conduct destructive direct-ascent anti-satellite missile tests and called on other countries to follow. On December 7 of the same year, the UN General Assembly adopted Resolution 77/41 by 155 votes in favor, 9 against, and 9 abstentions, calling on states to commit not to conduct such tests; China and Russia voted against, and India abstained. The resolution is not legally binding. One precondition that allows such norms to be discussed at all is that the debris produced by every test is cataloged and tracked. Only what can be seen can be constrained; a reproducible, auditable chain of evidence likewise begins with being able to see.
