## Chapter overview

Imagine you are sitting in front of the monitoring screens of a space operations center. At three in the morning, the system pops up a warning: a foreign satellite at an altitude of 550 km has had its orbital plane shift westward by a full 2.2 degrees over the past 12 hours.

Should the duty officer sound the alarm? Has this satellite secretly fired its thrusters in preparation for approaching our assets?

Not so fast. This 2.2-degree shift can be explained without burning a single drop of fuel. The main cause of the orbital plane's rotation is not a thruster on the satellite, but the Earth beneath our feet, which is "slightly thick around the waist."

Satellites in space have neither the asphalt lanes of cars nor the fixed air routes of airliners; at every moment they fly under the pull of invisible forces such as Earth's gravity, atmospheric drag, lunar and solar gravitation, and Earth's oblateness. An analyst who cannot read this "language of orbital mechanics" can easily misjudge nature's everyday rhythms as hostile maneuvers, or fail to notice a real threat when it occurs.

This chapter takes apart TLE (two-line element set), the most widely used public orbital data format in the world. You will learn to read the size, shape, and orientation of a satellite's orbit from just two lines of numbers, and to capture anomalous signals through differences between adjacent records. Finally, we take the literature knowledge map of Case 11 as the Main case and step into the archive of earlier research to see what methods researchers have tried over the past twenty-plus years, what limitations they encountered, and why this book chose a different path.¹

**Sidebar: Operation Moonwatch**

In 1956, Fred L. Whipple, director of the Smithsonian Astrophysical Observatory, launched Operation Moonwatch in conjunction with the International Geophysical Year, recruiting amateur observers to track artificial satellites. More than a hundred observing teams around the world, with each observer watching a small patch of night sky through a specially made telescope, served as backup to the optical network of 12 Baker-Nunn tracking cameras; the program continued until 1975. Satellite cataloging has involved citizens from the very beginning; today, anyone can download TLEs for independent analysis, continuing the same tradition.

## Semi-Major Axis: The Core Indicator Revealing Energy Changes

Of all orbital parameters, the one detection systems watch most closely is the semi-major axis $a$. For a near-circular low-Earth-orbit satellite, the semi-major axis minus Earth's radius is roughly what we commonly call the "satellite altitude."

A TLE does not state the semi-major axis directly; instead, it gives the mean motion $n$, that is, how many times per day the satellite circles the Earth. Through Kepler's third law, the semi-major axis can be derived:


$$
a = \left(\frac{\mu}{n^2}\right)^{1/3}
$$


where $\mu$ is Earth's gravitational parameter, approximately 398,600.4418 km³/s². The mean motion in a TLE is recorded in "revolutions/day" and must be converted to radians per second before substitution: n (rad/s) = n (rev/day) × 2π ÷ 86,400; the resulting a is in kilometers.

Behind this formula is an intuitive physical law: the lower you fly, the faster you go; the higher you fly, the slower you go.

- When a satellite's orbit gets lower, it circles faster, and the mean motion $n$ rises.
- When a satellite's orbit gets higher, it circles more slowly, and the mean motion $n$ falls.
- When a thruster firing changes the orbital energy, the semi-major axis $a$ shows a step-like jump, which may indicate a maneuver.

However, analysts must keep a clear head here: the mean motion in a TLE is an SGP4 mean element that already includes the averaged effect of long-period perturbations, and cannot be treated directly as the satellite's instantaneous (osculating) element at any given second. Therefore, the semi-major axis derived from a TLE can serve only as a consistent approximation for before-and-after comparison, not directly as the precise instantaneous orbital altitude; comparisons should fix the conversion method, time interval, and data source.

## J2 Perturbation: The "Background Noise" That Must Be Removed Before Detection

Back to three in the morning at the start of this chapter: why would the orbital plane of a satellite that has not fired its thrusters shift 2.2 degrees in half a day?

The reason is that the Earth is not a perfect sphere. Because of its rotation, the Earth's equator bulges slightly outward, like a slightly squashed orange. This effect of Earth's oblateness is called J2 perturbation. It continuously tugs on inclined satellite orbits, causing the entire orbital plane to precess regularly like a spinning top.

Take a Starlink satellite at an altitude of 550 km and an inclination of 53 degrees as an example: J2 perturbation causes the right ascension of the ascending node $\Omega$ to drift westward by about 4.5 degrees per day. If you directly subtract two TLEs taken 12 hours apart, you will see a change of about 2.2 degrees; a system that does not understand this principle will keep issuing false alarms of "large-scale orbit changes."

Therefore, before analyzing anomalies, a detection system must first remove the expected drift caused by Earth's oblateness:


$$
\dot{\Omega}_{J2} =
-\frac{3}{2}
n J_2
\left(\frac{R_E}{p}\right)^2
\cos i
$$


where $J_2 \approx 0.0010826$ is Earth's oblateness coefficient, $R_E = 6378.137$ km is Earth's equatorial radius, $p = a(1-e^2)$ is the semi-latus rectum, and $i$ is the orbital inclination.

**Sidebar: Measuring Earth's Flattening from Sputnik 2**

In 1958, R.H. Merson and D.G. King-Hele of the United Kingdom published a paper in *Nature* that used the precession of the nodal line of the Soviet Union's second artificial satellite, Sputnik 2, to estimate Earth's flattening. What they relied on was exactly the effect in this section: the equatorial bulge makes the orbital plane rotate slowly about Earth's axis, as opposed to orbital decay caused by drag. In 1959, the two published a new flattening value based on measurements of satellite orbits, about 1/298.1 according to the paper's abstract. The $J_2$ in the formula was first measured from the deflection of the orbital planes of the first artificial satellites.

Only after removing this known background noise, if the residual in the right ascension of the ascending node still deviates clearly from zero, might it be a maneuver, a tracking error, or some other anomaly.

**Sidebar: Vanguard 1 and the "Pear-Shaped Earth"**

Vanguard 1, which entered orbit on March 17, 1958, and lost contact in 1964, remains the oldest artificial satellite in orbit. Three NASA researchers, including John O'Keefe, analyzed changes in its orbit and published a paper in *Science* in 1959 indicating that the Earth's shape has a "pear-shaped" component, with the northern and southern hemispheres not symmetric. It is important to be clear: the pear shape refers to the third-degree zonal harmonic J3 of the gravity field, not the equatorial bulge J2 in this section. Inferring invisible forces from slow changes in an orbit is also the principle maneuver detection relies on.

## Differences: From Still Photos to a Live Monitor

Looking at a single TLE is like looking at a single photo: you only know "where the satellite was at that moment." To judge whether it has moved, you must put two adjacent TLEs together and calculate the difference:


$$
\Delta x = x_{k+1} - x_k
$$


where $x$ can be the semi-major axis, inclination, eccentricity, or the right ascension of the ascending node after removing the J2 effect. $k$ is the index of the same satellite's TLEs sorted by epoch; the difference is "the later record minus the earlier record," and a positive value indicates an increase. The time interval between two adjacent records is not fixed and depends on the TLE publication frequency, so the length of the interval must be considered along with the magnitude of change; if there is a long gap between two records, a single difference accumulates all the change during the gap, a problem left for Chapter 6. This seemingly simple subtraction is the smallest signal unit of the entire detection system.

On a difference curve, different physical behaviors leave different "fingerprints":

- Quiet period: when the satellite is not maneuvering, the difference only jitters within a noise band around zero.
- Natural decay: the difference shows a monotonic, small-step, continuous decline, usually because drag from the thin atmosphere is pulling the satellite down.
- Propulsive maneuver: the difference suddenly jumps up or down beyond the normal noise band, possibly due to a thruster firing.
- Data-gap trap: if a jump happens to appear right after an observation gap of several days without updates, extrapolation error must be checked first; it cannot be taken directly as a maneuver.

## Scientific Discipline: TLEs Are "Clues," Not "Courtroom Evidence"

Before moving on to more complex algorithms, one rule must be established: TLEs are a feature source for finding clues, not the answer key for verifying the truth.

Public TLEs are the product of mathematically fitting ground observations; they inherently carry errors, and their update times are irregular. We can use them to screen out targets that "seem a bit off" from a large number of satellites; but to claim that an algorithm's AUC, recall, or false-alarm rate meets the mark, more reliable ground truth must be produced, such as precision ephemerides published by satellite operators (e.g., MEME), or firing logs and ΔV information provided by the International Laser Ranging Service (ILRS) and the International DORIS Service (IDS).

This is the scientific discipline space situational awareness must follow: discover suspicious signals from public TLEs, then validate algorithms with rigorous ground truth.

### Case Background: Why Look Back Through Earlier Work?

In engineering and research, one of the most expensive forms of waste is believing you have invented a new method, only to find that others have already tried it, or even proven that it does not work.

### Four Approaches in Existing Research and Their Bottlenecks

Over the past twenty-plus years, research teams attempting to catch satellite maneuvers from TLEs have broadly followed four technical approaches, each with its own limitations:

| Technical approach | Representative methods | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Orbital mechanics foundations | SGP4 and SDP4 propagation models and TLE error characteristics | Lays the foundation for orbit propagation and error understanding | Responsible for predicting position; does not directly solve the detection problem |
| 2. Single-statistic thresholds | Δa threshold, polynomials, LOWESS | Simple logic, low cost, easy to deploy | Hard to balance false alarms and misses: too low a threshold causes false alarms to surge, too high misses small electric-propulsion maneuvers |
| 3. Statistical change-point detection | CUSUM, BOCPD, SSA, MAD | Can quantify changes in time series with probabilistic models | A single method is still limited, and tends to lose accuracy when facing data gaps and non-Gaussian noise |
| 4. Physical drag models | NRLMSISE-00, NRLMSIS 2.0 | Physically grounded; can estimate natural drag | Affected by space weather and model error; errors may swamp small maneuvers |

### How This Book's Method Differs: Not a Single Tool, but Multiple Layers of Filters

Having seen the four earlier approaches, this book does not pursue some single strongest detector, but builds an architecture that fuses multiple channels with rigorously tiered ground truth:

- From going it alone to joint operations: detection does not end with a single detector, but fuses rules, statistical change points, atmospheric drag residuals, and machine learning, so that different tools cover for one another.
- Building an exam hall with tiered ground truth: when evaluating models, TLEs are not used to test themselves; instead, independent sources such as MEME precision ephemerides and IDS/DORIS firing records are introduced.
- Refusing to look only at overall scores: beyond overall accuracy, unseen satellites (unseen-satellite hold-out), different time periods (out-of-time), and different evaluation units are deliberately separated to test generalization.
- Recording the paths that did not work: later chapters will candidly disclose which methods did not succeed, such as deep learning bringing no gain, and the failure to reproduce atmospheric density inversion from TLEs.²

### National Security Implications

In defense and national security systems, whether a detection algorithm can go into operation often depends not on how impressive its numbers look in the lab, but on whether it can withstand rigorous engineering scrutiny. A literature review avoids reinventing the wheel and avoids mistaking known limitations for new discoveries. Case 11 reminds us of four things:

1. A verifiable scientific basis: warning indicators must have a clear physical and mathematical basis and cannot be unexplainable black boxes.
2. Clear boundaries of innovation: one must know what the new system catches that traditional methods miss, and in which scenarios it fails.
3. Institutionalized records of failure: only by writing dead ends into the technical record can later teams avoid stumbling at the same spot.
4. Independent reproduction and review: only models that can be independently reproduced and reviewed by third parties deserve decision-makers' trust.
