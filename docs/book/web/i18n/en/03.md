## Chapter overview

Imagine a space detection problem: a low-Earth-orbit satellite adjusts its orbital altitude by 1 km.

If it completes this within 1 day, the ground monitoring system will see a clear signal: under this chapter's simulation assumptions, the single-step signal-to-noise ratio is about 52σ, far above typical detection thresholds. But if it splits the same 1 km displacement over 14 days, advancing only tens of meters each day, the single-step signal-to-noise ratio drops to about 3.72σ. The signal is still there, but more than ten times weaker than when completed in one day; if the noise is larger, or the maneuver is split over a longer period, it may fall below a reliable decision threshold.

This is the problem detection systems must face in the era of low-Earth-orbit mega-constellations: not every maneuver appears as an obvious jump; once a single adjustment is split into many small steps, the signal of each step is diluted.

In the past, space was dominated by a small number of large satellites, mostly using chemical propulsion, and a single large orbit change often left a fairly obvious change in the orbital data. Today, mega-constellations such as Starlink, OneWeb, and Qianfan are turning low Earth orbit into a dynamic network made up of tens of thousands of nodes. These satellites commonly carry electric propulsion systems with low thrust but long operating times, which also changes the operating logic of space traffic, orbit management, and national security.

This chapter takes Case 3, Starlink electric-propulsion maneuvers, as its Main case, showing readers two very different maneuver scenarios in mega-constellations: conspicuous, rapid post-launch orbit raising, and low-key station-keeping that tests the limits of detection. We will see why simply lengthening the observation time cannot solve every problem, and why one must learn to read the entire constellation in order to identify true anomalies.¹

## The Value of Low-Earth-Orbit Communications Satellites

Unlike geosynchronous satellites at an altitude of about 36,000 km, low-Earth-orbit communications satellites typically operate at altitudes from several hundred to a little over a thousand kilometers. Being closer to the Earth's surface, they have lower signal latency and lower terminal power requirements, making them suitable for broadband communications, the Internet of Things, emergency communications, and mobile connectivity, and also making direct-to-phone satellite connections possible.

**Sidebar: Iridium Nearly Deorbited Entirely**

On August 13, 1999, Iridium LLC, a pioneer of low-Earth-orbit communications constellations, filed for bankruptcy protection after defaulting on its credit, and stopped service late at night on March 17, 2000. According to Spaceflight Now's reporting at the time, the system had cost USD 5 billion, with handsets at about USD 3,000 each and calls at about USD 7 per minute; the 66 primary satellites were scheduled to begin deorbiting within weeks, at an estimated cost of USD 50 million and possibly taking two years. In the end, the satellites were not deorbited; according to reports, a new company bought them for USD 25 million. The fate of a constellation also depends on its business model.

For national security, this low-latency, wide-coverage constellation network is not merely a commercial service but also an important pillar of national information resilience:

- **Backup communications in disasters and conflicts**: when earthquakes, tsunamis, or military conflicts affect terrestrial fiber and base stations, low-Earth-orbit satellites can provide backup.
- **Filling geographic blind spots**: providing connectivity for ships on the high seas, remote mountain areas, and outlying islands.
- **Resilience of government and military communications**: thousands of distributed nodes are harder to paralyze completely with a single strike.
- **Remote sensing, navigation, and intelligence support**: supporting the downlink of remote-sensing data, navigation augmentation (LEO-PNT), and time synchronization, as well as space situational awareness and intelligence work.

When communications no longer depend solely on terrestrial base stations or a few high-orbit satellites, space infrastructure becomes part of national information resilience.

**Sidebar: OneWeb's Bankruptcy and Restructuring**

On March 27, 2020, OneWeb filed for bankruptcy protection in federal bankruptcy court in New York, intending to sell the company; at the time, 74 satellites were already in orbit. CEO Adrian Steckel said the company's situation was the result of the economic impact of the COVID-19 crisis. On November 20 of the same year, OneWeb emerged from bankruptcy, with a consortium of the UK government and India's Bharti Global injecting USD 1 billion in new equity. A national government directly funding the takeover of a low-Earth-orbit constellation shows that mega-constellations have come to be seen as strategic assets, not merely a business.

## The Orbital Logic of Mega-Constellations

Looking up at a mega-constellation, one might think it resembles a handful of beans randomly scattered into space; but in orbital mechanics, it is a highly structured three-dimensional traffic network, forming a predictable coverage network from altitude, inclination, orbital planes, and phase. A typical low-Earth-orbit communications constellation includes:

- **Multiple orbital planes**: spread around the Earth like the segments of an orange, so that as the Earth rotates, satellites pass over different longitudes.
- **Multiple satellites within each plane**: satellites in the same orbital plane maintain spacing, like a relay team linked head to tail.
- **Specific inclination groups**: determining how high a latitude the satellites can cover.
- **Layered orbital shells**: different batches of satellites are distributed in shells of similar but slightly different altitudes.
- **Regular station-keeping and orbit adjustments**: keeping the entire network in formation.

Taking Starlink as an example, its electric-propulsion maneuvers fall broadly into two categories: post-launch orbit raising is a rapid, continuous climb to operational altitude; station-keeping consists of small, distributed fine adjustments to maintain orbital position and phase. Under the observation windows and noise assumptions of the Starlink cases listed, the physical magnitudes of these two scenarios differ by two to three orders of magnitude, which also determines whether a ground monitoring system "sees through it at a glance" or "must carefully discriminate."¹

**Sidebar: Starship Delivers 26 Starlink V3 Satellites in One Flight**

At 22:47 (UTC) on November 13, 2026, SpaceX conducted Starship's 15th test flight from Starbase, Texas, under the mission name Starlink Group 31-2. The former is a planned value and the latter a preliminary post-insertion orbit; neither is the operational orbit. The satellites' subsequent climb is exactly the "post-launch orbit raising" described in the previous paragraph. The final report analyzed the first batch of 26 satellites (Group 31-1, NORAD 100855–100880, with TLEs from 2026-09-30): the median altitude on the first day was 267–274 km; they climbed to a parking altitude of about 316 km in 7–12 days and reached the operational altitude of about 345 km a median of 17 days after the first TLE; L1 covered about 40% of the post-insertion climb, and the uncovered part was added as low-confidence review items. The second batch has appeared in the public catalog since November 18 (international designators 2026-256A to AB, NORAD 101358–101383), with TLE altitudes of 274–307 km on that day, already above the insertion value; its orbital phases can be analyzed in the same way only after several weeks of TLEs have accumulated.³

## Rowing Against the Current: Why Do Low-Earth-Orbit Satellites Need Continuous Orbit Maintenance?

In low Earth orbit at several hundred kilometers, space is not an absolute vacuum. Thin molecules of the upper atmosphere continue to exert atmospheric drag on satellites, consuming orbital energy and causing altitude to gradually decrease. Without station-keeping, a satellite will eventually descend into denser atmosphere, accelerate its decay, and reenter the atmosphere.

Thus, the life of a low-Earth-orbit satellite is like rowing against the current; it must rely on its propulsion system to regularly perform:

- **Semi-major axis maintenance**: restoring altitude consumed by atmospheric drag.
- **Orbital phase adjustment**: fine-tuning speed to maintain spacing with the satellites ahead and behind.
- **Collision avoidance**: actively avoiding debris or other satellites when they approach.
- **Batch orbit raising or lowering**: when the mission is adjusted, a whole batch of satellites switches operational shells together.
- **End-of-life deorbit**: actively lowering the orbit so the satellite reenters the atmosphere, reducing debris that remains in orbit long-term.

For monitoring personnel, the real challenge is that these are all normal operational behaviors. Without a constellation-level baseline and physical models, a system may falsely report large numbers of routine fine adjustments as anomalies.

**Sidebar: Controlled Lowering of the Kuiper Prototypes**

On October 6, 2023, an Atlas V rocket launched Amazon's two Kuiper prototype satellites into orbit. In May 2024, Amazon announced that over several months it would perform a series of controlled maneuvers with the onboard propulsion systems to lower the two satellites to about 350 km, then let atmospheric drag carry them to reentry; Amazon also committed that its future satellites would actively deorbit within one year after the end of their missions. Looking back through the methods of this book, this kind of controlled lowering is a series of deliberate maneuvers, and its appearance in TLEs will differ from the natural decay of Chapter 5.

## From Single Satellite to Constellation

Faced with thousands of low-Earth-orbit satellites making fine adjustments every day, analysts who check them one by one will quickly be drowned in alerts. To understand mega-constellations, one must raise one's perspective to constellation-level behavior.

Satellites in mega-constellations often show synchronized maneuvers: satellites from the same launch batch may climb collectively to a new operational altitude within a similar time window; members of the same shell may also perform station-keeping on a schedule.

**Sidebar: The "String of Pearls" over Leiden**

On May 23, 2019, SpaceX launched the first batch of 60 Starlink satellites. The next evening, Dutch satellite tracker Marco Langbroek, using a low-light camera in Leiden, captured a line of points of light moving in a string and counted at least 56; at the time it was about 22.5 hours after launch, and the pass time differed by less than 3 minutes from the search orbit he had calculated himself. According to reports, a Dutch UFO reporting website subsequently received more than 150 reports. This string of lights was exactly the batch of satellites that had just entered orbit and had not yet raised their orbits and dispersed, and finding them relied on an orbit calculated by an amateur himself.

Only when the system first learns "how this constellation normally moves" and then judges which satellites deviate from normal does the logic of anomaly detection become clear:

- **A whole batch moving in sync**: may be normal deployment, shell adjustment, or collective avoidance.
- **Only one satellite deviating from its peers**: may be propulsion failure, a mission anomaly, or an event requiring further investigation.

But "deviating from peers" does not equal "hostile behavior." It is only a signal that needs verification; judging intent also requires mission context, multi-source data, and human review.

### Case Background: How Long Must the Observation Window Be to See Electric-Propulsion Maneuvers?

Case 3 answers a practical question: how long must the TLE observation window be to capture clear evidence of Starlink electric-propulsion maneuvers?

The answer is not a single number of days; two scenarios must be distinguished: the orbit-raising phase and the station-keeping phase. It also depends on data update frequency, noise level, maneuver duration, and whether the purpose of the analysis is real-time alerting or retrospective review.²

Below, we first look at observations of two real satellites, then use a simulation to examine how detection capability changes when the same net displacement is split more slowly.

### Scenario 1: The Orbit-Raising Phase, a Conspicuous Climb Signal

Newly deployed Starlink satellites are usually first inserted into a lower orbit and then gradually climb to operational altitude using electric propulsion.

Taking STARLINK-37457 as an example, its measured rate during the active climb was about 8.45 km per day, with a total semi-major axis change of about 58.1 km over 6.9 days. Calculated at a 1-day TLE interval, this corresponds to a signal-to-noise ratio of about 440σ.²

This is not a faint signal that needs careful searching; even if TLE updates are infrequent, 1 to 2 days are enough to form overwhelming evidence.

Zooming in on this trajectory reveals that it is not a smooth straight line but a "jump a bit, pause a bit" staircase. This reflects that electric-propulsion orbit raising, at TLE time resolution, often shows thrust arcs alternating with coasting segments.

### Scenario 2: The Station-Keeping Phase, the Real Technical Bottleneck

Once a satellite reaches operational altitude and enters the station-keeping phase, detection difficulty rises markedly.

Taking STARLINK-3005 as an example, its robust noise floor over the most recent 60 days is about 19 m, and its typical TLE update interval (median) is about 8 hours. Between November 4 and 5, 2026, this satellite showed a semi-major axis change of about -5,091 m; the change was concentrated within a single step, with a single-step signal-to-noise ratio of about 265σ, far above the 20σ high-significance limit, so in the R7 pipeline it is listed as a candidate event. The casebook says it "can be clearly judged to be a maneuver"; but from public TLEs alone it remains a candidate event, and whether it is a maneuver has to be confirmed with operator data or precise ephemerides.²,³

### What These Numbers Can and Cannot Show

This table does not say that "all Starlink satellites complete a 1 km maneuver over 14 days," nor that "detection will certainly be missed after 14 days." It examines the behavior of a point-by-point online detector under fixed assumptions: the more slowly the same net displacement is split, the lower the per-step signal-to-noise ratio, and the higher the detection difficulty.

The "approximate detection rate" in the table is the proportion of injected signals hit by the point-by-point detector in the simulation, such as 0.929 when spanning 14 days; it does not equal a reliable alert threshold for practical deployment. Whether alerts are reliable in practice also depends on the false-alarm rate, detection latency, and rules requiring several consecutive confirmations before an alert is issued, none of which are within this simulation. When measured against MEME ground truth, the final report's recall for small events (a single semi-major axis change of 1–5 km) at the 5% false-alarm operating point is only 0.091 (1 of 11), with signal-to-noise ratios often below 2; for the same net Δa, the detection rate of gradual changes spread over several days is also far lower than that of a single step. The 0.929 in the simulation should not be understood as an actual detection rate.³

The 19 m here is an estimate of the MAD of raw record-to-record differences over the most recent 60 days, belonging to a specific data window and estimator. For the same satellite, other reasonable definitions, such as excluding jumps or detrending, can give results differing by nearly a factor of 4; it is defined differently from the noise floor figures elsewhere in this book and cannot be directly compared with them.

"Differing by nearly a factor of 4" comes from a recomputation of Case 3 on 2026-09-18: at that time, the same method applied to STARLINK-3005's roughly 160 adjacent differences over the most recent 60 days, 9 of which exceeded 0.3 km in absolute value (maximum 564 m), gave 11.6 m; another script that excluded jumps and detrended gave only 3.12 m. This recomputation and the 19 m adopted in this chapter belong to data windows at different points in time.

The σ multiples in this chapter are calculated as follows. The noise floor is taken from STARLINK-3005's raw record-to-record semi-major axis differences over the most recent 60 days, robustly estimated with MAD, without removing jumps (the standard deviation over the same period including outliers is about 770 m); the satellite's typical TLE update interval (median) is about 8 hours. 440σ converts STARLINK-37457's single-day climb (about 8.45 km) using this noise floor; 265σ is the single-step signal-to-noise ratio of STARLINK-3005's change of about 5,091 m in that one step. The decision reference adopted by the case is: 2 to 3σ is usually regarded as a clear signal, while in the simulation, whether the per-step signal-to-noise ratio falls below 2 serves as the boundary for reliable decisions.

Measured results from a single satellite should also not be extrapolated to the average performance of the entire constellation.

### Key Insight: There Is No Universal Observation Window

The insight of Case 3 is: the existence of orbit raising is not hard to detect; station-keeping is the real limit of low-Earth-orbit electric-propulsion maneuver detection. But when orbit raising is spread over several days at a few kilometers per step, the L1 rules, which look only at single-step changes, can still cover only part of it: for the first batch of Starlink V3 in the final report, L1 covered about 40% of the post-insertion climb, and the low-confidence review items must be examined together with it.³

If the purpose is real-time alerting, a point-by-point online detector is limited by the per-step signal-to-noise ratio; the longer a maneuver drags on and the weaker the thrust, the more likely the single-step signal falls below a reliable decision threshold (here meaning the decision boundary for per-step signal-to-noise ratio in the simulation; practical alert thresholds must also incorporate false-alarm rate, latency, and consecutive-confirmation rules). If the purpose is retrospective review, directly comparing the net displacement between the start and end of the window may still yield a high signal-to-noise ratio. To reliably detect small, gradual, low-thrust maneuvers, MEME or precision ephemerides are needed, supplemented when necessary by dedicated sensor data.

This also shows that observation window length is not a universal solution; data quality, update frequency, maneuver duration, and evaluation goals jointly determine detection capability.

## National Security Implications

The rise of low-Earth-orbit mega-constellations forces national security agencies to rethink what counts as an "anomaly." Seeing a single satellite is no longer enough; systems also need three capabilities.

First, understand the physical characteristic of electric propulsion systems "breaking the whole into parts," and know where the detection limits of public data lie. Second, build a constellation-level baseline of normal behavior, filtering large volumes of routine operational signals from group patterns and concentrating analytical resources on individuals that truly deviate from normal. Third, when public TLEs reach their limits, bring in precision ephemerides, dedicated sensors, and cross-agency collaboration to supply the information needed for verification.

These capabilities share a common premise: an anomalous signal is only a starting point, not a conclusion. Between "a satellite deviating from normal" and "this is a threat" lie data quality, mission context, intent assessment, and human review.
