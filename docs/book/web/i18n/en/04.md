## Chapter overview

On September 2, 2019, the European Space Agency's (ESA) wind-observing satellite Aeolus, in an orbit about 320 km high, fired its thrusters three times in succession at 10:14, 10:17, and 10:18 UTC, intending to raise its orbit by about 350 m. About half an orbit later, at 11:02, it would otherwise have had a conjunction with Starlink 44; this Starlink satellite was at the time being deliberately lowered by SpaceX from its planned 550 km to about 310 km to test its propulsion system.

When ESA made its final decision the day before (a Sunday), the probability of collision was about 1 in 1,000, ten times its threshold of 1 in 10,000. On August 28, ESA had contacted the Starlink team, which replied within a day that it did not currently plan to take action. According to SpaceX afterward, the probability it had calculated at the time was only about 1 in 50,000; after the probability rose, a bug in its paging system prevented the on-call staff from seeing the follow-up emails. This was the first time ESA maneuvered to avoid a mega-constellation satellite, and coordination between the two sides relied on an exchange of emails.

How can someone on the ground who sees only orbital data tell that these three firings were avoidance, and not something else?

Satellites are not stationary in orbit. They continuously adjust their orbits because of atmospheric drag, gravitational perturbations, mission requirements, collision avoidance, and lifetime management. Understanding why satellites move is the first step in judging orbital anomalies. This chapter takes the manual avoidance maneuver of a Bangladeshi satellite in Case 29 as its Main case to explain how the purpose, timing, and decision conditions of a maneuver determine the security implications of an orbital change.¹

## Learning goals

After reading this chapter, readers should understand:

## Orbits Are Not Static

Even without thruster intervention, satellite orbits change over time. The main causes include:

- J2 perturbation caused by Earth's oblateness.
- Orbital decay caused by atmospheric drag.
- Solar and lunar gravitation.
- Solar radiation pressure.
- Non-uniformity of Earth's gravity field.
- Density variations caused by space weather.

## Types of Satellite Maneuvers

Satellite maneuvers can be divided into several types:

| Type | Purpose | Typical characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Station-keeping | Counteract drag and perturbations | Small, periodic |
| Orbit raising | Reach operational altitude or compensate for decay | Semi-major axis increases |
| Orbit lowering | Deorbit, reentry, or mission adjustment | Semi-major axis decreases |
| Collision avoidance | Reduce conjunction risk | Short duration, specific direction |
| Phasing | Adjust relative position within the constellation | Along-track change |
| Inclination change | Change covered latitudes | Rare and fuel-intensive |
| End-of-life disposal | Reduce collision and debris risk | Lower orbit or deorbit |

**Sidebar: The Space Station Dodges Fengyun-1C Debris in 2021**

At 20:15 UTC on November 10, 2021, the Progress MS-18 cargo ship docked to the International Space Station fired for 361 seconds, raising the station's orbit by 1,240 m to 420.72 km. Roscosmos said that without the adjustment, a fragment of Fengyun-1C would have approached to about 600 m the next morning. This fragment came from the 2007 anti-satellite test mentioned in Chapter 1. Compared with the table above, this was a typical collision avoidance: a short firing in a clear direction, aimed only at reducing conjunction risk.

## Why the Semi-Major Axis Alone Is Not Enough

The semi-major axis is an important signal for detecting maneuvers, but it is not the only evidence.

If the semi-major axis decreases, it may be due to:

- Atmospheric drag.
- Active orbit lowering.
- Deorbit operations.
- End-of-life decay.

If the semi-major axis increases, it may be due to:

- Orbit raising.
- Batch climbing.
- Station-keeping.
- Entering the operational orbit after deployment.

Therefore, the system must combine multiple orbital elements, physical models, and constellation behavior to make reliable judgments.

## Time Scales of Maneuvers

Satellite maneuvers can be divided into:

- Step maneuvers: completed in a short time, with an obvious jump in the semi-major axis.
- Gradual maneuvers: completed with low thrust over a long time, with the change spread over multiple TLEs.
- Continuous maintenance: periodic small adjustments.
- Anomalous behavior: cannot be explained by the mission, physics, or the constellation baseline.

Gradual low-thrust maneuvers are the main challenge in detecting low-Earth-orbit electric-propulsion satellites. For the same net change in semi-major axis, if completed as a gradual arc, the single-step increments are spread out, making it harder for change-point detectors to identify.

### Case Background

Case 29 describes a real manual avoidance maneuver: a Bangladeshi satellite, without VCMs or commercial collision-avoidance tools, relied on public warnings and manual calculation to avoid debris from a retired Indian satellite.²

This case is not one of this project's measured analysis cases; rather, it serves as a real-world counterpart to Case 28. Its value lies not in showing that this project's system detected the event, but in illustrating that when collision warnings, orbital uncertainty, and decision time pressure coexist, a collision-avoidance maneuver is a space security behavior that must be understood, verified, and quickly put into action.

The details of the event come from Bangladeshi media (The Daily Star, TBS News) and Taiwanese media (民生電子報, Min Sheng E-Paper) reporting the satellite operator's statements; at the time of verification, no corroboration from Indian official sources or independent third parties was found, and this project has not performed any recomputation or verification of this event.

According to these reports, the closest distance between the debris and BS-1 during the pass was 852 m, and the satellite was not damaged; this distance is likewise only the operator's statement, without independent corroboration.

### The Decision Chain of a Collision-Avoidance Maneuver

An effective collision-avoidance maneuver typically involves the following steps:

1. Obtain a conjunction warning.
2. Assess the time and distance of closest approach.
3. Estimate the probability of collision and its uncertainty.
4. Decide whether a maneuver is needed.
5. Plan the avoidance direction and ΔV.
6. Execute the maneuver.
7. Verify the new orbit and subsequent conjunction risk.

Case 29 shows that even without advanced commercial tools, public warnings, manual calculation, and swift decision-making can still possibly avoid a collision. But it also exposes a risk: without sufficient orbital uncertainty information, manual judgment may risk being too early, too late, or in the wrong direction.

**Sidebar: The Space Station's Emergency Reboost in 2020**

On September 22, 2020, a piece of debris was predicted to pass near the International Space Station, at a closest distance of about 1.39 km. The warning came late, and the three crew members were ordered to move to the Russian segment, near the Soyuz, in preparation for evacuation; at 5:19 p.m. Eastern Time, Progress 75 fired for 150 seconds to raise the orbit, and NASA said the crew was never in danger. NASA later determined that the debris came from a fairing cover of the H-2A rocket that had launched GOSAT-2: it broke up in July at an altitude of about 600 km, and one fragment was dragged down to the station's altitude by drag, reentering five days later. From warning to firing, the entire decision chain was compressed into less than a day.

### National Security Implications

The national security implications of a collision-avoidance maneuver lie in the following conditions, not in the maneuver itself:

- Whether a credible warning can be obtained in time.
- Whether the risk can be correctly assessed.
- Whether planning and execution can be rapid.
- Whether secondary conjunctions or new risks can be avoided.
- Whether responsible judgments can be made when information is insufficient.

These are exactly the problems that space traffic management and collision risk management must address.

**Sidebar: Sentinel-1A's 140 Meters**

On May 16, 2022, ESA's Sentinel-1A changed its orbit by 140 m to avoid a fragment of Cosmos 1408 a few centimeters in size, which might otherwise have collided with it almost head-on. Mission control had less than 24 hours of warning, and planning took only a few hours. Cosmos 1408's original orbit was about 200 km lower than Sentinel-1A's; it was the explosive energy when it was destroyed by an anti-satellite missile in 2021 that pushed some of its fragments up into Sentinel-1A's orbit. The maneuver was small; what mattered was whether the warning was credible and whether the decision was fast enough.

## Maneuvers and National Security

The national security implications of a satellite maneuver depend on its purpose, timing, target, and explainability.

- Normal maintenance: predictable, attributable, low risk.
- Collision avoidance: must be quickly confirmed to avoid misjudgment.
- Batch deployment: requires understanding of the constellation's strategy.
- Anomalous orbit lowering: may indicate malfunction, loss of control, or deorbit.
- Anomalous approach: may involve rendezvous, servicing, reconnaissance, or non-cooperative behavior.
- Cessation of maintenance: may be end of life, or an early sign of mission failure.

This study takes FORMOSAT-7 FS7-3 as an example, interpreting that it stopped orbit maintenance from May 2025 onward and decayed silently to 536 km, showing that the system can identify early signs of mission failure or cessation of maintenance.³
