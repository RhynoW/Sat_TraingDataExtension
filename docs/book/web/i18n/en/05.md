## Chapter overview

On March 10, 1989, the Sun produced an intense flare almost directly facing Earth, followed over the next few days by a series of X-class flares. On March 13, the Fredericksburg, Virginia, station recorded the highest daily geomagnetic index since 1960, and parts of Quebec lost power for as long as nine hours.

On the same day, orbit predictions at the U.S. Space Command's Space Surveillance Center began failing on a large scale. The predictions assumed that the most recently measured drag would hold steady, yet the upper atmosphere changed drastically within half a day; for a satellite with a 185-kilometer perigee, the predicted position could be off by 45 nautical miles within 12 hours. Differential correction rejected new observations, and the share of observations tagged as "unknown objects" rose from 18% to more than 40%. The lost-object list, normally fewer than 100, ballooned to more than 1,300, nearly half of the low-Earth-orbit population. Analysts worked around the clock for several days, matching observations by hand, to recover them, and it took several more weeks to return to normal.

The post-event technical report described a phenomenon: as drag increased, the semi-major axis shrank and the mean motion grew, while the other elements barely changed. These satellites had not fired any thrusters, yet their orbital elements looked as though they had been pushed. When a TLE time series suddenly "jumps," how do we know whether it was the satellite that moved, or the atmosphere?

In low Earth orbit, the atmosphere has not truly disappeared. The thin but persistent atmosphere exerts drag on satellites and gradually lowers their orbits. When solar activity strengthens or geomagnetic disturbances intensify, atmospheric density rises, drag increases, and satellites decay faster. Using a comparison of four real satellites in Case 5 and the negative B\* phenomenon in Case 30, this chapter explains how to distinguish natural decay caused by atmospheric drag from active maneuvers, and reveals the physical limits of the TLE drag parameter.¹

## How atmospheric drag affects orbits

Atmospheric drag reduces a satellite's energy, gradually decreasing its semi-major axis and lowering its orbital altitude. For low-Earth-orbit communication satellites, this is a physical phenomenon that must be dealt with every day.

For a Starlink satellite at 550 kilometers altitude during solar maximum, atmospheric drag alone may lower the semi-major axis by 0.01 to 0.1 kilometers per day (the range varies with solar activity and the satellite's area-to-mass ratio).²

This magnitude seems small, but accumulated over several days it can be confused with the orbital change from a small maneuver.

**Sidebar: GOCE's final three weeks**

ESA's gravity-field satellite GOCE was launched in 2009 and operated at an altitude of about 255 kilometers, using an ion thruster to continuously counteract atmospheric drag; it was later lowered step by step to about 224 kilometers. On October 21, 2013, ESA announced that its xenon fuel was exhausted and the mission had ended; on November 11, it entered the atmosphere over the South Atlantic near the Falkland Islands, only about three weeks after propulsion stopped. Once thrust stopped, the drop in semi-major axis caused by drag was no longer offset, and the decay curve in its TLEs bent accordingly.

## Why space weather matters

Space weather is a concrete physical process. Solar activity, the solar wind, geomagnetic storms, and energetic particle events all change the density of the upper atmosphere.

When solar activity strengthens:

- The upper atmosphere heats up and expands.
- Atmospheric density in low Earth orbit rises.
- Satellite drag increases.
- Orbital decay accelerates.
- The TLE semi-major axis shows a systematic decline.

**Sidebar: The solar observatory pulled down by solar activity**

NASA's Solar Maximum Mission satellite (SMM) was dedicated to studying solar activity. The U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) Space Weather Prediction Center wrote that during the March 1989 geomagnetic storm, SMM reportedly "dropped as if it hit a brick wall"; on December 2 of that year, it reentered over the Indian Ocean. A satellite that observed the Sun was ultimately dragged out of orbit at the height of solar activity by the heated, expanded atmosphere, a vivid illustration of the chain reaction above.

This study adjusts the P2 threshold using the F10.7 solar flux: the multiplier follows a parabola, staying at the baseline (×1.0) at or below 70 sfu, multiplied by 1.6 at 200 sfu, with a cap of ×1.8; F10.7 is the observed value on the UTC day of the epoch of the later TLE in the transition. When the Sun is active the atmosphere is more disturbed, so the threshold is raised accordingly; however, the rule-by-rule ablation in the final report shows that this adjustment (P5) has only a small effect on both recall and false alarms.²

**Sidebar: ADEOS-II's three minutes**

On October 24, 2003, the solar-array output of JAXA's satellite ADEOS-II (Midori-II), in a sun-synchronous orbit at about 800 kilometers, dropped from 6 kilowatts to 1 kilowatt within three minutes. This was four days before the X17 flare of October 28, in the early phase of that round of solar storms. A paper by JAXA researchers concluded that the cause was most likely a short circuit or open circuit in the solar-array wiring harness, possibly related to arcing induced by energetic electrons. The threat from space weather is not limited to drag; the methods in this book detect drag effects and cannot see such charging effects themselves.

## NRLMSIS: a third-party witness from a physical model

NRLMSIS is an empirical atmospheric density model that estimates atmospheric density from altitude, latitude, longitude, time, solar activity, and geomagnetic indices.

This study uses NRLMSIS as the basis for computing the drag residual, with two improvements:

1. The Ap index was upgraded from a daily mean to the standard 3-hour resolution ap(7).
2. The latitude and longitude used for the density calculation were changed from a fixed 0/0 to the actual sub-satellite coordinates solved by SGP4.²

As for other inputs, F10.7 uses the observed value and the 81-day centered average on the TLE epoch day, without intraday interpolation; the 3-hour ap above is entered in NRLMSIS 2.1's storm-time mode, and the daily average is used when a 3-hour value does not exist; for near-circular orbits, density is instead integrated from hourly orbit-averaged values; and the equivalent ballistic coefficient is calibrated as the median ratio over all transitions in the query period.² The two improvements above (3-hour ap and sub-satellite coordinates) are intended to let the model reflect the environment the satellite is actually in during geomagnetic storms; the final report also notes that the quantitative effect of these two changes on density and residual accuracy has not yet been verified.²

**Sidebar: The 38 satellites of Starlink Group 4-7**

On February 3, 2022, SpaceX launched 49 Starlink satellites. The geomagnetic storm that began the next day was only a minor G1 event (Kp about 5), yet SpaceX stated that, based on onboard GPS estimates, drag was up to 50% higher than in previous launches. The satellites entered safe mode and flew edge-on to reduce drag, but 38 of them were later counted as having reentered. Subsequent studies estimated the density increase anywhere from 20–30% to more than double, depending on method and reference baseline. The density estimated by a model itself carries uncertainty, and this must be kept in mind when computing the drag residual.

## Drag residual: subtract the known, keep the suspicious

The drag residual is computed as follows:


$$
\text{drag residual} =
\text{observed orbital change}
-
\text{change due to NRLMSIS expected drag}
$$


If the residual is close to zero, the orbital decline can be explained by atmospheric drag. If the residual departs clearly from zero, it may be a maneuver, a propulsion anomaly, data error, or some other unknown factor.

**Sidebar: ASCA loses attitude**

On July 14, 2000, the Sun erupted in a large flare, followed by an intense geomagnetic storm lasting several days. The astronomy satellite ASCA was subjected to atmospheric density disturbances beyond what its attitude control system could handle; it entered safe-hold mode and spun slowly, with its solar panels partly facing away from the Sun, and the Japanese team was never able to restore its attitude. On March 2, 2001, it reentered the atmosphere. Looking back with this book's methods, ASCA after losing control had only natural decay left, and its drag residual should be close to zero; a satellite that had been under control became, in the data, indistinguishable from debris.

This study's fusion scorer takes as input the statistics of five channels (CUSUM, BOCPD, SSA, MAD 3σ, and the NRLMSIS drag residual) within a ±24-hour window. The drag residual also serves as an auxiliary channel and a source of low-confidence review items in the R7 single-satellite report, and as the primary judge for non-Starlink satellites in the unified detection summary, but in neither role does it produce candidate events on its own.²

## Main case: Case 5　How do we tell active maneuvers from natural atmospheric-drag decay?

> **Data sources**: Computed by live queries of this project's TLE database: residual points FORMOSAT-3A 2,549, STARLINK-30273 551, ISS 47,801 (1998–2026, 47,802 TLEs), Van Allen A 1,124; residuals are obtained by differencing two adjacent TLEs, so the number of points is one fewer than the number of TLEs. The section on MEME thrust sawtooth uses SpaceX's publicly released 3-day predictions (from 2026-10-21, 6 Starlink satellites), which are precomputed frozen data.
>
> **Ground truth**: For the TLE portion of the four satellites, the case file does not state whether each event was checked against external burn records; "pure decay," "electric-propulsion station-keeping," "reboost," and "reentry" are interpreted from each satellite's mission status. MEME is SpaceX's own force model and prediction, not measured ground truth, but it is independent of this project's model.
>
> **Key assumptions**: Atmospheric density is computed with NRLMSIS-2.1, and an equivalent ballistic coefficient B is self-calibrated for each satellite (da/dt = -B·ρ·√(μa)); only the residual remaining after subtraction is treated as a signal requiring explanation.
>
> **Limitations**: For satellites that continuously compensate for drag with thrust, the self-calibrated B is not a physical drag coefficient, and the residual represents "deviation from this satellite's normal," not the magnitude of thrust; for the two mature satellites, the model prediction underestimates the MEME coast segments by about 9 times. Data after roughly hour 50 of each MEME file have been excluded, and there are no MEME data for 10-06 to 10-20. A residual above 0.30 kilometers is classified as "not explainable by drag" and counted as grounds for a maneuver candidate; this threshold appears in the final report's drag-residual section and key-parameter table, the case file itself does not state it, and the final report recommends adjusting it by altitude in future work.
>
> **When not to use this method**: When the perigee has already met the reentry criterion (perigee below 150 kilometers, or below 350 kilometers with a decline rate faster than 2 kilometers per day), the drag residual should not be used directly; the reentry gate must be applied first. The residual should not be attributed directly to thrust or used to estimate thrust magnitude; the physical determination should be understood as a classification of "explainable by drag / not explainable by drag (maneuver candidate)."

**Data specification**: Nature measured; Period ISS 1998–2026, FORMOSAT-3A, STARLINK-30273, and Van Allen A use all historical TLEs in the database (exported up to 2026-11-14; start date not stated in the case file), MEME is a 3-day prediction from 2026-10-21; TLE count residual points FORMOSAT-3A 2,549, STARLINK-30273 551, ISS 47,801 (47,802 TLEs), Van Allen A 1,124; Threshold a residual with absolute value above 0.30 kilometers is classified as not explainable by drag (maneuver candidate), and the reentry gate is perigee below 150 kilometers, or below 350 kilometers with a decline rate faster than 2 kilometers per day (using the most recent 45 days of data); False-alarm rate not stated in the case file.

### Case background

Case 5 uses four real satellites to show how "active maneuvers" and "natural atmospheric-drag decay" are distinguished. For each satellite, all historical TLEs in the database were used (exported up to 2026-11-14), and a residual with absolute value above 0.30 kilometers is classified as "not explainable by drag" and counted as grounds for a maneuver candidate; satellites whose perigee has already fallen into the reentry criterion are uniformly classified as non-maneuvering by the reentry gate:

- FORMOSAT-3A: pure atmospheric decay.
- STARLINK-30273: electric-propulsion maneuvers.
- ISS: real reboosts.
- Van Allen A: HEO reentry decay.³

The numbers of residual points for the four satellites are, in order, FORMOSAT-3A 2,549, STARLINK-30273 551, ISS 47,801 (1998–2026), and Van Allen A 1,124. Residuals are obtained by differencing two adjacent TLEs, so the number of points is one fewer than the number of TLEs; the ISS therefore has 47,802 TLEs, consistent with Chapter 6. Atmospheric density is computed with NRLMSIS-2.1.³ The reentry gate criterion is: using the most recent 45 days of data, compute the perigee altitude and its linear decline rate; if the perigee is below 150 kilometers, or below 350 kilometers with a decline rate faster than 2 kilometers per day, the object is classified as reentering.²

### FORMOSAT-3A: pure decay

FORMOSAT-3A's median residual is about 0.3 meters, its maximum residual is about 0.11 kilometers, and the number of points exceeding the threshold is 0.³

This means its orbital decline can be almost entirely explained by atmospheric drag, and the system should not misclassify it as maneuvering.

### Van Allen A: reentry decay

Van Allen A's median residual is about 568.1 meters, its maximum residual is about 708.58 kilometers, and the number of points exceeding the threshold is 0.³

The thousandfold difference between the median and the maximum is not a unit typo: this satellite decayed in an HEO orbit, and after its perigee fell into the reentry criterion, the raw drag residual model lost accuracy in the reentry phase, with the residual growing to about 709 kilometers; the maximum reflects that the model no longer applies in this phase, not that the orbit actually jumped by 708 kilometers. Without the reentry gate, the system might misclassify it as an unprecedented giant maneuver. The gate logic classifies it as natural reentry, with 0 maneuvers.

### Thrust signals in MEME

Case 5 also uses MEME prediction data to show how thrust leaves a sawtooth signal in the orbit: during coasting the orbit is pulled down by drag, thrust pushes it back up, and the net change over three days is almost 0.³

This shows that a TLE can only see the net change between two records; if thrust and drag cancel each other out in between, the TLEs may not reveal it. MEME, because it includes planned thrust, can show the finer alternation between thrust and drag. However, MEME is SpaceX's own force model and prediction, not measured ground truth.

### National security implications

The lesson of Case 5 is:

> Without a physical drag model, normal decay is mistaken for maneuvers; without a reentry gate, reentry is mistaken for the biggest maneuver in history. All three lines of defense are indispensable.³

## Main case 2: Case 30　Negative atmospheric drag?

> **Evidence level: A | Independently recomputed**
>
> **Data sources**: A census of 22,748 near-circular-orbit objects below 1,300 kilometers altitude from 2026-08 onward; ground-truth validation used 283 Starlink satellites and 6,461 TLEs (20 MEME files sampled uniformly per satellite, using only the first 24 hours of each); the L1 control group randomly sampled 300 objects from each population at 450–600 kilometers (fixed random seed).
>
> **Ground truth**: Starlink MEME precise ephemerides (based on the actual rate of change of the one-orbit mean altitude from MEME over the 12 hours before each TLE epoch). OneWeb and Kuiper have no public precise ephemerides and thus no ground truth.
>
> **Key assumptions**: SGP4 has no thrust term, so the extra along-track acceleration during continuous propulsion is fitted into B\*; the first 24 hours of MEME predictions are sufficient to represent the thrust direction.
>
> **Limitations**: Negative B\* for OneWeb and Kuiper can only be inferred indirectly from "far higher than debris at the same altitude," which is a lower level of evidence; the U.S. orbit determination procedure is not public, so the mechanism is inferred backward from results; debris above 800 kilometers also has substantial negative B\*, and B\* itself cannot be measured accurately.
>
> **When not to use this method**: In higher orbits above 800 kilometers, negative B\* alone should not be taken as evidence of thrust; it must be compared with debris at the same altitude. For constellations under propulsion, B\* should not be treated as a measurement of atmospheric density or drag, and satellites under propulsion must be excluded first when inferring thermospheric density from TLE B\*. Treating B\* as a new candidate source also cannot recover the recall ceiling caused by the time resolution of TLEs.

**Data specification**: Nature measured; Period census from 2026-08 onward (end date not stated in the case file), only the first 24 hours of each MEME ground-truth file used; TLE count ground-truth validation 6,461 TLEs (283 Starlink satellites), census 22,748 objects (TLE count not stated in the case file); Threshold no fixed threshold (described by the proportion of negative B\* and the median B\*; MEME altitude-change-rate groups are lowering < −1 km/day and raising > 0.5 km/day); False-alarm rate debris L1 false alarms 1.33% (per transition, computed with the older L1 rules of the case collection; the case file notes that debris does not maneuver, so all are false alarms; the final report measured 0.16 per thousand satellite-days with the current L1 rules on 300 LEO rocket bodies, and since the two differ in unit, sample, and rule version, they cannot be compared directly), single-record B\* judgment AUC 0.913 for lowering and AUC 0.743 for raising.

### Case background

SGP4 has no thrust term. For satellites under continuous propulsion, thrust may be fitted into the drag parameter B\*, producing a physically impossible negative B\*.⁴

Case 30 surveys about 23,000 low-Earth-orbit objects, validates this phenomenon against Starlink MEME ground truth, and then examines its actual impact on this project's detection rules. Only Starlink has ground truth: OneWeb and Kuiper have no public precise ephemerides, so their negative B\* can only be inferred indirectly; the U.S. orbit determination procedure is also not public, and this case describes a mechanism inferred backward from the results.⁴

Precisely, the census covers near-circular-orbit objects below 1,300 kilometers altitude from 2026-08 onward, 22,748 in total. Ground-truth validation used 283 Starlink satellites and 6,461 TLEs: 20 MEME files were sampled uniformly for each satellite, using only the first 24 hours of each, because MEME predictions lose accuracy beyond 24 hours during orbit raising. The control group for the L1 rule randomly sampled 300 objects from each population at 450–600 kilometers, using a fixed random seed.⁴

### Why negative B\* appears

B\* was originally a parameter describing how strongly a satellite is affected by atmospheric drag. In theory, B\* should be positive.

But when a satellite continuously compensates for drag with thrust, the TLE fit may absorb the thrust effect into B\*, making B\* negative. This is not real negative drag but a by-product of the model fit.⁴

**Sidebar: Skylab adjusts drag with attitude**

The U.S. space station Skylab weighed about 77 metric tons after reaching orbit. Solar activity higher than expected increased atmospheric drag and accelerated its decay; the Space Shuttle was also delayed, so the plan to have astronauts install a propulsion unit fell through. In 1978 the control team reactivated Skylab and put it in a low-drag attitude; on June 20, 1979, it was switched to a high-drag attitude to adjust the reentry time, and on July 11 it was allowed to tumble slowly. Most of the debris fell into the Indian Ocean, and some landed in southern Western Australia. When the attitude changes, the drag changes with it; the degree of drag that B\* describes is not a fixed property of the satellite.

### Impact on detection rules

If the system treats negative B\* directly as an anomaly, it may produce a large number of false alarms. If the system ignores B\* entirely, it may miss clues to thrust behavior.

Therefore, B\* should not be treated as an absolute physical quantity but as a feature requiring careful interpretation, judged together with the semi-major axis, the drag residual, constellation behavior, and mission context.⁴

The current R7 workflow of this project does not generate candidate events from B\*. The B\* auxiliary suppression rule (P3) was shown by ablation and FORMOSAT-7 ground-truth validation to have no effect on either recall or false alarms and is no longer included; B\* is used only in the P1 suppression condition (B\*>0) and in the "high drag" diagnostic tag of low-confidence review items.²

### National security implications

The lesson of Case 30 is: TLE parameters are not perfect physical quantities; they may absorb thrust, attitude, and fitting errors.

This reminds us that space situational awareness (SSA) cannot rely on a single parameter, but must build an interpretive framework that is multi-source, multi-channel, and capable of cross-validation.
