# Chapter 6　Tracking Gaps, Data Quality, and False Alarms

> *This English edition is an AI-assisted translation of the Traditional Chinese manuscript (v1.2, 2026-11-15). Where they differ, the Chinese original prevails.*

## Chapter overview

In December 2018, Spaceflight Industries chartered an entire SpaceX Falcon 9 for a mission called SSO-A, sending 64 satellites into orbit at once. Nearly 50 of them were similar-looking CubeSats, released one after another within a short period.

A swarm of new objects appeared in orbit; the hard part was attaching a name to each one. Operators relied on U.S. Air Force tracking data to find their own satellites, while the Air Force relied on operators to help with identification. According to The Verge, more than a dozen still could not be identified four months after launch. In late April 2019, Spaceflight said that 19 of the 64 were still awaiting identification and another 4 were unaccounted for. Tracking experts criticized the release of so many small satellites in such a short time as inherently hard to tell apart.

The orbits were already in the catalog, but the names had not yet been matched. If a set of TLEs is attached to the wrong number, no matter how accurate the downstream detection is, A's actions will be credited to B. Before we trust an orbital curve, what questions must we ask first?

A sudden jump in an orbital curve does not necessarily mean a satellite fired its thrusters. Before interpreting it, the analyst must first confirm several things: Are the data continuous? Can the two records be compared directly? Is what is on the screen the complete history, or a fragment that only began accumulating after some import? Using tracking gaps and database health checks, this chapter explains how data conditions affect the credibility of alerts.¹

The core principle of this chapter is: **first confirm what judgments the data can support, then decide what conclusions the algorithm should output.** The discussion below separates three kinds of content: recorded research results, engineering recommendations, and data snapshots that still need confirmation.

## Learning goals

- Distinguish observation gaps, publication delays, import interruptions, and un-backfilled history.
- Understand the difference between adjacent-TLE differences and comparisons propagated to a common epoch.
- Manage data-quality information separately from maneuver-detection scores.
- Use Case 8 to understand catalog expansion and changes in the data population.
- Preserve uncertainty when data are insufficient, rather than directly declaring normal or anomalous.

## Gaps have different sources

This book recommends splitting "no data" into four situations. This is an engineering interpretation framework; it does not mean the current system has implemented every category.

| Gap type | Evidence to check | Conclusions not to jump to |
|---|---|---|
| Insufficient observation or orbit-determination updates | Upstream publication records, intervals between adjacent epochs | The satellite has no activity |
| Publication delay | Data epoch and publication time | The latest downloaded data reflect the latest state |
| Import interruption | Download, parsing, and ingestion logs | Upstream published no data |
| History not backfilled | First ingestion time, historical query range | No orbital records existed before the satellite was ingested |

The purpose of the classification is to avoid handing blanks caused by different reasons to the same suppression rule. For example, if the data entry point is interrupted, the pipeline should be repaired first; if observation updates are insufficient, the timeliness limitation should be flagged on the interpretation side. Both can make the display stop updating, but they call for different responses.

## From differences to comparable signals

The first FORMOSAT-7/COSMIC-2 satellite once had a TLE gap of about 134 to 163 hours, and the jump in the first record after the gap was misclassified as a maneuver by the initial version of the rules. This study's response was to flag data with intervals longer than 48 hours as questionable and to lower the weight of the first record after a gap. This is a setting of the research system, not a standard applicable to all orbital regimes.¹

How long intervals affect results depends first on which kind of comparison is used:

| Comparison method | Issues to watch |
|---|---|
| Subtracting the mean elements of adjacent TLEs | Different intervals, accumulated natural perturbations, and fit updates are mixed in the difference |
| Propagating two TLEs to a common epoch | Propagation span and model error may affect position and velocity residuals |
| Comparing trends over a period | Trend estimates may be affected by missing values, outliers, and uneven sampling |

Therefore, this book does not uniformly call every post-gap jump a "propagation error." The method used to compute the signal should be stated first, and then the possible causes analyzed; when no propagation comparison has been done, it should not be implied that every difference is a propagation result.

## Update frequency needs an attached time window

In this study's sample, NAVSTAR 59 averaged about 1.3 TLEs per day, STARLINK-30805 about 2.3, and the International Space Station (ISS) about 3.4; STARLINK-30805 reached as many as 12 in a single day during frequent maneuvering. These values belong to a specific research data window and should not be written as fixed update frequencies of the satellites.¹

In engineering terms, it is recommended to report the number of records, the number of days covered, the median interval between adjacent epochs, and the proportion of long gaps together. Looking only at the daily average may mask a situation of "no data for several consecutive days and dense updates the rest of the time."

Frequent updates also do not automatically mean the information is independent, more accurate, or able to resolve every burn. Case 3 in Chapter 3 discusses the visibility of orbit raising and station-keeping under different sampling conditions and can serve as further reading for this section; this chapter does not restate it in full.²

## Separating quality from event scores

Whether the data are reliable and whether the behavior is anomalous are two different questions. This book recommends storing "data-quality status" and "event score" separately, so that low-quality data do not gain excessive confidence in a judgment because of a jump in values.

The following are suggested design fields; this does not claim that the current program has fully adopted this naming:

| Field | Suggested use |
|---|---|
| `epoch_gap_hours` | Records the interval between adjacent epochs |
| `data_age_hours` | Records how long the analysis time is from the latest data epoch |
| `ingest_status` | Records whether download, parsing, and ingestion succeeded |
| `history_coverage` | Records the available historical range and the first ingestion date |
| `quality_reason` | Records the reason for down-weighting, deferral, or manual review |

When quality is poor, the signal need not be deleted. Candidate events can be kept while lowering the strength of evidence and noting the limitations, striking a balance between suppressing false alarms and avoiding masking real maneuvers.

### Why the database seemed to cover only a few months

Case 8 began with a user's question: the data in the interface seemed to start only from March of a certain year; was it because the yearly Parquet files (data files split by year) were not enabled? The investigation pointed to a catalog expansion: a large number of objects were brought into tracking for the first time in the same phase, so the historical starting points of many satellites in the local database clustered together.³

What this case illustrates is not the volume of data, but this: **the build history of a database changes the satellite history we see.** First appearing in the local database does not equal first being observed externally, let alone the launch date.

**Sidebar: Whose rocket hit the Moon?**

In January 2022, Bill Gray identified the object designated WE0913A as the Falcon 9 second stage that launched DSCOVR in 2015, and predicted it would hit the Moon on March 4. On February 12, based on an email from a NASA JPL engineer, he publicly corrected this, reidentifying it as the upper stage of the Long March 3C that launched Chang'e 5-T1 in 2014. China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs denied it, saying that stage had long since reentered; according to SpaceNews, the U.S. Space Force said the "reentry" in the catalog was only an extrapolation, not actual tracking data. Identities and statuses in the catalog also have their own build history.

### Snapshot numbers must be time-stamped first

This study's database snapshot lists 34,823 tracked objects and 24,287,788 TLEs, with the earliest data date 1971-04-30 and the latest 2026-11-17. These are numbers from a specific data snapshot and do not represent the state of the database at other times.³

The Case 8 page of this project's case collection notes that the page's numbers are computed by the program through live queries of the local or remote TLE database, not pre-written text. In other words, this set of numbers corresponds to one particular query, not a fixed property of the database.

### Catalog expansion and historical backfill

In March–April 2026, a total of 29,494 objects were ingested for the first time, about 85% of the snapshot's total; in addition, the ISS had been backfilled to 1998-11-20, with 47,802 records in that snapshot. Each of these numbers has its own snapshot and statistical basis and should not be regarded as fixed properties of the database.³

Newly ingested objects are not uniformly unable to obtain pre-ingestion history; the ISS backfill is an example: **routine ingestion usually starts accumulating after tracking is enabled; whether earlier history can be backfilled depends on upstream retention, access rights, and backfill procedures, and cannot be determined solely from the local ingestion starting point.**³

**Sidebar: USA 193 could only be tracked externally**

The U.S. National Reconnaissance Office's USA 193 was launched in December 2006 and lost contact soon after reaching orbit. Its orbit and lifetime could only be estimated through external tracking: the estimate in early December 2007 was natural reentry about three months later. On February 20, 2008 (U.S. Eastern Time), USS Lake Erie fired a modified SM-3 missile and hit it at an altitude of about 240 kilometers; the United States said the reason was that the roughly 450 kilograms of hydrazine on board might survive reentry. Classification also limited the analysis: personnel without security clearances could only do parametric analysis. Who holds the data and who can access it determines who can answer the questions.

### Recommended health-check sequence

For the problems represented by Case 8, this book recommends confirming the following in order:

1. Whether the interface applies date, object-category, or record-count filters.
2. Whether the query targets the complete history table, or a rolling window or cache.
3. When the object was first brought into local tracking.
4. Whether earlier data exist upstream, and whether the backfill succeeded.
5. Whether the storage partitioning strategy actually affects queries, rather than only affecting file organization.
6. Whether the catalog expansion changed the statistical population, so that the periods before and after cannot be compared directly.

These are operational recommendations extended from the case, not the results of an actual database audit.

**Sidebar: Why Starlink's avoidance count doubled**

SpaceX reported to the U.S. Federal Communications Commission (FCC) that Starlink performed 13,612 propulsive maneuvers from June to November 2022; in the next period (to May 2023), the number rose to 25,299. SpaceX's maneuver threshold is a probability of collision greater than 10⁻⁵, an order of magnitude stricter than the 10⁻⁴ commonly used in the industry. The 2024 period approached 50,000, and researcher Hugh Lewis pointed out that this doubling came mainly from SpaceX lowering its threshold. The count is affected simultaneously by constellation size, debris events, and the threshold, so before comparing across periods, one must first confirm that the basis is consistent.

## From data quality to decision quality

This study lists operations and maintenance issues such as TLE import interruptions, database rebuilds, fail-stop, and backfilling of delayed data, and treats them as part of system resilience.¹

In an operational system, "fail-stop" should be explicitly limited to the affected tasks or conclusions: when data are anomalous, stop issuing new high-confidence alerts while preserving logs, displaying the stale status, and notifying maintenance personnel. Stopping output should not be described as always safer; without alternative monitoring and fault notification, a silent system can also create risk.

It is recommended that every event card delivered to analysts list the latest data time, the gap status, how the signal was computed, and the review requirements. Only then can analysts tell whether the change in front of them comes from satellite behavior or from the observation and data process.

**Sidebar: The fireball over Michigan**

At about 04:43 (UT) on October 20, 2021, a fireball lasting several minutes appeared over the U.S. Midwest and Michigan, and the American Meteor Society later received more than 150 sighting reports. Astrophysicist Jonathan McDowell pointed out that the fireball appeared exactly at the predicted passage time of the Russian reconnaissance satellite Kosmos 2551, and also fell within the reentry uncertainty window given by the U.S. Space Force. This satellite never adjusted its orbit after reaching orbit and reentered about 41 days later. A public catalog plus predictions with attached time windows were enough to match ground sightings to a specific object.
