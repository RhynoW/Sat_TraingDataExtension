# Chapter 9　The Statistical Layer: Judging "How Unusual"

> *This English edition is an AI-assisted translation of the Traditional Chinese manuscript (v1.2, 2026-11-15). Where they differ, the Chinese original prevails.*

## Chapter overview

In the spring of 2015, a Russian satellite moved into geosynchronous orbit between two international communication satellites, Intelsat 7 and Intelsat 901. It was called Luch, also known as Olymp-K, and had been launched only the previous September. Kay Sears, then president of Intelsat General, said: "This is not normal behavior and we're concerned."

That was only the beginning. According to a compilation by the U.S. think tank CSIS, between July 2017 and December 2020 it stayed at no fewer than 16 different orbital positions, becoming in turn the nearest neighbor of about 30 satellites. In September 2018, French Minister of the Armed Forces Florence Parly publicly charged that it had approached Athena-Fidus, a French-Italian military communication satellite, calling it "an act of espionage."

In geosynchronous orbit, a satellite's position can almost be condensed into a single longitude. Stop, drift, stop again: each is just a bend in the longitude curve. On a curve spanning several years with bend after bend, which bend counts as truly "unusual"?

The statistical layer is not responsible for declaring that a satellite "definitely maneuvered." What it must answer is: measured against the satellite's own history, similar satellites, and known physical conditions, how unusual is this change? This study uses CUSUM, BOCPD, singular spectrum analysis, and robust MAD as the main statistical channels, and expands the signal sources with the phase residual of Case 21.¹

The statistical layer must be interpreted together with data quality, rule suppression, physical residuals, and fusion decisions; a single statistical score cannot replace the complete chain of evidence.

## Learning goals

- Distinguish statistical anomalies, physical anomalies, and mission anomalies.
- Understand the different uses of CUSUM, BOCPD, SSA, and robust MAD.
- Understand why the phase residual can supplement the semi-major-axis signal.
- Use Case 21 to understand methodological errors and self-correction.
- Use Case 27 to understand the teaching and debugging value of record-by-record animation.
- Avoid interpreting statistical scores directly as calibrated event probabilities.

## What the statistical layer asks

The statistical layer does not presuppose what a maneuver looks like; what it asks is:

> **How far does this record deviate from its own historical behavior and from the behavior of its peers?**¹

Before answering, the baseline for comparison must be confirmed. The same semi-major-axis change may mean something entirely different for a satellite that has been stable for a long time, a newly deployed satellite, or a satellite continuously compensating for drag.

This book recommends that the statistical layer distinguish at least three baselines:

| Baseline | Compared against | Question it is suited to answer |
|---|---|---|
| Own history | Recent behavior of the same satellite | Does it deviate from its own past normal? |
| Peer population | Satellites in the same altitude band or of the same type | Does it deviate from the population's typical behavior? |
| Physical expectation | Drag, perturbation, or mission models | Can known physical processes explain the change? |

None of the three baselines equals ground truth. Own history may be too short, a population may mix in different missions, and a physical model may lose accuracy because of errors in attitude, density, or parameters.

**Sidebar: Cerise's broken boom in 1996**

On July 24, 1996, the French microsatellite Cerise, about 50 kilograms, at an altitude of about 670 kilometers, had its gravity-gradient stabilization boom severed by a cataloged debris fragment at a relative velocity of about 14.8 kilometers per second; the fragment came from the Ariane rocket body that had launched SPOT 1. The U.S. Space Surveillance Network first discovered a new object in the catalog; after analysis, NASA queried SSTL, whose telemetry confirmed that the satellite's attitude had changed suddenly at that time; the severed boom segment was cataloged as 23994. This event, widely regarded as the first confirmed collision between cataloged objects, was established through an operator-side anomaly and a new catalog object, not through TLE statistics. Looking back with this book's methods, once the configuration changed, the "own history" baseline also became invalid.

## Four main channels

This study uses four main statistical methods: CUSUM, BOCPD, SSA, and 3σ-MAD. Each has a different mathematical definition of a "sudden change in the signal," and when used alone, it is difficult to achieve both recall and false-alarm rate at once.¹

| Method | Core concept | Situations it is suited to capture | Main limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| CUSUM | Cumulative deviation.| Small, persistent shifts. | Sensitive to trends and the choice of baseline. |
| BOCPD | Bayesian change-point detection.| Changes in state or distribution. | A posterior does not equal a confirmed event. |
| SSA | Singular spectrum analysis.| Separating trend from residual. | Window length and reconstruction choices affect results. |
| Robust MAD | Thresholding by median absolute deviation.| Degree of outlyingness. | Sensitive to sample size and time intervals.¹ |

These four methods are complementary: CUSUM accumulates persistent deviation, BOCPD estimates the likelihood of a change point, SSA separates out trends, and MAD provides a robust scale of outlyingness. Placing them side by side gives signals of different shapes a chance to be seen; it is not about having the four channels vote.

**Sidebar: The longitude footprint of Shijian-17**

Shijian-17 (SJ-17), which entered geosynchronous orbit in November 2016, according to data from the commercial tracking company AGI, repeatedly stopped, drifted, and stopped again over the following year and a half: first operating in close proximity to ChinaSat 5A at 163 degrees east until the end of December; in the summer of 2017 it parked next to ChinaSat 6A; and in February 2018 it rendezvoused with ChinaSat 20, coming within less than 1 kilometer on several occasions. The official explanation is technology verification; other purposes are only outside speculation. This longitude series can be cut into several segments, and the start of each segment is exactly the position that change-point methods such as CUSUM and BOCPD are designed to find.

## A score is not a probability

No single statistical method was sufficient to reach the threshold of a large-event recall above 90%, hence the need for fusion. This is a conclusion under specific evaluation conditions and should not be extrapolated into a universal ceiling for all statistical methods.¹

This book recommends that the output of the statistical layer be labeled as:

- Anomaly score.
- Change-point likelihood or posterior quantity.
- Trend or residual quantity.
- Degree of outlyingness.
- Trigger threshold and version.

To call the output a "maneuver probability," calibration and validation against the deployment distribution must be completed first. Until then, a score of 0.9 only indicates that the model or statistic considers the event worth priority review; it does not mean the true event probability is 90%.

### Why the semi-major axis is not enough

Case 21 started from the discovery that some maneuvers are not evident in the semi-major-axis signal but may leave traces in the orbital phase. This study uses the argument-of-latitude phase-residual method as a detection channel beyond the semi-major axis. The argument of latitude is the satellite's angular position relative to the ascending node; Case 21 explains that for near-circular orbits it is more stable than the mean anomaly alone.²

This idea expands the physical quantities that can be observed. The semi-major axis reflects orbital energy and altitude, while the phase reflects the relationship between the satellite's position in its orbit and time; the two may have different sensitivities to different types of maneuvers. Case 21 itself also explains that this channel, used independently, still performs far below existing methods; it is suitable only as a supplementary reference, not as a standalone determination channel.

In validation on 23 benchmark satellites, this channel's mean F1 was 0.102. The benchmark ground truth consists mainly of conventional orbit-maintenance and collision-avoidance maneuvers, and the "phase adjustment with unchanged semi-major axis" scenario this channel targets is not among them; this F1 therefore measures the channel's sensitivity to general maneuvers, not its detection power for the scenario it was designed for.²

### The value of methodological errors

Case 21 went through one methodological error that was self-corrected, as well as two broad full-LEO scans. This process is worth preserving: building a statistical signal requires repeatedly checking definitions, time windows, comparison baselines, and data conditions; writing the formula is only the beginning.²

The two full-LEO scans differ in scale: the short window took the most recent 15 days, covering 28,005 satellites and 722,209 TLEs; the long window covered about 20 months from 2025-01-01, covering 29,128 satellites and 12,509,231 TLEs. Each scan uses the noise of each satellite within its own time window as the baseline, so the baselines for determining "anomalous" differ and the results should not be compared directly; neither has ground truth, and both are only candidate censuses.²

Geomagnetic-storm sensitivity was also revised. Case 21 switched to comparing the same set of 140 Starlink satellites with the same method: the trigger rate in the storm week of 2026-04-15 to 04-21 was 75.0% (105/140), and in the quiet control week 6.4% (9/140); but for the stronger storm of 2026-07-04, the trigger rate was only 1.4% (2/138), no different from the quiet week. The 6 FORMOSAT-7/COSMIC-2 satellites likewise responded only to the 2026-04-18 event. Taking the two constellations together, the evidence leans toward some special event at the data-processing or cataloging level on that day, though whether this is so has not yet been directly verified; until then, geomagnetic-storm sensitivity should be regarded as questionable.²

When interpreting such scan results, at least the following fields must be confirmed.

| Field to know | Why it is necessary |
|---|---|
| Definition of the phase residual | Avoid mixing different phase quantities into the same signal. |
| Time window and sampling conditions | Avoid directly comparing results from different periods. |
| Sample size and coverage | Avoid treating scan results as conclusions about the whole population. |
| Threshold and version | Avoid merging results from different versions. |
| Triggered and untriggered events | Avoid looking only at hits. |
| External validation or ground-truth comparison | Avoid self-validation using one's own detection results. |

### "Deliberate evasion" cannot be inferred directly

Case 21 once discussed "whether this is deliberate evasion of detection." This question cannot be treated as confirmed hostile behavior: a statistical anomaly can only support further investigation; it cannot by itself prove intent.²

A phase anomaly may come from a maneuver, data error, differences in orbit propagation, population behavior, or unmodeled perturbations. Discussing intent requires combining mission context, operational history, external intelligence, and multiple pieces of evidence; this is a decision problem belonging to Chapter 19, and this chapter cannot make this judgment on its own.

**Sidebar: Kosmos 2499, first cataloged as debris**

In May 2014, a Rockot launch vehicle carried, in addition to the three announced Russian military communication satellites, one more object. U.S. trackers first cataloged it as debris and only at the end of October reclassified it as a payload, named Kosmos 2499. It subsequently approached the rocket's upper stage twice, coming as close as about 0.53 kilometers; it maneuvered again in early 2017; and in January 2023 it broke up, with the U.S. Space Force tracking 85 fragments. Its changes in behavior were observed again and again, but its purpose remains only outside speculation. During the months when it was treated as debris, any rule assuming "debris does not move" would have skipped it.

### National security implications

From Case 21 we can conclude:

> **Not seeing it in the semi-major axis does not mean there was no maneuver; but an anomalous phase residual does not equal a confirmed maneuver either.**²

National security applications therefore cannot rely on a single orbital element. New channels must first pass methodological checks, and failing to trigger a threshold should also be recorded as a valid result; statistical anomalies must retain their uncertainty, and intent, even more so, cannot be inferred from a single signal.

### What the animation is for

Case 27 uses a record-by-record animation to show how the L1 rule gate, the L2 statistical channels, and the L3 fusion decision change as TLEs enter one by one. It is positioned as a visualization case that makes the detection methods "come alive."³

The animation's default comparison pair is FORMOSAT-5 (NORAD 42920, mean altitude about 723 kilometers, 450 TLEs) and STARLINK-3005 (NORAD 48881, about 563 kilometers, 278 TLEs). The former is in a higher orbit with small atmospheric disturbances, and the semi-major-axis changes caused by maneuvers are far above the threshold; the latter is in low Earth orbit, where electric propulsion and atmospheric drag coexist, the noise floor is high, and changes often sit close to the threshold, making it a hard low-Earth-orbit case.³

The animation cannot replace performance evaluation; what it is useful for is letting analysts see:

- Which record triggered which rule.
- Which statistical channel showed an anomaly first.
- Which suppression conditions were activated.
- How the fusion score accumulates with the evidence.
- Which events ultimately did not trigger the threshold.

Case 27 in this project's case collection lists several further limitations. The page does not overlay maneuver ground truth, so one cannot judge which method is more accurate from the number of lit indicators; SSA and MAD use statistics of the entire sequence, and the animation's "step-by-step reveal" displays precomputed scores in time order rather than being a strict record-by-record recomputation. To keep the web page small, the sequences were thinned of near-duplicate epochs (minimum interval 12 hours), taking at most the most recent 450 records; the database is a rolling window, and the actual length of the observation window varies by satellite. Each channel's events use the program's built-in determination, without tuning parameters for any single satellite, and more lit indicators do not mean better.

### L1/L3 are only upstream and downstream hints

L1 and L3 in the Case 27 animation serve only as upstream and downstream hints; this visualization tool must not be misunderstood as a fully validated decision system.⁴

The on-page L3 score table also shows this. For non-Starlink satellites, the L3 event-level F1 is about 0.43 (leave-one-satellite-out average), and even on altimetry satellites from the same distribution there is a considerable proportion of false alarms and missed detections; on the out-of-sample 6 FORMOSAT-7 satellites (weak ground truth), the L3 F1 is 0.19, actually lower than the L1 rules' 0.23. FORMOSAT-7 is at about 550 kilometers, in conditions closer to Starlink, and L3 generalizes less well to such low-Earth-orbit satellites than to higher-orbit altimetry satellites.³

The animation helps in understanding the workflow, but it cannot prove that:

- The rule thresholds have been optimized.
- The statistical channels have been correctly calibrated.
- The fusion model has ruled out label leakage.
- The system has passed deployment validation.
- Untriggered events definitely contain no maneuver.

### National security implications

From Case 27 we can conclude:

> **Visualization is a tool for understanding, not a tool for validation.**³

When used in national security settings, the interface must show data sources and versions, the animation must indicate the computation method and time window, untriggered events should still be queryable, and analysts must be able to trace the source of every score; visualization cannot replace regression testing.

## The division of labor between the statistical layer and other layers

The statistical layer does not replace the fusion layer, nor is it subordinate to the rule layer. It provides a quantified degree of anomaly; the rule layer provides explainable candidates and suppression reasons; the fusion layer integrates multiple channels; physical and applicability handling checks whether the change can be explained by drag or reentry; and whether the data are sufficient for a judgment is left to the quality gate. A table of the division of labor among the layers is in Chapter 7.

**Sidebar: Intelsat 33e's last "unusual" moment**

Intelsat 33e had held its station at 60 degrees east since January 2017. On October 19, 2024, it suffered an anomaly, lost power, and then broke up; Intelsat declared it a total loss two days later. The number of fragments varies by observer: the U.S. Space Force reported about 20, the commercial company ExoAnalytic first identified 57 and later reportedly more than 500, and the Russian side said more than 80. When a satellite that had long held station suddenly stopped holding station, statistics on the longitude series were enough to flag "this is unusual"; but whether it was a maneuver, a loss of control, or a breakup has to be answered by other layers and other sensors.

This is this book's recommended engineering division of labor; it does not mean every system must execute in the same order. An actual pipeline can compute in parallel or be adjusted to deployment conditions.
