# Chapter 11　Physics as a Third-Party Witness: NRLMSIS and Drag Residuals

> *This English edition is an AI-assisted translation of the Traditional Chinese manuscript (v1.3, 2026-11-19). Where they differ, the Chinese original prevails.*

## Chapter overview

From February 3 to 4, 2022, a geomagnetic storm swept past Earth. At an altitude of about 438 kilometers, the European Space Agency's Swarm-A satellite measured a 110% increase in the surrounding atmospheric mass density during the main phase; higher up, at about 505 kilometers, GRACE-FO measured 120%. The air density became more than twice its usual value, and the drag on satellites increased sharply as well.

Afterward, He and colleagues, 13 researchers in all, reran this storm with six atmospheric models, some empirical formulas and some first-principles models computed from physical equations. The density enhancements simulated by the six models differed from one another by as much as 70%.

When we see from TLEs that a satellite is falling faster than expected, we are used to saying, "The drag residual has grown." But if the "expectation" itself may be off by as much as 70%, how much of this residual is due to something the satellite did, and how much is due to our atmospheric model getting it wrong?

The physical model does not reach the final conclusion on behalf of the AI; it provides a set of checkable assumptions: given known altitude, latitude, solar activity, and geomagnetic conditions, how much orbital decay atmospheric drag ought to cause. This study uses NRLMSIS (a semi-empirical atmospheric density model) as the basis for computing drag residuals, and uses Case 10 to faithfully record the experience of failing to meet the goal of "inverting atmospheric density from TLEs alone."¹

This chapter treats the physical model as a third-party witness, not a final judge. It helps the system subtract known disturbances, but it cannot replace data quality, mission context, and human review.

## Learning goals

- Understand the role of NRLMSIS in drag residuals.
- Distinguish using a density model from inverting the true density.
- Understand the four versions and three root causes in Case 10.
- Use a negative result to understand the information limit of TLEs.
- Avoid treating the physical model as a cure-all.

## What the physical model is asking

What the physical model must answer is:

> How much of this descent should have happened anyway?¹

Before answering, the inputs must be confirmed. This study uses NRLMSIS as the basis for computing drag residuals and made two improvements: upgrading the Ap index from daily means to ap(7) at the standard 3-hour resolution, and changing the latitude and longitude used in the density calculation from a fixed 0/0 to the actual sub-satellite point coordinates solved by SGP4.¹ According to the final report, the current drag residual is computed with NRLMSIS 2.1: F10.7 uses the observed value and the 81-day centered average on the UTC day of the TLE epoch, without intraday interpolation, with 120 sfu substituted when missing; ap(7) uses the storm-time ap mode, falling back to the daily mean when 3-hour values are unavailable; for near-circular orbits, density is further replaced with the hourly orbit-averaged value; and the effective ballistic coefficient B_eff is calibrated as the median of the ratios over all adjacent transitions in the query period.⁴

These improvements allow the model to better reflect the space environment the satellite is actually in, but they do not mean the model is perfect. The density model is still affected by solar activity, geomagnetic disturbances, attitude, and parameter errors: comparing 265 passive satellites, the final report found that when the 81-day mean F10.7 exceeds 160 sfu, the NRLMSIS "expected/observed" ratio is about 17% higher than in low-activity periods (median 1.17), and FORMOSAT-7 precise orbits likewise show the model running about 20% high at solar maximum; the quantitative effect of the 3-hour ap and sub-satellite coordinates on density and residual accuracy during geomagnetic storms has not yet been validated.⁴

**Sidebar: The origins of NRLMSIS 2.0**

The final report states that the system in this study uses NRLMSIS 2.1; the NRLMSIS 2.0 series to which it belongs was developed by Emmert et al.; the code was released in 2020, and the paper was formally published in *Earth and Space Science* only in March 2021. It is a reconstruction of NRLMSISE-00, extending from the ground to the base of the exosphere and outputting temperature, densities of 8 species, and total mass density. The authors state in the abstract that new in situ measurements are still needed in the future, as is reconciling systematic biases among the various datasets. In other words, what it gives is an average state generalized from past observations, not the true density on a given day.

## Drag residuals: subtract the known, leave the suspicious

The drag residual is computed as follows:


$$
\text{drag residual} =
\text{observed orbital change}
-
\text{change due to NRLMSIS expected drag}
$$


A residual close to zero means the orbital descent can be explained by atmospheric drag; a residual clearly departing from zero may indicate a maneuver, a propulsion anomaly, data error, or other unknown factors.¹ According to the final report, the expected change is computed as the NRLMSIS density multiplied by the per-satellite calibrated effective ballistic coefficient B_eff, and a |residual| exceeding 0.3 km is judged "not explainable by drag"; because the drag coefficient, attitude, and density-model errors cannot be fully separated, the determination distinguishes only between "explainable by drag" and "not explainable by drag" and is not directly equated with a propulsive burn. For example, FORMOSAT-3 (792 km, no propulsion) had a maximum residual of 0.105 km over the whole period, while a Starlink satellite performing an orbit raise had a residual of 1.91 km.⁴

**Sidebar: The 2003 Halloween storms**

During the severe geomagnetic storms from October 29 to November 1, 2003, the CHAMP satellite at about 410 kilometers measured densities about 200% to 300% higher at peak than in quiet conditions; Sutton et al. compared the observations with NRLMSISE-00 in 2005 and pointed out some shortcomings in the model. In another storm on November 20 to 21 of the same year, CHAMP and GRACE measured increases of 300% to 800% at 400 to 500 kilometers. When density multiplies within a few days, empirical models cannot keep up; drag residuals at such times cannot be read directly as maneuvers. The system design in the final report is this: when all satellites descend together during a geomagnetic storm, the drag residual is used to judge whether the change can be explained by drag, avoiding misclassification as a maneuver, but the quantitative effect of the 3-hour ap input on residual accuracy during storms has not yet been validated.⁴

This book recommends that drag residual output be labeled with:

- The residual magnitude and time window.
- The version of the density model used in the calculation.
- Solar activity and geomagnetic indices.
- Sub-satellite point coordinates and sampling conditions.
- Known failure scenarios.
- Recommended follow-up data for review.

With these fields, an analyst can reconstruct the reasoning process instead of seeing only a red flag.

### Positioning of the case

Case 10 asks: can atmospheric density be inverted from TLEs alone? Through four versions, each attempt more rigorous than the last, it faithfully gives the conclusion: it cannot be done, and it explains why. The reference for Case 10 is the research of a team at Kyoto University in Japan (Yamamoto, 2026, *Earth, Planets and Space*, 78, Article 175): that team used precise ephemerides from about 1,200 Starlink satellites to invert the 2D distribution of thermospheric density as it varies with latitude and local time; "the Kyoto University method" and "Kyoto University–level" below refer to this research. Case 10 notes that its figures come from offline analysis output and record an attempt that failed to replicate the target result.²

This book treats Case 10 as a case illustrating the limits of the physical model, not as a complete validation of the whole system. The physical model's performance must be interpreted together with data quality, rule suppression, the statistical layer, the fusion layer, and the reentry gate.

The failure of Case 10 lies in "inverting density from TLEs"; using NRLMSIS to predict natural decay is a different problem. The final report validated it on 425 satellites with perigees of 300–1000 km: the correlation coefficient between TLE-measured decay rates and NRLMSIS predictions is 0.976, and the median per-satellite temporal correlation between decay-rate fluctuations and solar activity for satellites passive over the whole period is 0.923; validation with FORMOSAT-7 precise orbits gives a correlation coefficient of 0.962, and the correlation between TLE and precise-orbit 7-day-window decay rates is 0.996. Because B_eff is calibrated for each satellite with the whole-period median, the agreement in magnitude is partly a result of the calibration; when the decay rate is below about 1 m/day (above about 850 km), the measured change approaches TLE noise; and satellites with actively controlled orbits are also not suited to this per-satellite calibration.⁴

### The four versions

Case 10 tried four versions:

1. TLE-only version: select satellites whose orbital altitude declines monotonically and that are in the final stage of decay (584 in total), and, after calibration with the NRLMSIS atmospheric model, invert the profile of density versus altitude from their decay rates.
2. MEME precise-ephemeris version: switch to precise position/velocity data (MEME) for 283 Starlink satellites, and use the "specific-energy method" (inferring the magnitude of drag from the rate at which orbital energy is lost over time) to estimate atmospheric density directly, avoiding the fitting errors of the TLE-only version. MEME is SpaceX's own force model and predictions, not measured truth (see Chapter 5).
3. Per-satellite self-calibration version: split the data into 924 drag arcs and calibrate the "ballistic coefficient" (a coefficient describing how sensitive a satellite's shape and mass make it to drag) separately for each satellite, in an attempt to suppress the noise caused by uncertainty in attitude and shape.
4. Version faithfully replicating the Kyoto University method: further switch to the EGM96 degree-12 gravity field (an Earth gravity model more accurate than typical simplified models) to compute specific energy, staying as close as possible to the Kyoto University team's method settings, ultimately yielding density comparison results for 151 "clean" drag arcs.²

These four versions are not four repetitions of the same experiment, but attempts that progressively changed the input data, calibration method, and method settings. Therefore, the figures from one version cannot be taken directly as the results of all versions.

Among the 151 drag arcs of version 4, the median ratio of inverted density to NRLMSIS model density is 1.00, indicating no overall systematic bias; but the interquartile range of the ratio is 0.39–1.24, meaning that at the same altitude the inverted density may be only about 40% of the model value or as much as about 1.2 times it. This scatter is direct evidence that the Kyoto University result could not be cleanly replicated; note that this ratio is a comparison only with NRLMSIS model density, not with measured truth.²

### Three root causes

Case 10 identifies three root causes:

1. The sample itself is biased: the satellites that can be selected as "orbital altitude declining monotonically, clean enough for inversion" are almost all end-of-life, deorbiting satellites with uncontrolled attitude. For these satellites, orbital decay is dominated by "changes in effective drag area caused by tumbling attitude," not by changes in the true atmospheric density.
2. B\* circular reasoning: the ballistic coefficient B\* in a TLE is itself a parameter inferred from the orbital decay rate. Using B\* to divide the decay rate in order to estimate density amounts to "dividing the answer by the answer," and part of the density signal cancels itself out in the calculation.
3. Orbital coverage is too sparse, and TLEs themselves are noisy: Starlink's main shells are concentrated at about 53° inclination, and there are only about 13 polar-orbiting satellites. On the 2D "latitude × local time" grid, many cells have almost no data, and spatial sampling is severely insufficient; in addition, TLE position/velocity errors typically reach hundreds of meters to kilometers, so the spatial correlation coefficient of the final inverted density field is only r = 0.42 (0 means no correlation, 1 means perfect agreement).²

These three root causes are intertwined: sample bias affects calibration, B\* circular reasoning affects the signal, and insufficient coverage affects spatial reconstruction. Therefore, none of them can be treated as the sole cause.

**Sidebar: UARS slowed down on its last day**

In September 2011, the U.S. Upper Atmosphere Research Satellite (UARS), about 5.7 metric tons, was heading for an uncontrolled reentry. On September 22, NASA predicted it would reenter around 20:36 UTC on the 23rd, with an uncertainty of ±20 hours; the next day's update, however, said that solar activity was no longer the main factor in the rate of descent, and that the satellite's attitude or configuration seemed to have changed, slowing its descent. It finally reentered over the Pacific around 04:00 UTC on the 24th. When attitude changes, the effective drag area changes with it; this is the same principle as root cause 1: decay does not necessarily reflect density.

### The value of negative results

Case 10 points out that even after switching the input data to precise ephemerides and self-calibrating the ballistic coefficient per satellite, the density estimate for each drag arc remained stuck at a scatter of about 0.62 dex (dex is a unit on a base-10 logarithmic scale; 0.62 dex means fluctuation up or down by a factor of about 4), with almost no noticeable improvement before versus after calibration. Case 10 concludes from this that the bottleneck lies not only in the three root causes specific to TLEs but also in the precision limit of the single specific-energy method itself.²

This negative result records the failure as it is, without pretending the problem has been solved. Case 10 also proposes feasible next steps:

1. Compute accelerations directly from SpaceX's public precise ephemerides: avoid the circular reasoning of B\* in TLEs and estimate drag acceleration directly from position/velocity time series (Case 10 notes that this project has partially pursued this route).
2. For operational satellites, extract "pure-drag quiet periods": between two station-keeping maneuvers, select arcs with almost no thrust interference to recalibrate the ballistic coefficient, reducing signal aliasing caused by attitude and thrust.
3. Lower the goal, from 2D tomography to a global-mean indicator: instead of insisting on reconstructing Kyoto University–level 2D density tomography over latitude × local time, first build a qualitative indicator of "global mean density versus solar activity" as auxiliary validation data for existing atmospheric models.
4. To truly reproduce Kyoto University–level results: a POD (precise orbit determination)–level point-by-point drag acceleration inversion method is needed, together with a more rigorous error model and data assimilation, which is beyond the reach of this project's current "simplified specific-energy method" route.²

**Sidebar: How the U.S. military inverts density from orbits**

When the U.S. military maintains its satellite catalog, it relies on the HASDM data assimilation system: it continuously inverts atmospheric density from dozens of calibration satellites and then corrects the current JB2008 model with temperature correction coefficients, which are not public. In 2021, Space Environment Technologies, with approval from the U.S. Space Force, publicly released the reconstructed "SET HASDM density database," covering global densities every 3 hours from 2000 to 2019. Carefully selected calibration satellites and non-public correction coefficients are precisely the inputs that users with only public TLEs cannot obtain.

### National security implications

The lesson of Case 10 is:

> TLEs are not suitable for high-precision atmospheric density inversion; the method itself also has an information limit.²

When using a physical model, one cannot push TLEs beyond their design range, nor treat the physical model as a cure-all. Sample bias and insufficient coverage must be checked first, negative results must be kept, and model output cannot be treated as ground truth.

## Division of labor between the physical model and other layers

The physical model does not replace the rule layer, nor is it subordinate to the statistical layer; it provides checkable assumptions. According to the final report: in the R7 single-satellite report, the drag residual is one of the auxiliary channels; a |residual| exceeding 0.3 km within 24 hours before or after an event is counted, affecting only the evidence grade; it does not generate candidate events on its own and cannot raise an event of a non-Starlink satellite to E3; time points not marked by L1 but with a residual exceeding 0.3 km are listed as low-confidence review items, and those with a peak ≥ 1 km are listed for "immediate review"; the drag residual is also one of the five inputs to the fusion scorer; and in the App's unified detection summary, it is the primary determination method for non-Starlink satellites. The reentry gate additionally uses the most recent 45 days of data: when perigee is below 150 km, or below 350 km with a descent rate exceeding 2 km per day, the object is regarded as naturally reentering and the maneuver count is output as 0.⁴

| Question | Component best used first |
|---|---|
| Does it exceed a known threshold? | L1 rule layer. |
| Does it deviate from the recent norm? | L2 statistical layer. |
| Do multiple channels jointly support it? | Auxiliary-channel check (L2 statistical change points, drag residual, ML window model, fusion scorer; in the final report's R7 workflow, affects only the evidence grade). |
| Can it be explained by drag or reentry? | Physics and applicability processing. |
| Are the data sufficient to judge? | Quality gate. |

This table is this book's arrangement of the engineering division of labor; each channel can be computed in parallel, but the determination order for formal reports has been fixed by the R7 workflow of the final report, and other systems may adjust to deployment conditions.⁴

The fixed 0.3 km threshold is not suitable at all altitudes. Checking 309 satellites passive over the whole period, the final report found 44 false alarms per thousand satellite-days at 300 km and 3.6 at 400 km, while above 600 km the threshold is too conservative; none of the several altitude-dependent schemes reached the target in all altitude bins in out-of-sample validation, so for now this is only a directional recommendation and the production code has not been changed. In the ground-truth evaluation, drag-residual items on 15 non-Starlink satellites recovered 12 burns not detected by L1, with quiet-period false alarms of 0.09 per thousand quiet satellite-days (95% confidence interval 0.02–0.22); however, there were only 4 false alarms, so the confidence interval remains wide.⁴
