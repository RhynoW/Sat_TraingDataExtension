# Chapter 13　From Single-Satellite Monitoring to Constellation-Level Analysis

> *This English edition is an AI-assisted translation of the Traditional Chinese manuscript (v1.2, 2026-11-15). Where they differ, the Chinese original prevails.*

## Chapter overview

On August 22, 2014, a Soyuz rocket lifted off from French Guiana carrying the fifth and sixth satellites of Europe's Galileo navigation system. Both satellites separated from the Fregat upper stage, but tracking data showed they were not where they were supposed to be. According to the target published by the launch provider at the time, they should have entered a circular orbit with an inclination of 55 degrees and a semi-major axis of 29,900 kilometers; what they actually reached was an elliptical orbit with an inclination of 49.8 degrees and a semi-major axis of 26,200 kilometers, with a lowest point of only about 13,700 kilometers.

The error was not a few kilometers; the entire orbital plane was tilted by 5 degrees. The subsequent inquiry board found that an aluminum bracket in the Fregat upper stage had placed a propellant line too close to a cold helium line; the propellant line froze, and the attitude control thrusters failed as a result. Neither satellite was insured.

If all you have are public TLEs, looking at two points in a constellation whose inclinations clearly do not fit with the rest, how do you judge whether this was a deliberate maneuver or an accident nobody wanted?

Constellation-level analysis is not the summing of single-satellite detection results; what it must answer is whether there are signals in the whole constellation that need to be explained by group behavior. This study uses Case 6 to test Starlink in real time from three angles: orbital-plane consistency, batch maneuver identification, and formation phase error.¹

This chapter treats constellation-level analysis as an extension from individual behavior to group behavior.

## Learning goals

- Understand the difference between constellation-level analysis and single-satellite monitoring.
- Understand the uses of orbital-plane consistency, batch maneuvers, and phase error.
- Use Case 6 to appreciate the value of a threshold not being triggered.
- Avoid interpreting group indicators directly as confirmed events.
- Avoid extrapolating constellation-level results to all constellations.

## What constellation-level analysis is asking

What constellation-level analysis must answer is:

> Did a whole batch of satellites move together on the same day?¹

Before answering, the comparison baseline must be confirmed. The same batch maneuver may mean something entirely different for a newly deployed constellation versus a mature one, and for different orbital planes and different missions.

This book recommends that constellation-level analysis distinguish at least three baselines:

| Baseline | Compared against | Question it is suited to answer |
|---|---|---|
| Orbital-plane consistency | Satellites in the same orbital plane | Is the inclination abnormally high? |
| Batch maneuvers | Number of satellites maneuvering on the same day | Are abnormally many satellites moving together? |
| Formation phase | Spacing between satellites within the same orbital plane | Has the phasing drifted? |

None of the three baselines is ground truth: an orbital plane may include satellites from different missions, batch maneuvers may be affected by data conditions, and phase may be affected by propagation errors.

Each of the following three sections lists the output fields this book recommends. With these fields, an analyst can reconstruct the reasoning process instead of seeing only a red flag.

## Orbital-plane consistency

Orbital-plane consistency is used to judge whether the inclination of satellites in the same orbital plane is abnormally high. This study uses the median plus 3σ (σ being the standard deviation) as an adaptive threshold and caught 3 abnormally high orbital planes among 228.¹

**Sidebar: The first Qianfan batch and the upper-stage breakup (2024)**

On August 6, 2024, a Long March 6A launched 18 Qianfan satellites from Taiyuan into a polar orbit of about 800 kilometers, after which the upper stage broke up. According to LeoLabs radar data, the breakup occurred at about 20:10 UTC that day at an altitude of about 810 kilometers, producing at least 700 and possibly more than 900 fragments; U.S. Space Command confirmed more than 300 trackable fragments. These are figures from different organizations on different bases, not a step-by-step increase. With new satellites and hundreds of fragments appearing at the same time in nearby orbital shells, before computing orbital-plane statistics one must first sort out which catalog numbers are satellites and which are fragments.

This book recommends that orbital-plane consistency output be labeled with:

- Orbital plane ID and number of satellites.
- Median and standard deviation of inclination.
- The adaptive threshold.
- Abnormally high orbital planes.
- Recommended follow-up data for review.

## Batch maneuver identification

Batch maneuver identification is used to judge whether an abnormally large number of satellites maneuvered together on the same day. In the Case 6 data, the single-day maximum was 494 satellites (2026-10-27), the threshold K was about 600 (K being the threshold on the number of satellites maneuvering in a single day for a "batch event day"), and a total of 0 days were judged to be batch event days.¹

**Sidebar: SpaceX's preventive deorbit of about 100 early Starlink satellites (2024)**

On February 12, 2024, SpaceX announced that it would use thrusters to perform a controlled deorbit of about 100 "early version 1" Starlink satellites. SpaceX said these satellites could still maneuver and were serving normally, but that a "common issue" had been found in this small group of satellites that could increase the probability of failure; the nature of the issue was not disclosed, and the process was expected to be largely complete in about six months. Looked at with this book's method, orbit lowering spread over several months would not necessarily cross the threshold on any single day; what one would see is a group of same-generation satellites successively lowering their semi-major axes, and the reason would still depend on the operator's announcement.

**Sidebar: The first 27 Kuiper satellites climb together (2025)**

On April 28, 2025, an Atlas V launched 27 production satellites of Amazon's Project Kuiper from Cape Canaveral to an altitude of 450 kilometers. According to Amazon's published plan, they would then gradually climb to 630 kilometers using electric propulsion; the full constellation is planned at more than 3,200 satellites. A single batch of satellites rising together from the same altitude is the simplest form of batch behavior: looked at one by one, each shows only a continuous change in semi-major axis; only when the whole batch is put together does a consistent collective climb become visible.

This book recommends that batch maneuver identification output be labeled with:

- Date and number of satellites.
- The threshold K.
- Triggered and untriggered events.
- Data quality status.
- Recommended follow-up data for review.

## Formation phase error

Formation phase error is used to judge whether the spacing between satellites within the same orbital plane has drifted. Among 227 orbital planes, this study caught 290 phase-outlier satellites.¹

**Sidebar: Iridium NEXT slot-by-slot replacement (2017–2019)**

During its generational changeover, Iridium handed over slot by slot: an old satellite vacated its position and lowered its orbit, and a new-generation NEXT satellite took over the same slot. According to reports, the last slot swap was completed on February 5, 2019. According to the report Iridium submitted to the United Nations Office for Outer Space Affairs, the orbit lowering of SV097 in December 2019 was the last active operation of the entire deorbit program, in which a total of 65 old satellites were lowered. During the changeover, pairs of "new satellite in position, old satellite departing" appear within the same orbital plane; looking only at single satellites or only at phase outliers, it is easy to mistake a whole batch of normal handovers for a formation that has drifted.

This book recommends that formation phase error output be labeled with:

- Orbital plane ID and number of satellites.
- Median and standard deviation of phase.
- Number of outlier satellites.
- Recommended follow-up data for review.

## Main case: Case 6

> **Evidence level: A | Independently recomputed**
>
> **Data sources**: Starlink public TLEs, analysis window 2026-10-13 to 2026-11-12, covering 11,234 satellites and 828,374 TLEs; orbital-plane consistency covers 228 orbital planes, and formation phase covers 227 orbital planes. The figures are computed in real time by this project for the entire constellation.
>
> **Ground truth**: No external ground truth; comparison is only against the constellation's own adaptive thresholds (median + 3σ for orbital-plane consistency; batch event threshold K about 600 satellites). The case file does not state any item-by-item check against operator announcements.
>
> **Key assumptions**: Satellites in the same orbital plane should have almost exactly the same inclination; the batch threshold is determined adaptively from the constellation's own background rather than being a fixed value.
>
> **Limitations**: Represents only a single 30-day data snapshot; the page computes in real time, so the figures change whenever the data window changes; the causes of the 3 orbital planes with abnormally high inclination have not been confirmed, and the case file only speculates that they may be a new batch of satellites not yet fully in position or coordinated inclination adjustments between shells. Case 12's injection tests further indicate that the adaptive threshold is raised by background noise (Qianfan about 126 satellites/day; injecting 2 satellites simultaneously is not flagged); satellites whose inclination changes by as much as 1° are thrown out of their original orbital plane by the clustering preprocessing and thus escape the consistency check; the "zero anomalies" for OneWeb and Qianfan cannot distinguish quiet from insensitive.
>
> **When not to use this method**: Extrapolating the results of this data window to other constellations or other times; reading "not triggered" as "no coordinated maneuver" in a constellation with frequent background maneuvers and a raised threshold; group behavior that unfolds successively over several months will not necessarily cross the batch threshold on any single day; treating group indicators as confirmed events.

**Data specification**: Nature measured; Period 2026-10-13 to 2026-11-12; TLE count Starlink 828,374 records (11,234 satellites); Threshold orbital-plane consistency: median + 3σ (adaptive, not fixed); batch maneuvers: number of satellites maneuvering in a single day K≈600; formation phase outlier threshold not stated in the case file; False-alarm rate not evaluated (no external ground truth).

### Positioning of the case

Case 6 asks: in a mega-constellation like Starlink, can we catch "a whole batch of satellites being adjusted at once"? Using the most recent 30 days of real data, it tests from three angles: whether orbital planes are consistent, whether an abnormally large number of satellites maneuvered on the same day, and whether formation phasing has drifted.²

This book treats Case 6 as a case of constellation-level analysis, not as a complete validation of the whole system. The performance of constellation-level analysis must be interpreted together with data quality, rule suppression, the statistical layer, the fusion layer, and the physics gate.

### Data window and sample

The analysis window of Case 6 is 2026-10-13 to 2026-11-12, covering 11,234 satellites and 828,374 TLEs. The Case 6 page computes in real time for the entire Starlink constellation, and the figures cited in this chapter represent only the results for this data window.²

These figures belong to a specific data snapshot and should not be extrapolated to all constellations or all times. Nor do they mean the system has passed all deployment validation.

### The value of a threshold not being triggered

Case 6 points out that in these 30 days of real data, not a single day triggered the batch maneuver threshold (maximum 494 satellites < K about 600 satellites). This is itself a meaningful negative result: Case 6's interpretation is that Starlink's electric-propulsion maneuvers are continuous, distributed station-keeping by individual satellites, not centralized, constellation-wide synchronized actions.²

The methodological lesson this book draws from this is: a threshold not being triggered is still a valid result and should be kept as a negative result, not rewritten as a success; but not being triggered does not mean there is no anomaly either; the data conditions and the threshold version must still be checked, and the results for this constellation should not be extrapolated to other constellations.

Case 12's synthetic injection test (2026-09-10) provides a point of comparison: Qianfan's background baseline is about 126 satellites per day, and a maneuver injected simultaneously into 2 satellites is not flagged as a batch event. This shows that the adaptive threshold rises with the constellation's background, and "not triggered" must be interpreted together with how high the threshold is (see Chapter 12).¹

### National security implications

The lesson of Case 6 is:

> The value of constellation-level analysis lies not in catching every anomaly, but in giving group behavior checkable evidence.²

When doing constellation-level analysis, orbital planes, batch maneuvers, and phase must be tested separately; results must be labeled with the data window and sample; untriggered results must be kept; group indicators must not be treated as ground truth; and revalidation must be performed according to deployment conditions.

## Division of labor between constellation-level analysis and other layers

Constellation-level analysis does not replace the rule layer, nor is it subordinate to the statistical layer. It provides evidence of group behavior; the rule layer provides interpretable candidates and reasons for suppression; the statistical layer quantifies the degree of anomaly; the fusion layer integrates multiple channels; and physics and applicability processing provides additional plausibility checks.

| Question | Component best used first |
|---|---|
| Does it exceed a known threshold? | L1 rule layer. |
| Does it deviate from the recent norm? | L2 statistical layer. |
| Do multiple channels jointly support it? | L3 fusion layer. |
| Can it be explained by drag or reentry? | Physics and applicability processing. |
| Are the data sufficient to judge? | Quality gate. |
| Is there group behavior? | Constellation-level analysis. |

This table is this book's recommended engineering division of labor and does not mean every system must execute in the same order. An actual pipeline may compute in parallel or be adjusted to deployment conditions.
