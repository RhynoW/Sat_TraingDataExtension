# Chapter 15　Detection Limits: Which Maneuvers Go Unseen

> *This English edition is an AI-assisted translation of the Traditional Chinese manuscript (v1.2, 2026-11-15). Where they differ, the Chinese original prevails.*

## Chapter overview

At 17:07 GMT on August 23, 2016, the power output of one solar array on the Copernicus Sentinel-1A satellite, orbiting at an altitude of 700 km, suddenly dropped slightly. The drop was small relative to the total output and routine operations were not affected, but the engineers wanted to know what had happened. They switched on an onboard camera that had originally been intended only to monitor the deployment of the solar array in 2014.

The images clearly showed the impact: a damaged area about 40 cm in diameter on the solar array, struck from the rear side. The European Space Agency estimated that the incoming particle was only a few millimeters in size; a later image caption put it at "less than 5 mm." Whether it was a natural micrometeoroid or a piece of man-made debris was still under analysis at the time.

The same announcement contained one more sentence: from the ground, it is usually possible to track only objects larger than about 5 cm. Whatever hit Sentinel-1A had never been in any catalog; it had no TLE, and there was no warning. Objects that are too small go unseen; maneuvers that are too small or too slow likewise sink beneath the noise of the data. So how large must a change be before it can be seen?

The purpose of discussing detection limits is not to claim that a system can catch every maneuver, but to state clearly which maneuvers can still be caught and which can no longer be seen, under specific data conditions, a specific noise floor, and a specific threshold. This chapter uses Case 17 and Case 18 to show how to go from noise estimation to synthetic injection and establish a verifiable lower bound on detection.¹

A detection limit is a quantification of the "boundary of capability." It must be interpreted together with data quality, rule-based suppression, the statistical layer, the fusion layer, and physical gating; a single detection rate cannot replace a complete chain of evidence.

## Learning goals

- Understand the difference between a detection limit and a detection rate.
- Understand how the noise floor is estimated and its limitations.
- Use Case 17 to learn about synthetic injection and conditional detection curves.
- Use Case 18 to learn about the noise floor at different altitudes.
- Avoid extrapolating a single detection rate to all orbits and all maneuvers.

## What a detection limit asks

The question a detection limit must answer is:

> **How small can a maneuver be and still be caught by the system?**¹

Before answering, the basis of comparison must first be established. The same detection rate may mean entirely different things for different altitudes, inclinations, data conditions, and missions.

This book recommends that a detection limit distinguish at least three kinds of output:

| Output | Meaning | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Noise floor | The noise level of the data itself. | Does not represent the lower bound on detection. |
| Synthetic injection | Maneuvers injected under known conditions. | Does not mean real maneuvers have been verified. |
| Conditional detection curve | Detection rate under specific conditions. | Does not mean it applies to all conditions. |

The three are at different levels and cannot substitute for one another; when interpreting them, each should be explained according to the limitation column in the table.

**Sidebar: ESA *Space Environment Report 2025* (2025)**

The space environment report released by the European Space Agency in April 2025 states that space surveillance networks track about 40,000 objects, of which about 11,000 are active payloads; models, however, estimate more than 1.2 million debris objects larger than 1 cm, enough to cause catastrophic damage, and more than 50,000 larger than 10 cm. The number tracked and the number estimated to exist differ by a factor of about thirty. Detection limits address the other side of the same issue: what the data can see is only part of what actually happens.

**Sidebar: The impact crater on the window of the ISS Cupola (2016)**

In April 2016, ESA astronaut Tim Peake photographed, from inside the Cupola of the International Space Station, a circular impact crater 7 mm in diameter on one of its windows. ESA explained that the impactor was possibly a paint flake or a small metal fragment no larger than a few thousandths of a millimeter; the same page also notes that objects under 1 cm can disable satellite instruments or critical systems. Objects of this size can only be stopped by shielding; they cannot be avoided by tracking. Below the detection limit, the risk does not disappear; it simply has to be handled in a different way.

## Noise floor

The noise floor refers to the noise level of the data itself, that is, "how large the noise is when nothing is happening." Case 18 uses 8 passive geodetic spheres spanning 800–19,126 km to illustrate the noise floor at different altitudes. Passive geodetic spheres are satellites whose surfaces are covered with laser retroreflectors, with no thrusters, that do not actively maneuver; there is therefore no need to exclude maneuver periods, and they serve as a clean control group in themselves.¹

**Sidebar: LAGEOS-1, the geodetic sphere without thrusters (1976)**

LAGEOS-1, launched in 1976, is 60 cm in diameter and has a mass of about 407 kg (the ILRS figure is 406.965 kg; some sources give 411 kg). It has an aluminum shell around a brass core, and its surface carries 426 cube-corner retroreflectors, 422 of fused silica and 4 of germanium. It has no thrusters, its area-to-mass ratio is extremely small, and it is only minimally affected by atmospheric drag. Satellites of this kind are ideal control groups for measuring the noise floor: since they do not maneuver, any jumps seen in their TLEs can only come from the data itself.

This book recommends that noise-floor outputs indicate:

- The altitude band and sample size.
- The noise estimator and time window.
- Whether jumps were excluded or the data detrended.
- Known failure scenarios.
- Recommended data for follow-up review.

With these fields, analysts can reconstruct the reasoning, rather than seeing only a red flag.

## Main case 1: Case 17

> **Evidence level: A | Independently recomputed**
>
> **Data sources**: Noise and atmospheric-drag background from real FORMOSAT-7 (COSMIC-2) TLEs, with known semi-major axis steps injected; 15,775 individual trials over 478 satellite × time-window combinations. The data period is not stated in the case file.
>
> **Ground truth**: No ground truth for real maneuvers; the ground truth is the artificially injected known magnitude (synthetic ground truth).
>
> **Key assumptions**: A maneuver is represented as a semi-major axis step (or the same net displacement spread over several days); the detection rate is fitted with logistic regression on the effective signal-to-noise ratio (cluster-aware bootstrap with 2,000 resamples): SNR50 is 3.767 (95% CI [3.583, 3.961]), SNR90 is 9.868, and the drag coupling constant is 0.32 (95% CI [0.28, 0.4]).
>
> **Limitations**: The whole is a simulation under fixed assumptions; although the curve was tested on time-split held-out data (AUC=0.9773), what was tested is still synthetic injection, and it has not yet been verified with real maneuvers; only one constellation, FORMOSAT-7, was used.
>
> **When not to use this method**: Treating the synthetic-injection detection rate as the recall for real maneuvers; extrapolating to orbits, altitudes, or data conditions other than FORMOSAT-7; carrying over low-drag thresholds during high-drag periods or when the TLE update interval lengthens (under high drag, the magnitude needed to reach a detection rate of about 90% is about 3–4 times that under low drag, and when the update interval lengthens by a factor of 4, the gap widens to about 10 times); maneuvers executed in spread-out fashion (for the same 50 m net displacement, the detection rate is 55.4% when completed instantaneously and falls to 43.3% when spread over half a day).

**Data specification**: Nature Simulation (maneuver injection against a FORMOSAT-7 TLE background); Period not stated in the case file; TLE count not applicable (simulated injection, 15,775 trials distributed over 478 satellite × time-window combinations; the number of background FORMOSAT-7 TLEs is not stated in the case file); Threshold effective signal-to-noise ratio SNR50 = 3.767 (95% CI [3.583, 3.961]), SNR90 = 9.868 (50% / 90% detection-rate thresholds from the logistic regression fit); False-alarm rate not stated in the case file (time-split held-out validation AUC=0.9773).

### Case positioning

The question in Case 17 is: "How small can a maneuver be and still be caught by the system?" Using a FORMOSAT-7 (COSMIC-2) synthetic-injection experiment (15,775 trials, a simulation under fixed assumptions), it expresses the minimum detectable magnitude as a verifiable rule with confidence intervals, rather than as a single number.²

In this chapter, Case 17 is a detection-limit case, not a complete validation of the whole system; its results must still be interpreted together with the layers described above.

### Synthetic injection

Synthetic injection means injecting semi-major axis steps of known size into the noise and atmospheric-drag background of real FORMOSAT-7 TLEs, and then measuring whether the system can detect them. Using this kind of experiment (15,775 trials), Case 17 expresses the minimum detectable magnitude as a verifiable rule with confidence intervals.²

These 15,775 trials are distributed over 478 satellite × time-window combinations.²

The value of this experiment lies in advancing the detection limit from a single number to a verifiable conditional curve. The injected maneuvers are known signals added artificially, so the results do not mean that real maneuvers have been verified, nor that they apply to all conditions.

**Sidebar: The DebriSat hypervelocity impact test (2014)**

On April 15, 2014, at the U.S. Air Force AEDC Range G test facility, a 570 g hollow aluminum projectile traveling at 6.8 km per second struck DebriSat, a 56 kg satellite representative of modern low Earth orbit satellites. A rough estimate based on the NASA breakup model put the number of fragments 2 mm or larger generated by the impact at about 85,000; the fragments were collected in foam panels and have since been extracted by the research team on an ongoing basis. The logic of this test is the same as that of synthetic injection: only by first creating an event under known conditions can one test whether a model's estimates of real events are accurate.

### Conditional detection curves

Case 17 established a conditional detection curve and lower bound: the detection rate is not a fixed value but varies with maneuver magnitude, data update frequency, and atmospheric-drag state. The curve itself has been tested on time-split held-out data, but what was tested is still synthetic injection, and full verification with real maneuvers has not yet been completed.²

Case 17 fits a logistic regression curve on the effective signal-to-noise ratio (which accounts for both maneuver magnitude and drag-noise coupling, rather than the change in semi-major axis alone), and estimates confidence intervals with a cluster-aware bootstrap of 2,000 resamples: the threshold for reaching a 50% detection rate, SNR50, is 3.767 (95% confidence interval 3.583–3.961); the threshold for reaching a 90% detection rate, SNR90, is 9.868; and the drag coupling constant is 0.32 (95% confidence interval 0.28–0.4). Tested on time-split held-out data, the AUC is 0.9773.²

The conditional detection curve should therefore be labeled as follows: the detection-limit method has been established, but verification is still incomplete; the curve should not be extrapolated to all orbits and all maneuvers, and it must be re-verified against actual conditions at deployment.

### National security implications

The lesson of Case 17 is:

> **A detection limit is not a single number but a conditional curve.**²

In practice, the noise floor and the lower bound on detection should be reported separately, with the data conditions and threshold version indicated; unverified results should be retained and annotated, the detection rate cannot be used as ground truth, and re-verification is required when moving to new deployment conditions.

**Sidebar: Space Fence reaches initial operational capability (2020)**

On March 27, 2020, the U.S. Space Force announced that the Space Fence radar on Kwajalein Atoll in the Marshall Islands had reached initial operational capability, about 18 months later than the originally planned 2018. According to media accounts of official statements, it can track objects smaller than 10 cm; before this, space surveillance networks were tracking more than 26,000 objects, and the Space Force expected the number to increase significantly. New sensors can push the threshold lower, but the threshold remains; what the public catalog can show also depends on other parties' sensors and how openly they share.

**Sidebar: SpaceBEE launched without authorization (2018)**

On January 12, 2018, after the U.S. Federal Communications Commission (FCC) had denied its application, Swarm Technologies nonetheless had four SpaceBEE satellites launched on India's PSLV-C40; one of the FCC's reasons for denial was that the satellites were only 0.25U, raising concern that they would be difficult to track. In December of the same year the two parties settled, and Swarm paid a fine of 900,000 US dollars. According to Swarm, however, the first four carried passive radar reflectors and were tracked steadily by space surveillance networks and LeoLabs. Here, the detection threshold became not only a physical question but also a regulatory criterion.

## Main case 2: Case 18

> **Evidence level: A | Independently recomputed**
>
> **Data sources**: TLEs of 8 passive geodetic spheres (Stella, Starlette, Ajisai, LARES, LAGEOS-1/2, Etalon-1/2) at altitudes of 800–19,126 km; σ is the residual σ_resid after polynomial detrending within a 90-day window. The data period is not stated in the case file.
>
> **Ground truth**: No maneuver ground truth is needed: passive geodetic spheres have no thrusters and do not maneuver, so they are themselves a quiet control group; the LEO-band results (σ 0.17–0.46 m) were additionally compared with, and agree with, DORIS-certified quiet-period measurements (median about 0.2 m).
>
> **Key assumptions**: Jumps in the TLEs of passive geodetic spheres come only from the data itself; the lunar third-body perturbation in the Etalon residuals (main period 27.49–27.59 days, matching the sidereal month of 27.32 days) can be modeled and removed.
>
> **Limitations**: There are only 8 samples; Etalon's σ has two definitions: windowed detrending gives 6.2–7.7 m, while removing a linear trend over the whole series gives 7.42 m (Etalon-1) and 8.80 m (Etalon-2), which fall to 0.89 m and 0.35 m after removing the main lunar perturbation period and its subharmonics; most of the cross-altitude correlation before removal (Spearman ρ=+0.79, p=0.02) is a lunar artifact.
>
> **When not to use this method**: Comparing noise floors across orbital regimes without first removing known physical effects such as lunar/solar third-body perturbations and atmospheric drag; applying the noise floor of passive geodetic spheres directly to satellites strongly affected by drag (the technical appendix records a noise floor of 24–75 m in the 350–550 km band where Starlink operates); treating the noise floor as the lower bound on detection.

**Data specification**: Nature Measured; Period not stated in the case file (σ computed over 90-day windows); TLE count not stated in the case file (8 passive geodetic spheres: Stella, Starlette, Ajisai, LARES, LAGEOS-1/2, Etalon-1/2); Threshold no fixed threshold (measures the noise floor σ_resid; no detection decision is made); False-alarm rate not stated in the case file.

The question in Case 18 is: "What does the TLE noise floor look like at different altitudes?" It uses 8 passive geodetic spheres spanning 800–19,126 km to illustrate the noise floor at different altitudes.³

### Noise floor at different altitudes

Case 18 uses 8 passive geodetic spheres, from 800 km to 19,126 km, to compare the noise floor at different altitudes.³

Computed as the residual σ after polynomial detrending within a 90-day window, the σ of Stella, Starlette, Ajisai, and LARES in the LEO band (800–1,488 km) is 0.17–0.46 m, which agrees with the median of about 0.2 m from DORIS-certified quiet-period measurements; this amounts to independently verifying the same noise floor with an entirely different class of satellite.³ The technical appendix also records that in the 350–550 km band where Starlink operates, atmospheric drag is strong and highly variable, and the noise floor reaches 24–75 m; the results for passive geodetic spheres cannot be applied directly to such satellites.

The value of this result lies in extending the noise floor from a single number to a comparison across altitude bands. But it does not mean it applies to all altitudes, nor to all satellites.

**Sidebar: India's Mission Shakti anti-satellite test (2019)**

On March 27, 2019, India destroyed its own Microsat-R satellite with a ground-based missile. The satellite's orbit was about 274 km, and India stated that the intercept altitude was below 300 km; according to analysis by independent trackers, however, the apogees of some cataloged fragments were thrown up to about 2,250 km. A single low-altitude event scattered debris across several altitude bands, and each band has a different noise floor. The security and decision-making implications of this test are discussed in Chapter 19.

### The lunar perturbation artifact

Case 18 once produced a lunar perturbation artifact that was nearly misread as "MEO is inherently noisier." Here, MEO refers to the medium Earth orbit band at about 19,100 km where the Etalon satellites operate. The raw Etalon residuals appeared markedly larger, but most of that was a predictable, modelable lunar perturbation signal; after removing it, the noise floor rises only moderately from low Earth orbit to MEO.³

Etalon's σ has two definitions. Computed with the same 90-day windowed detrending as the other geodetic spheres, the σ of Etalon-1/2 is 6.2–7.7 m, about 30–40 times the median for the LEO band; before removing the lunar perturbation, σ rises with altitude across 800–19,126 km (Spearman ρ=+0.79, p=0.02). When the Etalon residuals are modeled for lunar third-body perturbation, the main period is 27.49–27.59 days, matching the sidereal month of 27.32 days; the lunar modeling instead uses the definition that removes a linear trend over the whole series, which starts slightly higher: 7.42 m for Etalon-1 and 8.80 m for Etalon-2; after removing the main period and its subharmonics, these fall to 0.89 m and 0.35 m, respectively.³

This experience also shows that the noise-floor method has been established, but full verification has not yet been completed. It should therefore be clearly stated that known physical perturbations must be removed before comparing noise floors across orbital regimes; the results should not be extrapolated to all altitudes, and they must be re-verified against actual conditions at deployment.

### National security implications

The lesson of Case 18 is:

> **The noise floor is not a fixed value but a quantity that varies with altitude and conditions.**³

In practice, different altitude bands should be reported separately, with the data conditions and threshold version indicated; artifact cases should be kept on record, the noise floor cannot be used as ground truth, and re-verification is required when moving to new deployment conditions.

## Division of labor between the detection limit and other layers

The detection limit does not replace the rule layer, nor is it subordinate to the statistical layer. It provides the boundary of capability; the rule layer provides explainable candidates and reasons for suppression; the statistical layer quantifies the degree of anomaly; the fusion layer integrates multiple channels; and physics and applicability handling provide additional plausibility checks.

| Question | Component best suited to use first |
|---|---|
| Does it exceed a known threshold? | L1 rule layer. |
| Does it deviate from recent normal behavior? | L2 statistical layer. |
| Do multiple channels jointly support it? | L3 fusion layer. |
| Can it be explained by drag or reentry? | Physics and applicability handling. |
| Are the data sufficient for a judgment? | Quality gate. |
| How small can a maneuver be and still be caught? | Detection limit. |

This table is this book's recommended engineering division of labor; it does not mean that every system must execute in the same order. Actual pipelines can compute in parallel and can also be adjusted to deployment conditions.
