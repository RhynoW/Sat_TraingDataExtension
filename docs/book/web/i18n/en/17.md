## Chapter overview

At 09:00:46 (UTC) on September 23, 1999, the Mars Climate Orbiter fired its main engine to enter Mars orbit. Four minutes later it passed behind Mars and its signal disappeared, but 49 seconds earlier than predicted. The expected 21-minute occultation passed, and the ground never received its signal again.

The navigation team's original target was for the spacecraft to pass 226 km above the surface on its first pass of Mars. A week before orbit insertion, the orbit determination result had dropped to 150 to 170 km; about an hour before insertion, the latest data indicated it might be only 110 km, while the lowest altitude the spacecraft could survive was 80 km. The revised estimate after the fact was 57 km.

In fact, as early as the spring and summer of that year, staff at the working level had noticed that different navigation solutions disagreed: the trajectory computed from Doppler data alone was consistently closer to Mars than those from other methods. This discrepancy was reported only informally and was never resolved; an emergency correction that could have raised the orbit was discussed verbally but ultimately not executed.

On September 29, the cause was found: a ground software file output values in pound-force seconds, while the interface document specified newton seconds, a difference of a factor of 4.45. How could a unit error like this go uncaught throughout a nine-month voyage?

For an AI system to be reproducible, auditable, and maintainable, it is not enough simply to get the model running; one must also explain whether the results can be reconstructed, audited, and maintained under a specific data snapshot, a specific time system, and a specific version. This chapter uses Case 4, Case 19, and Case 25 to show how to handle external ground truth, data ingestion, and statistical aggregation bias.¹

## What reproducible, auditable, and maintainable ask

The question this chapter must answer is:

> **Can this result be reconstructed, audited, and maintained?**¹

Before answering, the basis of comparison must first be established. The same version number may mean entirely different things for different data snapshots, time systems, and missions.

**Sidebar: The maiden flight of Ariane 5 in 1996**

On June 4, 1996, about 36.7 seconds after liftoff of Ariane 5 Flight 501, the backup and then the primary inertial reference systems failed, and at about 39 seconds the rocket began to break up and self-destructed. The inquiry board found that a horizontal bias variable in the alignment function overflowed when converted from a 64-bit floating-point number to a 16-bit signed integer. This code had been carried over from Ariane 4 and actually served no purpose after liftoff; Ariane 5's horizontal velocity was higher, exceeding the original numerical assumptions. The two systems ran the same code, and so failed at the same moment. Reusing code means reusing its assumptions about data ranges along with it.

This book recommends distinguishing at least three properties:

| Output | Meaning | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Reproducible | Whether the result can be reconstructed. | Does not mean all conditions have been verified. |
| Auditable | Whether the result can be checked. | Does not mean all biases have been ruled out. |
| Maintainable | Whether the system can keep operating. | Does not mean all risks have been ruled out. |

The three are at different levels and cannot substitute for one another; when interpreting them, each should be explained according to the limitation column in the table.

## External ground truth and time systems

Case 4 explains where the maneuver ground truth for 23 external benchmark satellites comes from and how it is handled, and points out that the three sources each use a different time system, which is where errors most easily occur.¹

| Source | Satellites covered | Count | Data type | Time system |
|---|---|--:|---|---|
| IDS/DORIS maneuver history files | 14 development altimetry satellites + hold-out SPOT-2/3/4/5, Sentinel-6B | 19 | Operator-certified maneuver logs (including per-burn ΔV) | TAI |
| NASA PO.DAAC SOE files | GRACE-A/B, GRACE-FO-C/D (all hold-out) | 4 | Operator-certified thrust events (window level) | GPS seconds |
| TACC FORMOSAT-7 leoOrb | 6 FORMOSAT-7 satellites (not among the 23; used separately as a TLE error baseline) | 6 | SP3-c precise orbit determination ephemerides | GPS time |

In the table, hold-out refers to satellites that never took part in development and are reserved for independent testing; operator-certified refers to records certified and published by the satellite operators themselves, not computed by this project. IDS is the International DORIS Service; SOE is the Sequence of Events file; TACC is the Taiwan Analysis Center for COSMIC of the Central Weather Administration (CWA) under the Ministry of Transportation and Communications, and leoOrb is its FORMOSAT-7 precise ephemeris product; SP3-c is a precise orbit file format (Extended Standard Product 3 Orbit Format). TAI is a time system that differs from UTC by a fixed number of seconds. ΔV is the velocity increment.

This table summarizes the three ground-truth sources; it does not mean that every system must handle things in the same way. Actual systems should adjust according to deployment conditions, data sources, and the scope of validation.

## Main case 1: Case 4

> **Evidence level: B | Official or primary source**
>
> **Data sources**: Three public sources requiring no account: IDS/DORIS maneuver history files (19 satellites), NASA PO.DAAC SOE files (4 satellites), and TACC FORMOSAT-7 leoOrb precise ephemerides (6 satellites, used separately as a TLE error baseline); the 23 benchmark satellites are divided into 14 for development and 9 hold-out. The demonstration case is the TLEs of Jason-3 (NORAD 41240) from 2026-06-20 to 07-02, compared against the four burns recorded in the IDS/DORIS logs.
>
> **Ground truth**: Maneuver logs and thrust events certified and published by the satellite operators themselves (operator-certified), not computed by this project.
>
> **Key assumptions**: The operators' records are complete and correct; the time systems of the three sources (TAI, GPS seconds, GPS time) have all been correctly converted to UTC.
>
> **Limitations**: The ground truth itself is a primary source, and this project only parses and compares it; the parsers and time conversions were written by this project, each with a regression test. The 9 hold-out satellites are "all remaining usable satellites outside the 14," not a random sample; TLE quality for the SPOT series before 2010 is poorer; PO.DAAC provides only window-level thrust events, with no per-burn ΔV.
>
> **When not to use this method**: External records should not be compared directly as ground-truth epochs before their time systems have been converted; for targets whose operators do not publish maneuver records, ground truth cannot be obtained from these three sources.

### Handling time systems

IDS uses TAI, PO.DAAC's SOE files use GPS seconds (counted from J2000), and the leoOrb precise ephemerides use GPS time; all three differ from UTC by a fixed offset of tens of seconds.²

**Sidebar: GPS's 13 microseconds in 2016**

On January 26, 2016, the U.S. Air Force's 2nd Space Operations Squadron confirmed that the UTC timing signal broadcast by GPS was off by 13 microseconds, exceeding design specifications; it took about five hours from confirmation to resolution. Core navigation functions were normal, and the U.S. side also stated that it was not jamming or spoofing. The cause lay not in the satellites themselves but in a flaw in the ground system software, which was triggered when the oldest satellite, SVN 23, was removed from the constellation, and which affected only the timing information on the legacy L-band signals. A routine decommissioning action caused a seemingly unrelated time-conversion parameter to go wrong; time conversion is itself data and likewise needs to be monitored.

This book recommends that time systems be clearly indicated with:

- The source and time system.
- The conversion method and fixed offset.
- The parser and regression tests.
- Known failure scenarios.
- Recommended data for follow-up review.

With these fields, analysts can reconstruct the reasoning, rather than seeing only a red flag.

**Sidebar: Starliner's 11 hours in 2019**

On December 20, 2019, after the uncrewed Starliner spacecraft launched, the onboard computer took its time from an earlier countdown sequence rather than the final countdown, so the mission elapsed timer was off by 11 hours and the orbit insertion burn did not occur at the scheduled time. After the spacecraft was stabilized, the remaining propellant was no longer sufficient to dock with the International Space Station, and it landed in New Mexico two days later. According to several media accounts of statements by the independent review team, integrated testing before launch had stopped before the orbit insertion burn, so the timer problem was not caught. "Where to get the time from" is itself an interface and needs to be tested end to end.

### National security implications

The lesson of Case 4 is:

> **Ground-truth sources and time systems must be handled separately.**²

In practice, ground-truth sources and time systems should be recorded separately, with the conversion method and fixed offset indicated; conversions should retain regression tests, external records cannot be compared directly as ground-truth epochs before their time systems have been converted, and re-verification is required when moving to new deployment conditions.

## Main case 2: Case 19

> **Evidence level: A | Independently recomputed**
>
> **Data sources**: This project's data download, detection, and import programs, and its TLE database; through a live check, the case file lists 1,614 objects with 6-digit IDs in the database (as of the 2026-11-14 export; the catalog keeps growing) (1,194 officially cataloged and 420 with analyst-assigned temporary numbers).
>
> **Ground truth**: No external ground truth; verified with this project's regression tests: 29 parameterized test cases, 2 tests for 6-digit import, and all 19 existing import tests passing after the modification.
>
> **Key assumptions**: Alpha-5 replaces the highest digit with a letter (excluding the easily confused I and O), with an upper limit of 339999.
>
> **Limitations**: The parsing stopgap (3 call sites, now consolidated into a shared decoder with a fallback retained for standalone deployment) and formatted output have been completed; migrating the main data source to OMM has not yet been completed, and if the catalog exceeds 339999 before the migration, another emergency stopgap will be needed. The tests cover only the known call sites and do not mean that all data ingestion points have been checked.
>
> **When not to use this method**: When numbers exceed the Alpha-5 upper limit of 339999, this case's gatekeeping regular expression and decoder no longer apply; other data ingestion points that have not been checked should not be assumed to be compatible.

**Data specification**: Nature Measured (database check); Period not stated in the case file (the count of 6-digit IDs is from a live check at the 2026-11-14 export); TLE count not stated in the case file (affected objects are the 1,614 with 6-digit IDs: 1,194 officially cataloged and 420 with analyst-assigned temporary numbers); Threshold no fixed detection threshold (Alpha-5 gatekeeping regular expression `[0-9A-HJ-NP-Z][0-9]{4}`, excluding I and O, upper limit 339999); False-alarm rate not evaluated (no external ground truth).

### Case positioning

The question in Case 19 is: "On the day the catalog passes 100,000 objects, is the code ready?" Using the real engineering story of the 6-digit NORAD/Alpha-5 migration, it shows that the bug was hidden in the gatekeeping regular expression at data ingestion, not in the conspicuous parsing lines.³

NORAD catalog IDs have traditionally been 5 digits, with an upper limit of 99999. Alpha-5 is the industry's transitional scheme: it replaces the highest digit with a letter, extending the upper limit to 339999. Any program that directly assumes "a NORAD ID is 5 digits" will break when it encounters this conversion.

This is not a hypothetical future problem. As of the 2026-11-14 export, this project's TLE database already contained 1,614 objects with 6-digit numbers, of which 1,194 were officially cataloged and 420 had analyst-assigned temporary numbers; the catalog keeps growing.³

In this chapter, Case 19 is a case about data ingestion and regression testing, not a complete validation of the whole system; the behavior of data ingestion must still be interpreted together with the layers described above.

### Alpha-5 data ingestion

The bug in Case 19 was hidden in the gatekeeping regular expression at data ingestion, not in the conspicuous parsing lines. The gatekeeping regular expression is the regular expression that filters TLE data lines before parsing; as originally written, it accepted only numbers beginning with a digit, so Alpha-5 data lines beginning with a letter were filtered out entirely before entering any parsing logic. No error was raised; there was just slightly less data, which made it hard to notice. The parsing stopgap for this part has been completed and is covered by regression tests.³

**Sidebar: The first six-digit number in 2026**

According to the notice at the top of the CelesTrak page, the U.S. Space Force catalog ran out of five-digit numbers on July 11, 2026. According to a KeepTrack write-up, the first six-digit number, 100000, was assigned to the Portuguese CubeSat Saramago and written as A0000 in TLEs. That article did not report any actual software failures; it only listed possible failure modes, for example that A0000 would sort before 99999 in a string sort. If data ingestion validates numbers as five digits, new objects will be silently filtered out; the gatekeeping regular expression in Case 19 is exactly this kind of quiet failure.

Alpha-5 data ingestion should therefore be clearly labeled: the ingestion method has been fixed, but this does not mean all data ingestion points have been checked; the fix should not be extrapolated to all catalogs and data sources, and it must be re-verified against actual conditions at deployment.

### Regression testing

Case 19 takes a pragmatic stopgap and candidly leaves the root fix open. The stopgap is covered by regression tests; the root fix, namely migrating the main data source to the new-generation orbit data format (OMM) that natively supports numbers of any length, has not yet been completed. If the catalog exceeds the Alpha-5 upper limit before the migration is complete, another emergency stopgap will be needed.³

**Sidebar: Huygens's Doppler problem in 2000**

In 2000, while Cassini–Huygens was en route to Saturn, ESA engineer Boris Smeds insisted on more complete communication testing and found that the Doppler effect would compress the signal from the Huygens probe, so that the receiver on Cassini could not find the bit synchronization pulses and data would be intermittently lost, while the firmware could not be modified after launch. The team ultimately changed Cassini's trajectory design to reduce the Doppler shift. Problems that cannot be seen in a specification only surface through end-to-end testing under near-realistic conditions; the same is true of regression testing.

Regression testing should therefore be clearly labeled: the scope covered by the tests has been established, but full verification of the whole has not yet been completed; the test results should not be extrapolated to all data ingestion points, and they must be re-verified against actual conditions at deployment.

### National security implications

The lesson of Case 19 is:

> **Gatekeeping at data ingestion matters more than the conspicuous parsing lines.**³

In practice, data ingestion and parsing lines should be checked separately, and both the gatekeeping regular expressions and the regression tests should be clearly indicated; the parts that have been stopgapped and those still awaiting a root fix should be recorded separately, an absence of errors at data ingestion does not mean the data are complete, and re-verification is required when moving to new deployment conditions.

## Main case 3: Case 25

> **Evidence level: A | Independently recomputed**
>
> **Data sources**: Of 284 Starlink satellites with MEME data, 283 were valid; 2026-05-02 to 09-17 (about 4.5 months); downsampled to one record per day, for a total of 35,460 semi-major axis residuals (a_TLE − a_MEME). Solar and geomagnetic indices were taken from NASA OMNI2 and are not data built by this project.
>
> **Ground truth**: MEME (SpaceX precise ephemerides), independent of TLEs; this project used SGP4 to propagate "the most recent TLE before that time" to the same time and then compared point by point.
>
> **Key assumptions**: Extrapolating from the most recent past TLE corresponds to how the reporting API and the rule-based detector actually operate; residuals were not screened for quiet periods, detrended, or cleaned of jumps.
>
> **Limitations**: The rho = 0.564 obtained from weekly aggregation (21 data points) fell to rho = 0.063 when recomputed over 35,460 individual points, smaller by nearly a factor of 10; a large sample makes even tiny effects significant, which does not mean they are of practical significance; the specific mechanism proposed by the committee member (F10.7 rising during propagation, with B\* unable to respond in time) was not supported, and the physical mechanism remains unclear; a comparison of re-propagation with a different force model has not yet been completed. The sample covers only Starlink, over about 4.5 months.
>
> **When not to use this method**: Correlation coefficients after weekly or other aggregation should not be treated as the actual effect size; the correlation between F10.7 and TLE error should not be interpreted as causal; while Starlink continuously performs station-keeping, the rate of change of MEME's own semi-major axis is dominated by kilometer-scale maneuver signals, making it unsuitable for testing drag effects.

**Data specification**: Nature Measured; Period 2026-05-02 to 2026-09-17 (about 4.5 months); TLE count not stated in the case file (35,460 semi-major axis residuals, 283 Starlink satellites, downsampled to one record per day); Threshold no fixed threshold (correlation analysis; weekly aggregation uses only residuals with extrapolation <24 hours, 21 weeks in total); False-alarm rate not stated in the case file.

### Case positioning

The question in Case 25 is: "A single follow-up question from a committee member punctured a statistical illusion in which weekly aggregation exaggerated the effect size by nearly a factor of 10." This case compared the TLEs of 284 satellites against MEME (SpaceX precise ephemerides) ground truth, and at first seemed to confirm that solar activity affects TLE error, until a review committee member asked, "Shouldn't MEME be affected too?"⁴

In this chapter, Case 25 is a case of statistical aggregation bias, not a complete validation of the whole system; the results of statistical aggregation must still be interpreted together with the layers described above.

### Statistical aggregation bias

Case 25 originally used 284 satellites × MEME ground truth and seemed to confirm that solar activity affects TLE error, until a committee member asked, "Shouldn't MEME be affected too?"⁴

The value of this case lies in showing how statistical aggregation exaggerates effect sizes, without pretending the problem has been solved. Weekly aggregation first averages the point-by-point residuals by week; the noise is averaged out, and the correlation is thereby amplified. After recomputing with all the point-by-point data, the effect size became smaller by nearly a factor of 10. Case 25 also points out that this is a "statistical illusion," not a "causal relationship": the point-by-point data still show a weak but statistically significant association, but the specific mechanism proposed by the committee member was not supported, and the real physical mechanism remains unclear.⁴

The basis of this comparison is: of 284 Starlink satellites with MEME data, 283 were valid; the period was 2026-05-02 to 09-17, about 4.5 months, downsampled to one record per day, for a total of 35,460 semi-major axis residuals. Weekly aggregation leaves only 21 data points, with a correlation coefficient between the residuals and F10.7 of rho = 0.564; recomputed with the 35,460 individual points, rho falls to 0.063.⁴

**Sidebar: BICEP2's gravitational-wave signal in 2014**

In March 2014, the BICEP2 team announced the detection of B-mode polarization in the cosmic microwave background, interpreted as primordial gravitational waves from inflation. At the end of January 2015, a joint analysis by BICEP2, the Keck Array, and Planck found that a large part of the signal came from Galactic dust; after removing it, there was no statistically significant evidence, leaving only an upper limit of r < 0.12. The original paper was not retracted; the correction came through the joint analysis. BICEP2's error lay in not fully removing foreground dust, a mechanism different from Case 25's weekly aggregation; what they share is that systematic bias can overestimate an effect size, which shrinks only once external data are added.

The lesson of Case 25 is:

> **Statistical aggregation does not equal a causal relationship.**⁴

In practice, statistics and causal relationships should be stated separately, with the effect size and confidence interval indicated; biases and limitations should be kept on record, aggregated correlation coefficients cannot be treated as the actual effect size, and re-verification is required when moving to new deployment conditions.

## Designing reproducible, auditable, and maintainable systems

This book recommends that a reproducible, auditable, and maintainable system include:

- Data snapshots and versions.
- Time systems and conversion methods.
- Ground-truth sources and grouping.
- Data ingestion and regression tests.
- Statistical aggregation and effect sizes.
- Known failure scenarios.
- Recommended data for follow-up review.

With these fields, analysts can reconstruct the reasoning, rather than seeing only a red flag.

**Sidebar: Galileo down for about a week in 2019**

On July 11, 2019, Europe's Galileo system gave advance notice that service might be degraded; on the 13th it announced a service outage, and service was not restored until the 18th, about a week in all; the search and rescue service was not affected. The European GNSS Agency at the time (now EUSPA) explained that the cause was an equipment malfunction in the ground infrastructure, which affected the time and orbit prediction calculations needed to generate the navigation message. According to secondary sources summarizing technical briefings, the backup system was unavailable at the time, and new equipment was also mishandled during an upgrade. Orbit and time are one and the same product; a temporary loss of backup during an upgrade is often the moment when a system is most vulnerable.

## Division of labor between reproducibility, auditability, and maintainability and other layers

Reproducibility, auditability, and maintainability do not replace the rule layer, nor are they subordinate to the statistical layer. They carry engineering responsibility; the rule layer provides explainable candidates and reasons for suppression; the statistical layer quantifies the degree of anomaly; the fusion layer integrates multiple channels; and physics and applicability handling provide additional plausibility checks.

| Question | Component best suited to use first |
|---|---|
| Does it exceed a known threshold? | L1 rule layer. |
| Does it deviate from recent normal behavior? | L2 statistical layer. |
| Do multiple channels jointly support it? | L3 fusion layer. |
| Can it be explained by drag or reentry? | Physics and applicability handling. |
| Are the data sufficient for a judgment? | Quality gate. |
| Can this result be reconstructed, audited, and maintained? | Reproducibility, auditability, and maintainability. |

This table is this book's recommended engineering division of labor; it does not mean that every system must execute in the same order. Actual pipelines can compute in parallel and can also be adjusted to deployment conditions.
