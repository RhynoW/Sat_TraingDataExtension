## Chapter overview

In 2013, after the second Vega flight, VV02, delivered its satellites to orbit, it left behind the upper part of the VESPA payload adapter: a cone of 113 kilograms and about two meters in diameter. In December 2020, the European Space Agency (ESA) signed a contract worth 86 million euros to remove it, in a mission named ClearSpace-1.

On August 10, 2023, the U.S. Space Force's 18th Space Defense Squadron notified ESA's Space Debris Office that new objects had been detected near this target. ESA published its analysis on August 22, concluding that the most likely explanation was that a small, uncataloged object had struck VESPA at hypervelocity. The main body of the adapter remained largely intact and its orbit did not change noticeably, but new debris now accompanied it.

The removal spacecraft was still on the ground, and the target it was meant to remove had already been hit by an unseen piece of debris. ESA later switched the target to PROBA-1, a 95-kilogram retired satellite, and the launch slipped to 2029. If even a piece of debris selected for removal, and given special attention, can be hit by something not in the catalog, how will future autonomous space situational awareness deal with the part it cannot see?

The focus of future space situational awareness is not to stuff all data into one model, but to answer: under specific data conditions, orbital regimes, and missions, how should a system act autonomously, integrate, and gate? This chapter uses Case 13, Case 20, and Case 26 to illustrate MEME forecast limitations, the LEO-PNT gate, and the propagator's domain of validity, and on that basis proposes research and integration routes; these routes should not be taken as capabilities that are all already deployed.¹

## What the future is asking

The question future space situational awareness must answer is:

> **How should the system act autonomously, integrate, and gate?**¹

**Sidebar: ESA's Space Environment Report 2024**

In July 2024, the European Space Agency released its *Space Environment Report 2024*, concluding that the collective behavior of nations and companies is "unsustainable in the long term": the number of satellites launched in 2023 reached a record; about 35,000 objects were tracked, of which about 26,000 were debris larger than 10 centimeters; and although more satellites were being deorbited, the amount of debris was still growing on net. The more crowded the orbits become, the more "who is doing what" needs to be verifiable; autonomy, integration, and gating are therefore not merely engineering preferences.

Before answering, the baseline of comparison must be confirmed. The same future blueprint can mean something entirely different in a different orbital regime, data condition, and mission. This book recommends distinguishing at least three kinds of output for the future:

| Output | Meaning | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Autonomy | Whether the system can judge autonomously. | Does not mean all conditions have been verified; the system outputs in the final report are always candidate events and must be reviewed by analysts. |
| Integration | Whether the system can integrate multiple sources. | Does not mean all biases have been eliminated. |
| Gating | Whether the system can stay within its domain of validity. | Does not mean all risks have been eliminated. |

The three must not be conflated: being able to judge autonomously does not mean all conditions have been verified; being able to integrate multiple sources does not mean biases have been eliminated; and being able to stay within the domain of validity does not mean risks have been eliminated.

**Sidebar: ADRAS-J backs off at 15 meters**

Astroscale's ADRAS-J was launched on February 18, 2024, targeting the H-IIA upper stage left in orbit after the 2009 launch of GOSAT. It approached from hundreds of kilometers away using angles-only navigation and completed a fly-around observation at about 50 meters in July. On November 30, during its final approach to about 15 meters below the target, an unexpected anomaly appeared in the upper stage's relative attitude; the onboard collision avoidance system autonomously aborted and backed off as designed. The decision on the final step was handed to the onboard system, and it chose to give up.

## MEME forecast limitations

MEME is the precise ephemeris published by SpaceX. Case 13 uses MEME to illustrate forecasts and methodological pitfalls, including three findings: when comparing osculating semi-major axes, short-period oscillation caused 99.5% mislabeling; SpaceX pre-encodes planned maneuvers into its ephemeris files; and maneuver contamination inflated extrapolation error 14-fold.¹

**Sidebar: MEME is a reference frame, not a brand**

According to the peer-reviewed literature, SpaceX has published Starlink ephemerides on Space-Track since May 2021. They are mainly predicted orbits for about the next 3 days—forecasts, not measurements. The filename prefix "MEME" is an abbreviation of the reference frame "Mean Equator Mean Equinox," not a Starlink-specific name; before comparing with SGP4, the data must first be converted to TEME. Since July 28, 2025, Space-Track no longer provides SpaceX ephemerides; they are provided on the SpaceX website instead.

This book recommends that MEME forecast limitations be labeled with:

- The forecast method and version.
- The data snapshot and time window.
- The osculating semi-major axis and short-period oscillation.
- Planned maneuvers versus actual execution.
- Known failure scenarios.
- Recommended follow-up data for review.

With these fields, analysts can reconstruct the judgment process instead of seeing only a red flag.

### Short-period oscillation of the osculating semi-major axis

The osculating semi-major axis oscillates up and down because of J2 short-period perturbations caused by Earth's oblateness, which can look very much like a maneuver signal. The first version of Case 13's method directly compared the osculating semi-major axes of adjacent snapshots and as a result misclassified 99.5% of normal samples as maneuvers; Case 13 explains that this was a systematic error in the method itself, later corrected by averaging the semi-major axis over an entire ephemeris file. This means that this project has established a method for analyzing extrapolation error and maneuvers with MEME, but has not yet completed full validation.²

Only after the correction did the noise floor for known stationary satellites fall below the 0.2 km maneuver detection threshold.²

### Planned maneuvers and actual execution

Case 13 found that SpaceX pre-encodes planned maneuvers into its ephemeris files: files published before and after a burn already contain the same future semi-major axis change over their overlapping period. Maneuvers in MEME files are therefore pre-written plans and are not equivalent to actual execution observed after the fact. This likewise means that the analysis method has been established but not yet fully validated.²

**Sidebar: ELSA-d's plan and execution**

Astroscale's ELSA-d was launched in March 2021 and on August 25 completed a demonstration of releasing and recapturing a client satellite with a magnetic mechanism. In January 2022, after the servicer began autonomous relative navigation, it detected an anomaly and paused capture; in April of that year the company explained that 4 of its 8 thrusters had failed. On April 7, the servicer approached from about 1,700 kilometers to about 159 meters and abandoned the capture for mission safety. Once propulsion capability degrades, planned maneuvers cannot be executed on schedule; the gap between plan and execution must be labeled separately.

### Maneuver contamination and extrapolation error

Case 13 points out that maneuver contamination inflated extrapolation error 14-fold. According to Case 13's measurements, this comes from freezing one TLE for each of 50 satellites and extrapolating for 7 days, comparing the median position error of the group that maneuvered during the period with that of the pure-extrapolation group; what is measured is TLE extrapolation error, not the error of MEME itself. This likewise means that the analysis method has been established but not yet fully validated.²

Of these 50 satellites, 38 were purely extrapolated over the 7 days without maneuvering, and the other 12 maneuvered during the period; this measurement also spanned a data-download gap of about 6.7 days.²

This book recommends that all three items—the osculating semi-major axis, planned maneuvers versus actual execution, and maneuver contamination versus extrapolation error—be clearly labeled: an analysis method has been established, full validation has not been completed, results should not be extrapolated to all ephemerides, and they should be revalidated according to deployment conditions.

### National-security implications

The core lesson of Case 13 is:

> **MEME is a forecast published by the operator; when it is used as ground truth, plan and execution must be separated.**²

Therefore, forecasts and measurements must be separated, and planned maneuvers and actual execution must be labeled separately. The final report (Section 7.1) uses MEME precise ephemerides (meter-level position accuracy) as the strict ground truth for Starlink, for model training and performance validation; when citing it, one should still remember that it is a forecast product published by the operator, that the maneuvers in its files are pre-loaded plans, and that cross-file comparison cannot pinpoint the burn time.⁶ Unverified results should be retained; when deployment conditions change, revalidation is also needed.

**Sidebar: Ephemeris coordination in lunar orbit**

ISRO and NASA JPL predicted that the Chandrayaan-2 orbiter and the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) would meet near the lunar north pole at 05:45 (UTC) on October 20, 2021, with a radial separation of less than 100 meters and a closest distance of about 3 kilometers. In the preceding week both sides analyzed the event independently, reached the same conclusion, and agreed that Chandrayaan-2 would maneuver; the burn at 14:52 (UTC) on October 18 was nominal, after which the orbit was redetermined from tracking data, confirming there would be no further close approach in the near term. Exchanging forecasts and agreeing in advance on who moves is the prototype of ephemeris sharing.

## Main case 2: Case 20

> **Evidence level: A | Data independently recomputed (reusing Case 13); the application conclusion is an unverified inference**
>
> **Data sources**: Reuses results already computed in Case 13 (284 Starlink satellites, about 26 million data points; median position error of fresh TLEs about 1.5 km, MEME about 5 m); no new computation.
>
> **Ground truth**: Same as Case 13, with MEME precise ephemerides as reference.
>
> **Key assumptions**: A satellite's own position error is the lower bound of positioning accuracy; satellites that have just maneuvered can be flagged by the detection system in real time.
>
> **Limitations**: The numbers themselves are this project's recomputation; but "maneuver detection can serve as a LEO-PNT ephemeris gate" is an extended argument at the application level and has been neither built nor validated. After screening out recently maneuvered satellites, the TLE accuracy of the remaining satellites is still at the kilometer level; the machine-learning models of the maneuver detection system are trained only on Starlink, and their quantified performance holds only within the Starlink low-Earth-orbit range, while the L1 rules have additionally been validated with IDS/ILRS precise-orbit satellites and FORMOSAT-7 precise orbits (final report, Sections 7.4–7.6).
>
> **When not to use this method**: When meter-level positioning is needed, TLEs should not be used as ephemerides (even with maneuvered satellites screened out); for constellations outside the Starlink low-Earth-orbit regime, and before feasibility of integration into an actual positioning pipeline and latency requirements are validated, it should not be treated as a usable gating mechanism.

**Data specification**: Nature inference (data reuse Case 13 measurements; the application conclusion is an unverified inference); Period reuses Case 13, no new computation (the constellation-wide comparison is this project's continuous comparison over two and a half years, start and end dates not stated); TLE count reuses Case 13: 284 Starlink satellites, about 26 million data points (TLE count not stated in the case file); Threshold no fixed threshold (an extended argument at the application level; the gating mechanism has not been built or validated); False-alarm rate not stated in the case file (the gating mechanism has not been built or validated).

### Case positioning

The core question of Case 20 is "Can maneuver detection be turned around to safeguard Starlink-based positioning?" It extends Case 13's conclusion that "TLE and MEME differ by three orders of magnitude" and argues for the application value and boundaries of maneuver detection as a real-time gating mechanism for LEO-PNT ephemeris trustworthiness.³

LEO-PNT stands for Low Earth Orbit Positioning, Navigation and Timing, referring to the use of low-Earth-orbit satellite constellations as a backup or supplement to GPS for positioning, navigation, and timing. According to Case 20, this case reuses results already computed in Case 13 and only makes an extended argument at the application level, with no new computation. Case 20 is a case on the LEO-PNT gate; it does not represent complete validation of the entire system.

**Sidebar: Europe's low-orbit navigation demonstrator Celeste**

Celeste is a low-orbit PNT in-orbit demonstration mission approved at ESA's 2022 Ministerial Council, renamed from LEO-PNT in September 2025. The first two satellites, IOD-1 and IOD-2, originally scheduled to launch in December 2025, slipped to March 28, 2026, when they lifted off from New Zealand on a Rocket Lab Electron; the demonstration constellation targets 11 satellites, with the rest scheduled to launch in 2027. ESA lists the "integrity concept" as one of the key technologies still to mature. Low-orbit satellites maneuver frequently, making ephemeris integrity harder to manage than for medium-orbit GNSS.

### The LEO-PNT gate

Case 20 proposes using maneuver detection as a real-time gating mechanism for LEO-PNT ephemeris trustworthiness: before use, screen out satellites that have recently maneuvered and whose TLEs are temporarily untrustworthy. According to Case 20, this is an extended argument at the application level, not a positioning system this project has already validated; at present only an application path has been proposed, and it has not yet been built or validated.³

**Sidebar: Positioning with Starlink signals**

In 2021, Zak Kassas's team (UC Irvine, later moving to Ohio State) tracked the Doppler of 6 Starlink satellites and achieved a horizontal error of 10 meters; a carrier-phase experiment reached 7.7 meters with a known receiver altitude. The papers state explicitly that satellite positions were taken from TLEs and SGP4, and the TLE epoch had to be adjusted satellite by satellite to compensate for ephemeris error. Todd Humphreys's team at the University of Texas at Austin decoded the structure of the Starlink downlink signal, and he said in an interview that positioning to within about 30 meters was possible. The accuracy ceiling is often limited by ephemeris quality.

### Application value and boundaries

Case 20 argues for the application value and boundaries of this gating mechanism. Case 20 lists three boundaries: after screening out recently maneuvered satellites, the TLE accuracy of the remaining satellites is still at the kilometer level; meter-level LEO-PNT requires MEME-grade precise ephemerides, which are not currently publicly available in real time; and the machine-learning models of the maneuver detection system have quantified performance only within the Starlink low-Earth-orbit range, and although the L1 rules have additionally been validated with IDS/ILRS precise-orbit satellites and FORMOSAT-7 precise orbits, the feasibility and latency requirements of integration into an actual positioning pipeline remain future work.³,⁶

The basis for the first boundary is Case 13's constellation-wide comparison: the median position error of fresh TLEs (epoch less than 3 hours old) is about 1.5 km, versus about 5 m for MEME. A satellite's own position error is the lower bound of positioning accuracy; for meter-level positioning needs, the two differ by nearly three orders of magnitude.³

**Sidebar: Xona's commercial low-orbit navigation**

Xona Space Systems, founded in 2019, launched its technology demonstrator Huginn on May 25, 2022, and its first production-class satellite Pulsar-0 on June 23, 2025; to speed up launch, Pulsar-0 has no propulsion system. What the company emphasizes is signal authentication: a cross-satellite mechanism authenticates both the data stream and the ranging signal to guard against spoofing. Authentication deals with whether a signal is genuine; integrity deals with erroneous data within genuine signals, such as a failed ephemeris. No public material could be found in which Xona describes its integrity monitoring architecture.

This book recommends that the LEO-PNT gate and its application value and boundaries be clearly labeled: it is currently only an application path, full validation has not been completed, it should not be extrapolated to all positioning missions, and it should be revalidated according to deployment conditions.

### National-security implications

The core lesson of Case 20 is:

> **Maneuver detection can serve as a positioning gate, but cannot replace the positioning system.**³

Therefore, gating and positioning must be separated, and the application value and boundaries must be labeled. Unverified results should be retained, and gating must not be treated as positioning; when deployment conditions change, revalidation is also needed.

## Main case 3: Case 26

> **Evidence level: A | Independently recomputed**
>
> **Data sources**: Starlink satellites, with the sample expanded from 3 and 14 satellites to 100 (randomly sampled from 283 Starlink satellites with MEME data), 352 valid evaluation points; this project's own numerical integrator (J2–J5 gravity, NRLMSISE-00 drag) and SGP4, starting from the same TLE.
>
> **Ground truth**: MEME precise ephemerides, treated as ground truth.
>
> **Key assumptions**: The integrator's drag coefficient was back-estimated by least squares from 288 MEME observations on the first day, giving 0.0388 m²/kg, without using the B\* conversion formula.
>
> **Limitations**: The overall median is 256.4 m for SGP4 and 339.3 m for the full-physics model, with the latter more accurate in 47.7% of cases; the full-physics model's advantage appears only on days 2–3 and in outlier cases where SGP4 loses accuracy; the comparison extrapolates only to day 4; the preliminary conclusion from the 3-satellite sample once went completely astray.
>
> **When not to use this method**: For short-term extrapolation within 1 day after calibration, the full-physics model should not be expected to outperform SGP4; for extrapolation beyond day 4, the case file provides no basis; the sample is Starlink low Earth orbit only and should not be directly extrapolated to other orbital regimes.

**Data specification**: Nature measured; Period extrapolated to day 4 after calibration (Day 0–Day 4), start and end dates not stated in the case file; TLE count not stated in the case file (100 Starlink satellites, 352 valid evaluation points, randomly sampled from 283 Starlink satellites with MEME data; SGP4 and the integrator start from the same TLE; drag coefficient back-estimated from 288 MEME observations on the first day); Threshold no fixed threshold (median position errors compared using MEME as ground truth); False-alarm rate not stated in the case file (a propagator accuracy comparison, not a detection task).

### Case positioning

The core question of Case 26 is "One remark from a committee member forced a home-built orbit propagator—after blaming the wrong culprit five times, the truth was hidden on day three." It compares whether SGP4 or the full-physics model is more accurate, and the answer is not "which one wins" but "where each one's territory lies."⁴

SGP4 is the propagation model behind TLEs; the full-physics model in Case 26 refers to this project's own numerical integrator, whose gravity terms include J2–J5 and whose drag term queries densities from the NRLMSISE-00 atmospheric model in real time. Case 26 is a case on the propagator's domain of validity; it does not represent complete validation of the entire system.

### SGP4 vs. the full-physics model

Case 26 compares SGP4 with the full-physics model and concludes that each has its own territory, rather than one winning.⁴

According to Case 26, "territory" refers to the extrapolation time window: SGP4 performs best in the short term right after calibration, while the full-physics model catches up and overtakes it as the time extends. This comparison uses Starlink satellites as the sample and MEME precise ephemerides "treated as ground truth." The value of this case lies in illustrating the propagator's domain of validity, not in pretending the problem has been solved; Case 26 emphasizes "each one's territory," not "who wins."⁴

Case 26's sample expanded step by step from 3 and 14 satellites to 100, randomly sampled from 283 Starlink satellites with MEME data, for 352 valid evaluation points in total. The integrator's drag coefficient was back-estimated by least squares from 288 MEME observations on the first day, giving 0.0388 m²/kg. For the overall median error of the 100-satellite sample, SGP4 is 256.4 m and the full-physics model is 339.3 m, with the full-physics model more accurate in 47.7% of cases; broken down by extrapolation day, SGP4 is most accurate on days 0–1 after calibration, is overtaken by the full-physics model on days 2–3, and is nearly tied again on day 4.⁴

**Sidebar: The Unified Data Library (UDL)**

The Unified Data Library (UDL) is a cloud database initiated in 2018 by the Air Force Research Laboratory and developed by Bluestaq, later placed under Space Systems Command, that aggregates space awareness data from many parties; since April 2022 it can ingest Space Fence radar observations. Officials have said that UDL is not directly connected to the military's operational systems for predicting on-orbit collisions; "digital twins" are also still individual outsourced contracts, such as the Digital Space Twin awarded to Slingshot in 2022. Centralizing data does not mean predictions are correct; the propagator's domain of validity still awaits the analyst's judgment.

## Division of labor between the future and other layers

Future designs for autonomy, integration, and gating do not replace the rule layer, nor are they subordinate to the statistical layer. Under the final report's R7 workflow, candidate events are generated only by the L1 rules, with explainable trigger and suppression reasons; L2 statistical change points, the fusion scorer, the ML window model, and the drag residual serve as auxiliary channels that affect only the evidence grade and neither add nor remove events; physics and applicability handling provide additional plausibility checks.⁶

| Question | Component best used first |
|---|---|
| Does it exceed a known threshold? | L1 rule layer. |
| Does it deviate from recent normal? | L2 statistical layer. |
| Do multiple channels jointly support it? | Auxiliary-channel cross-check (ML window model, fusion scorer, drag residual, L2 statistical change points; under the final report's R7 workflow, these affect only the evidence grade). |
| Can it be explained by drag or reentry? | Physics and applicability handling. |
| Are the data sufficient for a judgment? | Quality gate. |
| How should the system act autonomously, integrate, and gate? | The future. |

This table is this book's summary of the engineering division of labor; the channels can be computed in parallel, but the decision order for the formal report has been fixed by the final report's R7 workflow, and other systems may adjust according to their deployment conditions.

The follow-up recommendations listed in the final report (Section 11.5) include: seeking precise ephemerides or other high-precision tracking data for priority targets; expanding the training range of the machine-learning models with GNSS precise positioning data from the FORMOSAT series; accumulating review results through a 4–8-week operational trial; routine monitoring of non-cooperative constellations centered on the NRLMSIS drag residual and the L1 rules; archiving TLE and space weather snapshots at delivery; and studying altitude-dependent adjustment of the drag-residual threshold (Section 11.6, as a candidate rule for report R8, to be compared in parallel with R7 rather than replacing it directly). On deep learning, the final report (Chapter 12) fully trained and evaluated three deep sequence models, an LSTM autoencoder, PatchTST, and bi-GRU; all performed worse than the fusion scorer and were not included in the delivered system; if positive samples increase, self-supervised pretraining could be evaluated again. These are recommendations and research directions, not deployed capabilities.⁶

**Sidebar: TraCSS's unfinished handover**

On September 30, 2024, the U.S. Department of Commerce's Traffic Coordination System for Space (TraCSS) began Phase 1 service, with the first 9 operators receiving conjunction data messages via Space-Track. Under Space Policy Directive-3 of 2018, civil and commercial space situational awareness services are to be transferred from the Department of Defense to the Department of Commerce. According to a Via Satellite report in August 2026, more than 70 operators have joined TraCSS, yet it remains in pilot mode because of budget "uncertainty"; the Department of Defense catalog and operator ephemerides have not yet been fused, commercial data procurement has been suspended indefinitely, and full operation still has no date.
